Ancient Greece - the Hellenic Period

The Mycenaeans led the way to a period in which ancient Greece became a great power, politically, culturally and scientifically. The glory that was Greece lasted almost 1,000 years, from about 1200 BCE up to the death of Alexander the Great in 323 BCE.

Initially, Greece’s emergence was typified by various city-states vying, even warring, with each other. Particularly mutually belligerent were Sparta and Athens, but Greece eventually came together as a unified whole, with Athens to the fore.

Greek control then broadened out to colonized Italy, Sicily and parts of western Turkey. The Macedonian Greek, Alexander the Great, went much further. He conquered an enormous area, beating up Darius and his Persian Empire, including Syria, Egypt, and large chunks of old Mesopotamia. He even sorted out Afghanistan then reached further, to the Indian Punjab. It was here that his weary troops said enough is enough and forced Alexander to call time on any more eastern conquests. Greek influence extended over parts of three continents in his time, Europe, Africa and Asia. In his travels Alexander founded several cities, perhaps best known is the Egyptian seaport of Alexandria, now a city of over 5 million people. Alexander, a military genius, became leader of Greece, overlord of Asia Minor, great king of Persia and Pharaoh of Egypt, all at the age of 25.

The philosopher Aristotle, travelled with Alexander, as his tutor. It’s impossible to know exactly how much influence he had on Alexander. The great man obviously learned something of philosophy and philosophers - what and he became a cynic living in a barrel?

All early civilisations have left something behind to tell the modern world what they were about. There are magnificent ruined buildings, art objects, tombs and written work - sometimes on stone. They may have demonstrated expertise in various fields, like astronomy. Altogether though they don’t get anywhere near the impact ancient Greece has had. No archaic civilisation has so firmly and widely imprinted itself on modern life as ancient Greece.

Here’s what we owe them:

  • Language - at least 500 English words come from Greek. This is important because English is a common world language, generally used in commerce, science and politics;

  • Democracy - Athenian men would gather equally on a hill, the Pnyx, to talk and decide on laws, justice and who should be exiled. This was the start of democracy, not for women though! The Greeks also originated the name. Democratic government is now seen as the fairest form of government in the Western World and in some other parts. Several departments of the United Nations actively support the world-wide establishment of democracy and democratic government;

  • Math’s, Science, Logic: Some of the best-known mathematicians and scientists, among many, are: Archimedes who calculated the arc of a parabola and an accurate version of Pi. His ‘Archimedes Screw’ for transporting liquids and other substances is still used today; Democritus computed the volume of a cylinder and proposed the atom as the smallest indivisible substance; Eratosthenes invented latitude and longitude in his work on geography; Euclid is known as the father of geometry; Hipparchus is thought to be the founder of trigonometry and discovered the precession (rotation) of equinoxes; Pythagoras introduced his famous theorem of the right-angled triangle. Aristotle, the great polymath, proposed formal rules for Logical analysis.

  • Medicine - Hippocrates was a great physician and known as the Father of Medicine. His ethical standards were enshrined in the Hippocratic Oath, still used today, with amended wording;

  • Philosophy - challenging accepted thinking was a new concept to ancient people. Usually the accepted norm was so because it had powerful support, sometimes of the deadly variety, from governance or the Gods. The Greeks by discovering philosophy originated this philosophical exploration of reality. The list of groundbreaking Greek philosophers is too numerous to put down here. Thales started them off, as the founder of western philosophy. Much later there was Socrates, claimed the wisest and possibly the most annoying man in Greece. A

p.122

little later came Plato. Whitehead said the whole of western philosophy was a series of footnotes to Plato;

  • Architecture - the remnants of iconic ancient buildings still remain. The Parthenon and Temple of Zeus in Athens are but two of many to inspire more modern architecture. To present arts, sport and theatre the Greeks designed amphitheaters, on which many people could stand and sit on a circular, raised dais. These were the precursors to modern stadia and indoor theatres. The Hellenic Greek style of columns is still copied, more than 2,500 years after their original introduction: Doric, Ionic and Corinthian;

  • Literature and Entertainment - What a lineup of talented writers those ancient Greeks had, starting around 2,700 years ago, with Homer and Hesiod. These two wrote entertaining and poetic pieces of myths and Gods. They laid the foundation for Greece’s ancient religious beliefs as the myths and Gods became believable. Their works have also been the base for other ancient Greek writers. In modern times the myths invented by Homer and Hesiod’s have been used in stories and films. Here are some: Hercules; Jason and the Argonauts, Clash of the Titans, The Immortals, Helen of Troy, the 1987 TV series The Odyssey and others. We’ve also enjoyed, through film, the adventures of Perseus, the hero who killed the monster Medusa, a snake head Gorgon. Apart from Homer and Hesiod we also have Aesop, the black slave of 620 to 560 BCE. He gave us his witty and pointed fables. Many of these offered moral advice. Their phraseology has become part of the English language with terms such as: “crying wolf”; “the lion’s share”, “sour grapes” and “dog in a manger”. One of the best known is the tale of the hare and the tortoise. The fables may have originally been in oral rather than written form. There’s Sappho with her lyrical poetry, Aeschylus, the dramatist and originator, known in the theatre world as the father of tragedy. He wrote Agamemnon among 90 plays. Sophocles was another playwright who wrote more than 120 plays, including Ajax and Electra. Aristophanes was yet another playwright, but not a writer of tragedies. He was possibly the first writer of comedy that we know of. His most famous surviving play is Lysistrata. This is about Greek women coming together to deny men sex, unless the Peloponnesian War is ended. Finally, we have the third great ancient Greek tragedian, Euripides. He introduced a new approach by casting his mythical characters as relatively ordinary people caught up in extraordinary circumstances. His influence extends to the present day. He also shocked ancient Greek audiences by making his characters break accepted norms, e.g. his heroine, Medea, says:

p.123

“Sooner would I stand Three times to face their battles, shield in hand, Than bear one child!”

  • Art - development in Greek art saw the human body depicted in more realistic ways, this had influence on all art that followed. Despite her missing arms, the Venus de Milo in the Louvre Museum is rated one the best sculptures in the world. It’s thought to have been chiseled by Alexandros of Antioch about 2,100 years ago;

  • History - in 425 BCE the Greek Herodotus showed the way to all subsequent historians. His immense work on the Greco-Persian wars, was a meticulous and systematic account. It explored the cause and effect of these conflicts in an analytic fashion. Herodotus showed all later historians how to write up on history;

  • Sport - they invented the greatest mass sports event of modern times, the Olympic Games. Just as they did, we hold this event every four years. They started up events like javelin, discus, marathon and long jump. Greeks also liked boxing and horse racing. Greek athletes and keep-fitters worked out in a Gymnasium. Today we still use this type of torture chamber - it is for some of us. But we shorten the name to Gym;

  • Wonders of the world - of the seven wonders of the world five were constructed wholly or partly by Greeks between 550 and 280 BCE: The Temple of Artemis; Statue of Zeus at Olympia; Mausoleum at Halicarnassus; Colossus of Rhodes and the Lighthouse of Alexandria.

Greek Religion: Homer and Hesiod, put a big oar in here, as previously mentioned. They were aided by some transcription from the preceding Minoans and Mycenaeans. The Greeks endowed a great deal to our modern world, but not with their religion, that is, except obliquely. The highly entertaining, but unbelievable antics of Greek Gods helped to inspire new thinking in the way of metaphysical philosophy. It could be said Greek religion kick-started philosophy.

The religion was quite a mixture of mythology, heroes, rituals and beliefs. There were a number of Gods and Goddesses. The top twelve Gods resided on high, on Mount Olympus. Of fourteen possible contenders, they are generally regarded to be:

p.124
  • Zeus (King-like, with his thunderbolts, father of Hercules),
  • Hera (Zeus’ Queen, protector of heroes),
  • Poseidon (trident wielding sea God),
  • Demeter (Goddess of fertility and harvest),
  • Athena (Goddess of reason, wisdom and war - a very curious mixture. Could should she not use the first two to avoid the third?),
  • Ares (the manly God of war, hated son of Zeus),
  • Aphrodite (Goddess of love and beauty),
  • Apollo (twin of Artemis, good with a bow, God of lots - music, poetry, art, truth, knowledge, medicine, prophecy - must have been exhausting for him),
  • Artemis (daughter of Zeus, the virgin huntress and sort of early feminist),
  • Hephaestus (God of fire, a builder and craftsman, the only ugly God. He sometimes swapped his Olympian accommodation with Hades - must have been hell for him),
  • Hermes (Godly messenger, cunning and tricky, possibly the modern God of fake news) and
  • Dionysus (God of the vine).

Athena was a well-known Greek God, or Goddess. She seemed to vie with Ares as the God of war. Maybe she sub-contracted this job out to him occasionally. Athena must have been very busy sometimes. Besides looking out for war and conflict she also had to do work as the Goddess of wisdom and act as the patron deity of Athens. She should have complained to her trade union. Athena had a second name, Pallas Athena.

The Gods were human like, often quarrelling with one another, or supporting different sides in disputes in the human world. Zeus was top God but he did not have absolute control.

In addition to the Olympus Gods and Goddesses there were also other supernatural entities the three fates (Moirai), nine muses - important to music and art. Then we have the many beautiful, nubile, naturistic maidens - the nymphs. They include Naiads, water nymphs and Dryads, tree nymphs. Lastly, there’s three no one wanted to meet - the Furies, also known as the Erinyes. These were the Goddesses of vengeance - Tisiphone, who punished murderers; Megaera, she dealt severely with thieves, oaths breakers and infidelity; finally, Alecto who sorted out anybody considered to be morally corrupt. Poor Alecto, her two sisters would be busy but as nothing compared to her. She must have had by far the heaviest workload and probably had to work at break-neck speed around the clock.

p.125

The Gods vied for Earthly control with the Titans, a related group of mighty God like beings. Zeus’ lot eventually overthrew the Titans.

Ancient Greek religion believed humans had souls and it told of a hereafter. We have the tale involving the bolshie boatman, Charon. He who complainingly ferried souls across the River Styx to the underworld of Hades, but only if they paid the fare.

  • When over the other side the departed faced three judges. These decided where each soul should end up. If they had been very good, it’s the blissful Elysian Fields for them; the bad went to Tartarus, in a pit to be tortured forever. The in-betweeners were sent to Asphodel Meadows, a place that was a pale shadow of the world they had left. Did this story give later Abrahamic religions their idea of heaven, hell and purgatory?

  • Every Greek city had its own patron God or Goddess. To get on the right side of these Gods, Greeks left offerings of wine, food, and flowers in temples. Sometimes an animal would be sacrificed. There were quite a lot of festivals with dancing and feasting in honour of the Gods. These may have encouraged Abrahamic religions to engage in something similar, in altered form.

The ancient Greeks had a large, complex, polytheistic religion, full of stories and heroes. The impact of early, dismissive Greek Milesian and other philosophers eroded belief to an extent. However, the Greeks passed on their religious baton to eager and willing Roman successors.

Roman times:

The Romans established another great civilisation and empire. Legend says brothers Romulus and Remus founded Rome in 753 BCE. They then had a serious falling out resulting in Romulus dispatching Remus and naming the new city after himself. Had it been the other way round Rome might have been called Reme. Then we would have had the Remians instead of the Romans.

Rome prospered, based on trade. As it grew in strength it first beat up its Lords and near neighbours, the Etruscans. Then, with the conclusion of the 120 year long Punic wars, it destroyed the power of Carthage, modern day Tunis. This gave Rome a huge, Mediterranean based, area of control. At the time the Greeks were a waning power and Rome took over from them. The many things Greek were much admired and taken up by the Romans.

p.126

In architecture Romans followed principles laid down by Greeks. Greek was the language of the elite in culture and commerce. In politics Romans took on board Greek democracy and gave it their own slant. Under the Roman Republic, all citizens of a certain elected class were represented in government by elected representatives. These were elected to a Senate of 100 people. These effectively ruled. Eventually the Senate expanded in size, weakened in authority, and became a body that simply rubber-stamped the wishes of powerful Roman Emperors.

Roman Religion:

In the earliest period Rome religion was mainly a form of animism. Romans believed spirits abounded. They were in everything around them, humans included. Ancestors had a vital role. Romans thought their ancestors’ spirits watched over them, a bit like the story of Noth earlier in this book. A little later a triumvirate of Gods, called the Capitoline Triad, were added to these spirits. These three were Jupiter - the supreme God, his wife Juno and their daughter, Minerva. This Roman concept of three Gods, has resonance with Christianity’s Trinity - Father, Son and Holy Spirit. Did the Biblical writer’s basic idea of the Holy Trinity, which includes Jesus as both a human and God, arise from knowledge of Roman religion?

Another very important God for the Roman’s was Quirinus. He was originally Romulus who, as we just learned, killed his twin brother Remus and remained alone, after his elevation to Godly status. Not surprisingly he became the city-God for Rome. And in another curious link with Christian concepts, we have Romulus, or Quirinus, both a human and a God, sounds familiar?

As Rome grew in power it took over territory that had been part of the Greek empire. Romans learned of and liked the idea of the Greek Gods. So, their later religion became a close copy of that of ancient Greece. Names of Gods were changed but they had similar functions. The Greek chief God Zeus became Roman God Jupiter, Hera was now Juno, and Poseidon became Neptune, Aphrodite - Venus, Athena - Minerva, Artemis - Diana, Demeter - Ceres, Dionysus - Bacchus, Ares - Mars, Hephaestus - Vulcan, Hades - Pluto and Hermes - Mercury. Curiously Apollo stayed the same, as both a Greek and Roman God. 2,000 years later he became a mint with a hole.

p.127

Rituals were important in Roman religion, often taking place in the many temples. These temples were named after one or other of the Gods. The Gods had to be appeased and consulted constantly. Before major battles priests examined the entrails of animals or the flight of birds to read the omens. Spurinna, a Roman soothsayer did this and, apparently, foresaw the death of Julius Caesar.

The priests were part of a public office. They did not have their own separate organization. Religion was closely associated with the state. From the time of Emperor Augustus, the residing Emperor had the title of Chief Priest, giving him additional authority. Romans intelligently did not insist that the people they conquered convert to Roman religious beliefs.

The Roman Empire period lasted from 27 BCE to about 500 CE, about half as long as the preceding Greeks. Visigoths put a big dent in the Empire by sacking Rome in 410 CE, then the Vandals did the same in 455 CE. Before these final death blows, Roman Emperor Constantine I had already seen the writing on the wall. In 330 CE he chose Constantinople, rather than Rome, as his new capital. Constantinople was situated in the ancient Greek colony of Byzantium. A much-reduced Roman Empire transmogrified into a more eastern, Byzantine Empire.

Constantine was clearly an innovator. Five years before his capital idea he also chose a new official Roman religion at the Council of Nicaea, 325 CE. He made Christianity the official religion for the entire Roman Empire.

At its largest extent, in about 117 AD, the Roman Empire covered parts of Europe, Africa and Asia, all centered on the Mediterranean, and reached as far north as Britain. An estimated 21% of the world’s inhabitants were inside the empire’s 5 million square kilometers.

England owe the Romans at the least the concept of straight roads, central heating, plumbing, our calendar, and town planning. Latin - the Roman language - is used in science, law and botany. Many school children may not love it but still start their Latin with amo, amas, amat, amatus, amatis, amant. Well done you Romans.
