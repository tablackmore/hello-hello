Chapter 10

What of God and the pre-history Hominins?

Pages 81–86

There’s evidence indicating that some form of spiritual belief goes back 300,000 years. This comes from a cave site at Atapuerca in Spain, which was probably occupied by Hominins, Homo Sapiens and predecessors, for 800,000 years. Here were found signs of intentional burial, although not all experts agree. Skulls and complete skeletons have been discovered, either of Neanderthals or Homo Heidelbergensis. The earliest undisputed MH (modern human) ritual burial was at Qafzeh, in Israel, where a mother and child had been buried with their bones stained with red ochre. The find dates back 100,000 years. Red ochre is thought to have been an important part of burial for many thousands of years.

Some later signs of stone age spirituality arose in the upper Palaeolithic era. Earth mother statuettes began to appear. The famous Venus of Willendorf and Venus of Hohle Fels have already been mentioned. These were only a few of many such statuettes. They were sculpted over a period of at least 24,000 years, from 35,000 to at least 11,000 years ago (the Venus of Monruz). Then there are the cave paintings, also produced over several thousand years by stone-agers. They too are thought to have some spiritual significance.

If God exists and is interested in Homo Sapiens, then what evidence, if any, do we have of His contact with Palaeolithic man? Why did He allow these ancient people to go down the relatively blind alley of animism or shamanism type spiritualism? After all, Stone Age people had the same intelligence and desires that Modern Humans have. Surely God does not start his serious involvement with humanity from say a mere 3,000 years ago, which is about the time the first books of the bible were written?

How did God give guidance to all those humans that lived before the biblical period? Did God trigger the Palaeolithic Transition? Did He encourage agriculture, because this would give humans greater thinking time? They might even think of Him.

You don’t need to be good at arithmetic to work out that the pre-history Palaeolithic Transition took place many, many generations before the first glimmerings of the bible story, before the Qur’an, Torah, sacred Vedas and other holy texts. These all emerged many tens of thousands of years later.

Out of the Palaeolithic and into the Neolithic

We are going to move on from the Palaeolithic to the Neolithic. This means by-passing the Mesolithic. This latter is a slightly controversial, variable length, middle era between the Palaeolithic, old stone age, and the Neolithic, new stone age. Some palaeontologists think it is an unnecessary distinction.

Sitari’s story.

The seeds she had dropped accidentally on the muddy soil some months before were now tall grass plants. The clan had returned from their wanderings to the area. Between two mighty rivers this flat plain was a favourite hunting ground. Imago, the clan chieftain, decided they should spend increasingly longer periods in this one location, because the hunting was good and the climate mild.

Sitari wondered about the plants waving greenly in front of her. When her tribe returned again in the autumn, would the plants bear seeds that she could harvest? If so why not press a few more seeds into the ground from her store. Fortunately, the men had had a succession of successful hunts. There was plenty of meat, river fish too. The store of seeds Sitari held were not needed immediately as food. She could spare a few so took out a small handful of from the storage horn. After clearing a small area of damp soil, she pressed the seeds into the red earth. Sitari marked her seeds with a small branch from a nearby bush. She tended them over the summer months, watering and pulling up other competing plants. In the autumn she was rewarded with a tall, ripe crop. This gave her many more seeds than she had originally planted.

Neolithic people co-operate to break the ground and sow seed

p.83

Sitari knew she must tell chieftain Imago of her success and persuade him to have a much larger area of ground cleared for more seed sowing. Fortunately for Sitari her clan leader was willing to accept new ideas. He was impressed with this assured source of food and decided that the clan should stay put and build more permanent shelters. He ordered that, under Sitari’s guidance, the clan should clear a large area of land for cultivation.

About 10,000 years ago an unknown woman, or women, like Sitari, started the Neolithic (late Stone Age) revolution. This probably happened somewhere in the middle east. The best evidence is that plant cultivation began at Abu Hureyra, which is located in the Euphrates valley in present day Syria. The site was originally occupied by a culture known as Natufian hunter-gatherers.

Sitari, the imaginary first agriculturist, had made a crucial step forward from being a hunter/gatherer. She was trying to exercise more control over her food supply by growing her own food near her dwelling place. This was a big break with the more uncertain world of hunting and searching far and wide for animals, fruit, berries and roots of wild plants. She was the originator of farming on planet Earth. This is an assumption. We don’t know of any specific woman, or man, who first planted seeds and tended the soil.

p.84

But someone must have started agriculture. It’s more likely to have been a woman than a man because generally, women were the gatherers of seeds, nuts and berries while men hunted - although South American women appeared to share the hunting with men.

Agriculture is likely to have grown up independently in different areas of the world. Around 10,000 years ago the development of agriculture, and domestication of animals, which ran alongside, marked a sea-change for people. A food supply on the doorstep meant there was no need to continue the nomadic way of life of the hunter gatherer. This switch to a sedentary lifestyle was by no means all for the best. Those producing the food, the Neolithic farmers, had to work longer hours on average than their hunter-gatherer forefathers. Diet was more restricted and generally poorer. In consequence, average height fell from about 5ft 10in to 5ft 5in for men with a similar reduction for women. Denser populations also meant diseases were rifer, sometimes transferred to humans from domesticated animals, but over time humans developed some resistance to diseases.

There were advantages too. Agriculture allowed food supply to become more reliable, especially in winter. People no longer needed to move on when the local supply of prey animals, fruit, nuts and berries had run short. They could stay put, plant, harvest and store food. This meant building permanent instead of temporary shelter. They could now live together in larger groups. Surplus food meant some people could turn their skills to activities other than farming. These new artisans, producing necessary tools at least first bartered and then later sold goods and services to the Neolithic farmers.

There emerged more pros than cons for MH with the emergence of agriculture. The new way of life was more demanding for most, but it meant more food was available for any given area of land. On balance, agriculture was more favourable to the development of MH than his previous hunter-gathering lifestyle. A crucial fact supporting this contention is that world population expanded under agriculture rather than contracted. Studies in France suggest that in the upper Palaeolithic there was 1 person per 3 km² but by the middle Neolithic this had changed to at least 1 per km².

Increasing populations and the need for focal points to meet, exchange goods and services led to the building of first hamlets, then villages and then towns. Finally, some social organisation was achieved resulting in the appearance of

Jericho and Catal Höyük

p.85

These two were probably the first such town-states but were initially too small to be named a city. Jericho, originally known as Tel es-Sultan, holds a further distinction. It has been continuously occupied for a longer period than any other town or city on the planet. Located in the West Bank, Jericho’s first dwellings go back to about 9,000 BCE, that is 11,000 years ago. Then the town consisted of about 70 dwellings, occupied by 200 or so people. Jericho is made famous, or infamous, through its story in the Bible. Probably all Christians and Jews know the deadly tale of Joshua and the battle of Jericho.

Çatalhöyük as it may have looked, about 7,100 BCE: houses built against each other, entered by ladders through the roofs

Catal Höyük, in Turkey, is not quite so old, at about 7,100 BCE. It eventually became home to about 5,000 people. At its height, the surrounding farm land was probably rich and productive. There is some evidence that the townsfolk engaged in trade with roving merchants. The town was curiously constructed of linked buildings with access through ladders and roof entrances. There were no doors.

Catal Höyük had no written language, and unusually there were no priests or social hierarchy. It is thought, without absolutely clear evidence, that religious beliefs would be simple, based on previous hunter-gatherers. Religion may have been practiced in household shrines and the dead buried beneath floors of houses. From evidence of female figurines recovered the stone-age reverence for an earth mother figure may have persisted in Catal Höyük, but this is not certain.

Mother Goddess figure 6,000 BCE Catal Höyük From Ankara Museum

p.86

Catal Höyük did not last anything like as long as Jericho. It existed for about 1,400 years, being finally abandoned in 5,700 BCE, maybe as a result of climate change, or because of pressure from other peoples. Then again, perhaps the inhabitants got fed up with having no streets and having to use the rooftops and ladders. Anyway, whatever the reason this iconic early human town was eventually abandoned.

Another very ancient archaeological site is also from Turkey, Göbekli Tepe, (Turkish for “Potbelly Hill”). This one is not quite so unusual, building wise, as Catal Höyük, but its origins are even older, 9,000 BCE. It had more than 200 stone pillars, but their purpose is unknown, perhaps a temple.

Göbekli Tepe is on a par with Jericho in terms of age, but Jericho remains ahead as the oldest continuously occupied city.
