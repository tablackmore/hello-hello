Chapter 6

Why Transition, and what is it exactly?

Pages 49–53

No one seems to know why the Transition, or Upper Palaeolithic Revolution, occurred when it did. After a more or less static period for Modern Humans, in technical, linguistic and artistic development lasting about 260,000 years major changes occur. These were briefly referred to earlier. But it’s important to know more precisely what the new developments were during this time. For a start, Noth type individuals (see previous chapter) would have led the development in religious understanding and belief, despite this was largely restricted to a spirit world and shamanistic practices.

Apart from Noth and religious change, new developments took place in three areas:

  • Tools - both hunting and other tools;
  • language;
  • art.

Perhaps, through the necessity of adapting to climate change and prey availability, Stone Age man invents more sophisticated and delicate stone tools. These are differentiated and made for specific purposes: for example, burins for working antler bone or for drawing. Harpoons and darts were made along with other piercing points for hunting weapons and for more delicate needles, these were used for sewing clothes. There are also the very first signs of fishing. We know this from finds at the Blombos caves in South Africa. Upper Palaeolithic man’s language develops allowing him to communicate more clearly, although some palaeontologists debate this. What is indisputable is that he also engages in activities like rock painting and more elaborate ritual burial.

It is a little misleading to suggest these changes “suddenly” happened. This is only correct if measured against a geological timescale. If we compare the rate of change in the Palaeolithic Transition to other major technical and culture transformatory periods, it’s like comparing a tortoise to a race horse or cheetah. The Industrial Revolution lasted at most a couple of hundred years from Victorian times. The more recent Space Age and Computer Age have been around for much shorter timescales still. The Transition period took place over a more leisurely period; a few tens of thousands of years in fact.

Cave Paintings

p.50

Of all the products of the Transition, archaeologists have pondered most over the mysteries of Palaeolithic rock paintings. This Stone Age art has intrigued palaeontologists for ages past. Particularly enthralling is the Upper Palaeolithic period when Homo Sapiens artists were daubing and scratching on cave walls. What was their purpose? Were the images simply a form of artistic expression without any significant underlying meaning? Did they relate in some way to the lives that people led at the time? Was there any spiritual or religious meaning? What was the reason for the chosen subject matter?

Favourite, but unintended models, were the animals Stone Age hunters pursued. The depiction of prey animals and hunting scenes was a general feature of ancient cave galleries. This may be associated with a form of spiritual belief. There is a commonality but not absolute uniformity between the various cave galleries. The paintings do show some variations, not surprising when considering they are found in different, widely separated locations and are sometimes thousands of years apart. However, what they definitely all have in common is a complete lack of depiction of a white, bearded old man, thundering down edicts from a fleecy cloud. If God had been involved in influencing the thoughts of Stone Age artists, then one might expect to see some clearer evidence of His remotely controlled brush-work.

It is possible that Godly influence, if there was any, was subtler in nature and because of this open to some misinterpretation by the painter. Of course, the issue then becomes why a supremely powerful God allows any form of misinterpretation.

Cave paintings have been found all over the habitable world. Some of the most ancient are at East Kalimantan in Borneo. Thousands of paintings have been found here. A hand stencil dates back 52,000 years and paintings of aurochs are 40,000 years old.

p.51

In India, there are Bhimbetka rock shelters with paintings dating from 30,000 years ago. Indonesia though, has the current prize for the oldest figurative artwork at Sulawesi. Here, rock paintings have been discovered which are nearly 40,000 years old. They show dwarf buffaloes and mammals being hunted by figures part-human and part-animal. It may be the scene was painted over a number of years. Adam Brumm, archaeologist at the Research Centre for Human Evolution at Griffith University, Australia said: “We were stunned by the implications of this image,” and “It suggested to us that this extraordinary scene suggests a story or some kind of myth.” This seems clear evidence that some rock paintings, or perhaps all of them, are associated with stories of a world beyond man.

But the best-known and most prolific region for these paintings is Europe, particularly in France and Spain. Nearly 350 sites have been discovered in these two countries. The artistic endeavors of our ancestors in gloomy rock shelters cover a span of more than 20,000 years; dating from the Upper Palaeolithic to the Neolithic age. Some of the earliest, i.e. Upper Palaeolithic were found at the Chauvet-Pont-d’Arc cave, in the Ardeche region of southern France. This cave is huge and contains a series of chambers. It would have made fine living accommodation for a Stone Age clan. The animals depicted on the walls were drawn by skilled artists approximately 32,000 years ago. An unusual feature is the subject matter of the paintings. Normally these would be hunting scenes, depicting prey animals, but many of the animals on the rock walls of Chauvet are dangerous. They include lions; bears and rhinoceros. Perhaps the Chauvet clansmen were particularly macho, and liked to court with danger when hunting!

The most famous cave paintings are those found at Lascaux, in the Dordogne region of France. Some 600 images have been discovered here, dating back 17,000 years. Lascaux is the Stone Age equivalent of the National Portrait Gallery. Except that most images are of horses, stags and bison. These animal animals were of more interest and importance to Stone Agers than the human animal of latter-day portraitists.

There are some human images too but not many. One is of a man who has a bird’s head. It’s suggested he could be a shaman, who carried out rituals in the cave. Other theories say some of the paintings are linked with the constellations of the Pleiades and Taurus. These might be associated with ritual dancing. In some cases, this can induce trances and cause visions. What is for sure is at Lascaux there was no Palaeolithic equivalent of Constable, or Turner either, because landscapes are notable by their absence.

The archaeologist, Lewis-Williams, has drawn an interesting parallel between Palaeolithic rock art, typified by Chauvet and Lascaux, with that of the modern San bushmen of the Kalahari. He suggests that by studying the San we can get a clue as to why Stone Age artists depicted mainly prey animals. The San used their drawings as a link to the spirit world. Perhaps Stone Age man had this intention too. Curiously, this has some vague similarity with Plato’s idea of “perfect forms” as the essence of animals.

Cult of The Bear

It was not only through cave paintings that Stone Agers depicted their spiritual attachment to prey animals (assuming Lewis-Williams is correct). Palaeolithic people had some affinity with cave bears; massive creatures, up to 800 kilograms in weight. We know this from the thousands of cave bear skulls found. Some of these were seemingly placed in specific positions which could indicate a type of spiritual ritual. We also know, from ancient cultures that still survive into fairly modern times, that the cult of the bear is not uncommon. Bear cultism survived mostly in Northern Polar Regions.

The Aino people (in their language their name means people, who number about 150,000 in Japan still have a bear God named Kamui which is also a general name for Gods. They eat bears believing the meat and fur was a gift from their God.

Other Northern ethnic groups to engage in bear worship include the Sami (or Laplanders); pre-Christian Finns and the Nivkh. The latter are now just a few thousands occupying land in the vicinity of the Amur River in Russia. They still practice shamanism and hold a winter Bear Festival. They think the bear is an earthly materialisation of both ancestors and Gods.

Voluptuous Venus

p.53

So, we have some archaeological evidence of cave paintings with a possible spiritual dimension; ritual burial and cave bear cultism. What else is there that indicates shamanistic, primal religion abounded in the Stone Age and after?

Well, there have also been finds of voluptuous female figurines symbolizing not only fertility but the important status of women in Stone Age societies. Of the figurines, one of the best known is the Venus of Willendorf. This carved figure was discovered in 1908 near Willendorf in Austria. It dates from about 23,000 to 25,000 years ago. An even older Venus figurine was found in 2008 at Schelklingen, Germany - the Venus of Hohle Fels. It dates from at least 35,000 years ago. That suggests pre-historic Venus sculptors were at it for at least 10,000 years; not quite as long as their cave painting brethren, but still an impressive period of time.

Most Venus figurines were found in Europe but discoveries stretch to Eurasia, with the furthest find, so far, at Lake Baikal in western Russia.

These small sculptures are thought to represent either an Earth mother Goddess or fertility charm, perhaps both. Either way they were a symbolic object to which Palaeolithic man, or woman, attached some form of supernatural or spiritual power.

Much of the Stone Age focus so far has been on Europe. That’s because we have more evidence from here of the Palaeolithic Transition than anywhere else. But Homo Sapiens populated nearly all of the rest of the planet before they were found in Europe. How and when did this happen? Well, let’s have a look at the story of mankind’s, i.e. Homo Sapiens, origin and associated dispersal theories. As Prof. Alice Roberts said in 2008: “We’re all members of a young species… Wherever we’ve ended up, all over the world, we’re Africans under the skin. And uncovering that story, retracing the steps of our ancestors, has given me a profound sense of our common humanity: our shared past, and our shared future”

Uncovering the human story reveals much about why many humans hold belief in supernatural entities.
