Chapter 12

Now for The Pharaohs and Ancient Egypt

Pages 98–107

To the west of Mesopotamia and the Hittite empire was another fertile area. This also had an inviting climate, and a major river, for transport and irrigation, the Nile. The region was relatively easily accessible from MH original home in Africa. It’s not surprising then that another early, major civilisation grew up here, in ancient Egypt.

According to some the first Pharaoh (Egyptian King) was Narmer also known as Menes. He started to unify Upper and Lower Egypt about 3,000 BCE. At this time, Egypt was contemporary with Sumer, some claim Egyptian civilisation is even older than Sumer. Egypt began as a narrow strip of land, about 20 Km wide, bordering the fertile flood plain of the River Nile. It stretched 500 Km from the Mediterranean. Eventually the Egyptian Empire expanded to control an area hundreds of kilometres long north to south bordering the Red Sea and west to east along the Mediterranean coast. To the west ancient Egypt, later under Ramesses II, went into part of what is now Libya and to the east through modern day Sinai and Syria. Much was sparsely populated desert or semi-desert. In terms of size, this Empire was probably larger than any preceding or contemporary empire between the Persian Gulf and Mediterranean or Black Sea.

Ancient Egypt was a dominant power in the region for more than 2,000 years until the Assyrian Ashurbanipal’s army arrived in 667 BCE. Apart from his fame as a conqueror, Ashurbanipal also gains kudos for establishing the oldest library in the world at Nineveh, his capital.

p.99

Egyptian Pharaohs had a long run before Ashurbanipal upset the apple cart. They even continued after him, for another 600 or so years, until Cleopatra VII had the distinction of being the last of the Pharaohs. But they never again wielded the same influence and power. So, what can be said about the great line of pharaohs who ruled Egypt for such a huge length of time, nearly 3,000 years? Well, apart from being rulers of the longest lasting empire, Pharaohs had the biggest, most luxurious, burial tombs on planet Earth. They are the pyramids of Egypt.

See below the pyramids in the Giza complex:

There are over 100 pyramids built by various Egyptian royalty. The first was completed by 2611 BCE, for Pharaoh Djoser and the last by Pharaoh Ahmose, more than 1,000 years later, in 1525 BCE. These colossal constructions were amazing feats of architecture and building. They required enormous resources to complete and were a testament to the mighty power of the reigning Pharaoh.

Like Mesopotamian rulers and Hittites, the Pharaoh was considered divine while retaining his humanity. He was most closely identified as Horus, son of the God Osiris. Ancient Egypt’s rulers therefore followed the established trend of claiming Godly authority over their subjects.

p.100

When passing on from their earthly life the Pharaohs joined the Gods, so they hoped, and achieved some form of existence after death. The mummified bodies of some have been dis-entombed from their original resting places, 2,000 or so years later, by eager archaeologists. In some cases, the prospect of unearthing gold and valuable objects, was the main driver. Much of what western Egyptologists and explorers unearthed in the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries now reside in various European museums. Their findings gave fresh insight into long dead Egyptian Pharaohs.

Among those Pharaohs who now enjoy a new remembrance, either through being de-mummified or other reasons are:

Akhenaten, originally Amenhotep IV

1353 to 1336 BCE

This chap might have been fourth but clearly he wanted to be noted as first at something. The Greek form of his name meant “satisfied”. He was far from this, starting with his discontent with Thebes as Egypt’s capital. Akhenaten was prepared to ignore Thebes long history as Egypt’s governing city. He set up his own capital city. Moving downstream to Amarna he established his new capital. But Akhenaten wanted more than just a new location with new scenery. To emphasise who it was that founded Egypt’s new capital he had it named Akhenaten, this was followed by much building of palaces and temples. For speed, they were constructed with small and easily removable mud bricks - a fatal error in terms of ensuring longevity. Then he had a close look at Egyptian traditional religion, with its many, many Gods. This polytheism is not good enough he thought. Someone has to be the sole, supreme Godly authority. The mighty shining sun, Aten, was Akhenaten’s choice of a pre-eminent, universal God. He also promoted himself to God status as the son of Aten.

It’s possible that Akhenaten suffered from an inherited form of epilepsy. This can cause religious visions on sunny days. If so, hence his monotheistic sun God Aten. Pharaoh Amenhotep IV became Pharaoh Akhenaten.

So, with a new capital and new religion, this was a good start in making sure posterity would remember him. He made a little mistake though in the choice of one of his five consorts, Nefertiti (meaning: “The beautiful one has come forth.”). She assumed the status a female God.

p.101

Nefertiti’s name is better remembered than Akhenaten, mainly the result of her lovely gold bust, that is a statue type not bra type. Of course, she may also have had a gold bust of the other sort as well, given Egypt’s sunny climate.

Nefertiti’s bust is kept at the Berlin Museum. Here she is.

Nefertiti tried hard to keep things going once her husband had died. She approached the Hittite King and asked him to send one of his sons so she could marry him. Had she succeeded in joining the two main powers of the region, she might have established a new, huge and powerful empire. However, the priests of the old God Amun were not happy at the prospect of a continuation of Akhenaten’s form of monotheism. They had the Hittite prince murdered on his way to Egypt.

Akhenaten’s capital, its temples and palaces, were pulled down and plundered - the small bricks were relatively easy to remove. The God Amun disappeared from the main scene, the old Egyptian religion was re-established and Nefertiti faded into the background. But Akhenaten still had one last card to play in ensuring his name lived on, although he could not know that this was a top trump. His progeny included the legendary Tutankhamun.

Tutankhamun

1336 to 1327 BCE

Poor boy, he became chief aged 9 and, a sickly youth, died at 19. Tut was not much of a Pharaoh, no serious smiting of enemies, no new territories added to the Egyptian empire, no new God, no interesting consort or new capital city. He was quite boring really. But Tut was smart enough to change his name from Tutankhaten to Tutankhamun. The winds of change were clear to him, or more likely to his advisers. His father’s new Godly supremo, Aten, was on the way out. God Amun and the multiplicity of Egyptian Gods were back in the ascendency.

p.102

He was fortunate to have a well-hidden tomb, unlike the more demonstrative constructions of other Pharaohs. Tut’s tomb in the Valley of The Kings remained undiscovered and unrobbed for 3,000 years, until Howard Carter arrived in 1922. Then Tut became the most talked about Pharaoh in the world. This is because of the stunning finds of jewelled chairs, gold death mask, golden coffins, three of them, and chariots among thousands of other items.

All this, together with his mummified body was contained in one of the smallest Egyptian tombs. One intriguing find was of a golden fan. This originally had 42 ostrich feathers. The tale of the fan, etched on its handle, says these feathers were from birds the Pharaoh himself had hunted. Some Egyptologists speculate this could suggest the Pharaoh died on an ostrich hunt. Hunting ostriches though must have been safer than hunting lions.

Ramesses II

1279 to 1213 BCE

This Pharaoh was known as Ramesses the great for good reason. He reigned for 66 years and made his mark on Egypt and beyond. Under him Egypt took back control of two territories, the Levant in the east and Nubia in the south. He had a massive battle at Kadesh (Syria) with the Hittites. Up to 6,000 chariots and 50,000 infantry were involved. Employing an early form of fake news Ramesses claimed victory. Historians think it more likely to have been a draw. The Hittites won the first half but Ramesses warriors came back to take the second half.

p.103

As a result, Ramesses showed what wise statesman he was. He signed a peace treaty with King Hattusilis of the Hittites. Ramesses gained a historical accolade for the first recorded peace treaty between two states. As part of the deal two Hittite princesses joined Ramesses collection of wives. These two were just add-ons. His Great Royal Wife and favourite was Nefertari. Her name meant beautiful companion. Rameses built a great temple for his favourite wife at Abu Simbel and had a greater one constructed for himself.

Abu Simbel, Temple of Nefertari on the left and Ramesses II on the right

Nefertari has appeared in novels and in these films: Exodus: Gods and Kings 2014; The Ten Commandments 1956 and The Mummy Returns 2001. She did not make any money though from these artistic depictions of her, having been dead for 3,200 years by the time they appeared.

Like Akhenaten before him, Rameses also wanted to establish his own capital. He chose Piramesse, on the eastern side of the Nile delta. Pi - Ramesse meant the domain of Ramesses. Centuries later, due to the shifting of the Nile’s deltaic course, this city silted up and was abandoned. French Egyptologist, Pierre Montet re-discovered it in 1929. Pierre was quite a thorough Egyptian tomb raider. He discovered or disentombed Egyptian pharaohs at Tanis (an ancient capital in lower Egypt): Psusennes I, Amenemope, and Shoshenq II and Takelot I.

Pierre found gold belonging to Osorkon I here including the representation of a Godly triad, Osiris, with his son Horus on the left and wife Isis on the right.

When it came to tomb raiding those films of Indiana Jones and Lara Croft had nothing on Pierre.

Cleopatra

69 to 30 BC

p.104

We’ve now heard of Nefertiti and Nefertari, but the most famous Egyptian woman of all is Cleopatra, or Cleopatra VII Philopator to give her full name. Well, you would think that she was Egyptian, but no, Cleo was a Greek. Some 250 years before Cleo Alexander the Great had taken control of Egypt. He even adopted the Egyptian God Amun. The Greek dynasty Ptolemy followed and Cleo was a descendant of this family. The Rosetta Stone, key to the translation of Egyptian hieroglyphs, was inscribed in the time of one of the Ptolemy’s, number V.

Cleo at first ruled jointly, with her father, later with her two brothers. But she was the real power. A clever woman, she spoke several languages. Cleo claimed to be the reincarnation of the Goddess Isis, no doubt expecting this to give her greater authority. She tried to re-establish Egyptian prominence by cementing close ties with the burgeoning power of Rome. So close were these ties that she bore a son, Caesarion, to Julius Caesar.

Cleopatra, holding an ankh and going for a bath?

After Caesar’s assassination Cleo attached herself to Mark Antony, bearing him three children. He was a contender for head of the Roman Empire. She backed the wrong man though; Antony and Cleo’s forces were defeated by Octavian at the Battle of Actium. Shortly after that Antony and Cleo committed suicide. Her departure was via an asp, a poisonous snake, although this is disputed. Young Caesarion, was killed by Octavian, who became Emperor Augustus, and Egypt was demoted to become a Roman province. Cleo obviously tried to cultivate powerful friends but ended up putting all her eggs in the wrong basket. 1,600 years after her demise, Cleo became a Shakespearean star and later still appeared in films.

Ancient Egyptian Religion, Science and Women of Power

When it came to Gods, ancient Egyptians did not do things by halves, but by thousands. It is estimated there were well over 2,000 Egyptian Gods and Goddesses, all were anthropomorphic types. Some Gods had the head of an animal on the body of a man. Egypt at this time was the epitome of polytheism.

p.105

There was something of a changing hierarchy. Those Gods at the top of the tree varied over time, not surprising since the religion lasted over 3,000 years. At one stage Ra, the sun God was top dog, later it was the turn of the creator God Amun and then Isis, a mother Goddess. For a short while Aten, on his own, was elevated by Pharaoh Akhenaten.

The Egyptians had three sun Gods, the rising sun was Horus (more on him later), the hot, strong mid-day sun was Ra and the evening sun was creator God Amun. How far did Horus rise before he had to give way to Ra? Exactly what time did Amun take over from Ra? Egyptians did not have super-accurate atomic clocks to help them so there must have been a bit of uncertainty, at change-over time, about which God was now in the sky. The Gods themselves might have engaged in a few punch-ups because of the inexact junctures. Other well-known Gods included Osiris, Anubis and Horus.

Gods Osiris, Anubis and Horus

Osiris (god of the afterlife, rebirth, fertility and agriculture), Anubis, God of the dead, and Horus, at first a sky God then God of royalty. Horus beat up Seth, for killing Osiris and then used his gouged-out eye to resuscitate him. So, the “eye of Horus” is associated with sacrifice and healing.

Another God associated with Horus is his mother, Isis. She was also the wife of Osiris. Isis was one of the greatest Egyptian Goddesses; revered as a mother figure and thought to have magical powers.

Isis - A ‘goodie’ Egyptian Goddess, she protected women and children and healed the sick

Yet another Goddess was Hathor, originally a cow Goddess, associated with the Milky Way. The Milky Way was thought to be the milk of a heavenly cow. Hathor challenged Isis, eventually, for the title of the most renowned Goddess. She was worshipped in several countries outside of Egypt; seen as the Goddess of women, childbirth, beauty and the stars among other things. Like Hathor, Egyptian Gods and Goddess, changed their attributes and sometimes their names over time.

p.106

Religion permeated every aspect of Egyptian life. There were Gods or Goddesses for every occasion and for every natural aspect - sun, moon, motherhood etc. Egyptians always had one or other specialist God they could pray or appeal to. Fortunately for them, their religion did not require them to pray regular to all 2,000+.

There were lots of temples and rituals. Some annual festivals included a procession, such as Opet Festival celebrated in Thebes. This was where the God’s image in a model ship was trundled around temples. Opet was the mother of Osiris. The Pharaoh was seen as the intermediary between people and the Gods. Egyptians believed in an afterlife and thought the souls of the dead could be preserved after death by proper provision of grave goods. They believed in an original creation too. There were slight variations of a basic creation myth which said the dry earth emerged from an ocean of chaos.

Egyptian’s may have been the original cat lovers. Egyptian cats did not just have staff, they had followers. The reverence and love of cats was such that Egyptians had a domestic cat Goddess, Bastet. She was thought to protect against evil and had a festival in the northern city of Bubastis.

Goddess Bastet

Religion in ancient Egypt was a huge industry. It supported those in power, particularly the divine Pharaoh. Resources invested were large but Egypt could afford it, benefitting from its wealth as a country astride the mighty and life-giving River Nile. Wealth gave them time to think as well as revere their Gods. They were expert mathematicians and astronomers. The first known measure of weight was the Egyptian beqa. They invented the 24 hour day, a base 10 numbering system and perhaps the first sun-dials. They used the stars as their calendar. Their year was 365 days but with 12 months of 30 days plus 5 feast days. The summer solstice marked day one.

Egyptian women? Women rights were not perfect, but certainly ahead of their time in ancient Egypt. They were legally equal to men in court and could own property. Some women were allowed to become powerful figures too. There were women Pharaohs, sometimes disguised in the form of a regency, such notables were: Nitocris; Sobekneferu; Hatshepsut; Neferneferuaten, Twosret and several Cleopatras.

p.107

Hatshepsut - 5th Pharaoh of the 18th Dynasty Hatshepsut was the most successful of these women, and had the longest reign, possibly 21 years. Unlike most male Pharaohs, Hatshepsut avoided regular military conflict with surrounding states. Nubia was an exception. Nubia was sorted out militarily with Hatshepsut firmly at the fore of the conflict. Nubia aside, trade and diplomacy were Hatshepsut’s weapons of choice. Her clever diplomacy helped Egypt become wealthier by establishing new trade routes and good relations with surrounding states. She also had hundreds of new buildings constructed, including the colonnaded Temple of Karnak with its statue of Rameses II.

Other than Hatshepsut there were the powerful consorts Nefertiti and Nefertari. About 350 AD Hypatia, a philosopher and teacher, was a major figure in Egypt’s Alexandria. After the Islamic conquest of 642 AD women were not allowed to achieve nationally powerful positions. Egypt has not seen any women of similar prominence to those of old Egypt in the 1,400 hundred years since the arrival of Islam.

Ancient Egypt’s Decline

It may be that Ashurbanipal, in 667 BCE brought about the demise of the Egyptian hegemony, as mentioned earlier. However, the decline and the final and complete disappearance of Egyptian Gods, hieroglyphics and power may have been a more gradual process, over 1,200 years or so, from the time Ashurbanipal. Other major players in ancient Egypt’s downfall include the Greek, Alexander the Great in 332 BCE; the Roman Emperor Augustus (Octavian) in 30 BCE; Roman Christian Emperor, 306 to 337 CE and finally the Rashidun caliphate who oversaw the Muslim conquest of Egypt during 639 to 646 CE.
