Chapter 20

The 4 Horsemen of new Atheism - plus 1 Horsewoman?

Pages 215–219

This is a reference to four successful authors and debaters whose works really took off not long after the devastating attacks called ‘9/11’. These took place in 2001 in the USA. Nearly 3,000 people were killed.

Their works might, just might, be the slow burning fuse setting off a religious apocalypse, but it’s clear that religion is far from coming to an end yet, if ever. The 4 set out their criticism of religion and, powerfully articulated the case for atheism, or very thin agnosticism, in these books, among others:

  1. Richard Dawkins - “The God Delusion”;
  2. Daniel Dennett - “Breaking the Spell”;
  3. Sam Harris - “The End of Faith, Letter to a Christian Nation”;
  4. Christopher Hitchens - “God is not Great”.

Since these 4 came to prominence, Susan Blackmore, with her ideas on consciousness, and memes might be added, as a horsewoman of atheism.

Richard Dawkins

Richard is an evolutionary biologist and a very successful author. He was Oxford University’s Professor for Public Understanding of Science for thirteen years, from 1995 to 2008. He has won a number of awards. His main claim to fame though comes from his books which attack religious belief. These have been the subject of films and TV programmes. The main point he makes is that there is no evidence supporting any form of religious belief. He particularly has it in for religious extremists: “Religious fanatics want people to switch off their own minds, ignore the evidence, and blindly follow a holy book based upon private ‘revelation’.”

Of many penetrating observations by Richard is: “one of the truly bad effects of religion is that it teaches us that it is a virtue to be satisfied with not understanding.” and, “It’s a tragedy that people are being actively led astray by medieval or pre-medieval superstitions and bronze age myths written down more than 2,000 years ago.” About the Bible, he points out one of the blatant mistakes, concerning the Catholic Church’s claims of Jesus resulting from a virgin birth. Richard says this is simply wrong.

It’s because of a mis-translation from Isaiah into Matthew. The original Hebrew referred to “young woman” but it was translated into the Greek word for “virgin”. In his 1996 book, “The Blind Watchmaker”, he argues forcefully against the ‘intelligent design’ claimed proof of a God. Evolution, selection and mutation are not something that is guided by a supernatural being according to Richard. He pointed out that Charles Darwin, in 1859, explained natural selection was the process by which all life evolved. See TED 2002, Militant Atheism, here he talks about Creationism as opposed to evolution. His best seller: “The Selfish Gene” adds more support to Darwin and introduces the ‘meme’, an ideas replicator.

Richard’s later, 2006 book, ‘The God Delusion’ takes a big swipe at more attempted proofs of God’s existence. There are six main points by way of explaining his arguments against the attempted proofs of God’s existence:

  1. Throughout time, it has been hard to explain why everything looks like it has been designed by a designer.
  2. It’s a natural urge to say a designer made everything.
  3. Ultimately a designer causes more problems, especially because who designed the designer?
  4. Natural selection is that problem solving theory. Creationism is a trick.
  5. We cannot fully explain how everything got here.
  6. Just as it took scientists time to discover Natural Selection, it will also take time to discover where everything came from and the weak theories we have now are at least better than anything a theist has ever produced or will produce.

His astutely framed logic offers serious refutation of Godly existence.

Richard gave an example of the possible range of beliefs on a scale 1 to 7. Here, 1 (one) would be for those who have absolute, certain sure belief and 7 complete non-belief. He has been careful to explain that he is not quite an atheist, saying he was at 6 on his 1 to 7 scale. This 6 score, he said, represented for him the very low possibility a God existed, to the same extent of his belief there are fairies and unicorns. Richard is a wafer-thin agnostic then! He explained his belief was passionate, rather than fundamentalist. In a 2007 entry on his website headed: “How dare you call me a fundamentalist”, Richard wrote: “No, please, do not mistake passion, which can change its mind, for fundamentalism, which never will. Passion for passion, an evangelical Christian and I may be evenly matched. But we are not equally fundamentalist. The true scientist, however passionately he may ‘believe’, in evolution for example, knows exactly what would change his mind: evidence! The fundamentalist knows that nothing will!” His general stance against all religion is summed up by his comment: “Religion is a virus of the mind.”

Daniel Dennett

Dan’s specialist areas include: philosophy of science, philosophy of mind and philosophy of biology. He is an atheist and secularist. Like Richard Dawkins, he too is associated with Oxford University. After studying under Gilbert Ryle, he received his Doctor of Philosophy there in 1965. Again, like Richard, he has been given numerous awards.

His book “Breaking the Spell” sets out a cogent argument that religious belief is a natural phenomenon. That’s because it has had an evolutionary advantage and it is a part of the human psyche to lean towards some form of supernatural belief. He credits the work of anthropologists Pascal Boyer and Scott Atran. These demonstrate the human tendency to believe in spirits and ghosts. Dan clearly expresses his views on religion and the unfounded sureness of its chief proponents: “I for one am not in awe of your faith. I am appalled by your arrogance, by your unreasoning certainty that you have all the answers.” and, “I am confident that those who believe in belief are wrong. That is, we no more need to preserve the myth of God in order to preserve a just and stable society than we need to cling to the Gold Standard to keep our currency sound.” What Dan is saying here is relevant to all religions and societies throughout history. States of all persuasions have used religion a device to keep people in check. He also says religions are natural phenomena, re-designed by humans over thousands of years. View his TED talk, TED2006, “Let’s teach religion, all religion, in schools.” Just teach facts, not values, he said. His talk was basically about the key role of evolution in refuting the claim of Intelligent Design.

In “Darwin’s Dangerous Ideas” Dan said: “I think Darwin’s idea of natural selection is the best idea anybody ever had.” In addition, argued that evolution can account for the origin of morality. He co-authored a book in 2013 with Linda La Scola: “Caught in the Pulpit: Leaving Belief Behind”. This was based on fascinating research into clerics who were actually atheists in secret. These clerics kept their jobs on the basis that they were doing some good by providing the comfort that religion can bring to people. They also had a non-altruistic attachment to their jobs, with the finance, homes and security that their church provided.

Dan accepts that people can gain from religious beliefs, whether or not their beliefs are mistaken. He’s written: “I have known people whose lives would be desolate and friendless if it weren’t for the non-judgmental welcome they have received in one religious organisation or another.”

Sam Harris

p.218

Here’s the third horseman. Sam’s major tome is: “The End of Faith, Letter to a Christian Nation”. He’s an American neuroscientist and philosopher. His trenchant criticism of religion can be gleaned from some of these things he’s said: “The problem with faith, is that it really is a conversation stopper. Faith is a declaration of immunity to the powers of conversation. It is a reason, why you do not have to give reasons, for what you believe.” He added: “We have names for people who have many beliefs for which there is no rational justification. When their beliefs are extremely common we call them ‘religious’; otherwise, they are likely to be called ‘mad’, ‘psychotic’ or ‘delusional’… Clearly there is sanity in numbers. And yet, it is merely an accident of history that it is considered normal in our society to believe that the Creator of the universe can hear your thoughts, while it is demonstrative of mental illness to believe that he is communicating with you by having the rain tap in Morse Code on your bedroom window. And so, while religious people are not generally mad, their core beliefs absolutely are.”

Christopher Hitchens

p.219

This determined, and fiercely combative atheist wrote an international best-seller: “God is Not Great”. In which he claimed religion poisons everything. Curiously, he was respected by some believers. As he was suffering from terminal cancer, an informal group promoted “Everybody pray for Hitchens day”. Not unexpectedly, the prayers were insufficient because Christopher died of cancer related pneumonia in December 2011.

His personality, wit and debating expertise made him a favourite for media interviews and TV. Some quotes that encapsulate his anti-religious stance: “To terrify children with the image of hell, to consider women an inferior creation - is that good for the world?” “Owners of dogs will have noticed that, if you provide them with food and water and shelter and affection, they will think you are God. Whereas owners of cats are compelled to realize that, if you provide them with food and water and shelter and affection, they draw the conclusion that they are Gods.” “Human decency is not derived from religion. It precedes it.” and finally: “Many religions now come before us with ingratiating smirks and outspread hands, like an unctuous merchant in a bazaar. They offer consolation and solidarity and uplift, competing as they do in a marketplace. But we have a right to remember how barbarically they behaved when they were strong and were making an offer that people could not refuse.” Maybe a little bit unfair this last about the gentle souls of the TDF crowd - see chapter 22.

Susan Blackmore

Prof. Sue Blackmore is a freelance writer, lecturer and broadcaster. She is an atheist with a keen interest in the mind, consciousness and memetics - the theory of memes. Following up on Richard Dawkins concept of memes her several books include ‘The Meme Machine’. A meme she describes as “that which is imitated”, or “information that is copied”. Further explaining that at the base of human evolution there are two replicators on the planet: 1. Genes; 2. Memes. She adds a new, third replicator, the “teme”, something that propagates via new technology.

Just as the 4 horsemen, Sue is a great admirer of Charles Darwin and his Genesis-shattering discovery - evolution. The evolutionary algorithm she explained, as have others: “If you have variation, selection and heredity then you must get evolution.” Her key word is “must”. As with the 4 horsemen, there is no mention by Sue of a God’s involvement in this process. In an interview by Robert Kuhn she said: “I couldn’t find any evidence of life after death.” And, “…certain things won’t work. You know, inventing spirits and souls.” (see the 5 W’s analysis of souls in Chapter 18a)

She has interesting, and somewhat original views of self, free will and consciousness: “Actions do exist and also their consequences, but the person who acts does not.” - see YouTube, August 2017. Jordan Peterson, God supporter, who said the Bible, was a route for fallen humanity, engaged in debate with Sue in the Big Conversation, June 2018. Sue appeared more credible, and relaxed than the author of “Twelve Rules for Life”. Jordan’s best seller includes the charming Rule 12, “Pet a cat when you encounter one on the street.” Yes indeed, but to avoid alienating canine supporters, and his daughter Mikhaila, let alone Sikko, his loved, clever dog, Jordan sensibly does include the petting of dogs.
