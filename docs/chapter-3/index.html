COTHAM - the six “proofs”, what do they mean and where do they come from?

There are more attempted proofs than these 6. Anselm came up with a few more - weak, and rarely mentioned. If you want to see some brilliant spoofs try Rebecca Newberger Goldstein’s book: “36 Arguments for the Existence of God”. These include: “The Argument from Dappled Things”, “The Argument from the Eternity of Irony” and “The Argument from Fraught Distance”. You can probably get the drift from these titles. I’m going to pass on these and concentrate on the 6 proofs that are the most commonly mentioned by deeply thoughtful believers.

1. Cosmological Argument

An eternal God, called the unmoved mover (after Aristotle) lights the blue touch paper to kick-off the universe. HE’s the prime source. This is also known as the First Cause. In essence, this is the cosmological argument for the existence of God.

St Thomas Aquinas (1225-1274) is the original main champion of the cosmological argument. He was later supported by René Descartes. In more recent times, William Craig’s 1979 book, The Kalām Cosmological Argument, attempted to give support to this idea.

More than a thousand years before Aquinas, those very clever Greeks, Aristotle and Plato, thought the universe had no beginning and no end, so a God is not needed they concluded. And Parmenides, who preceded them said: “nothing comes from nothing”, “ergo as God is something HE cannot come from nothing.” We have no evidence to suggest Aquinas view is superior to that of the ancient Greeks.

David Hume argued against the Cosmological Argument. In effect he said: 1. We can conceive of things without a cause, and 2. Explanation of the parts is sufficient. He meant we can look at and explain individual items within our universe, but we don’t need to explain the whole thing.

p.22

Immanuel Kant, slightly later than Hume, claimed that God is not logically necessary. In 1947 Bertrand Russell, termed his objection: “The Fallacy of Composition”. What he meant was just because parts of something have a certain property it does not mean the whole does too, e.g. if a fence is made up of small panels the whole fence is not necessarily going to have the property of smallness. Ergo, because everything in the universe has a cause it does not mean the whole universe has to have a cause. Bertrand is with those ancient Greeks, saying the universe just is. In modern times, renowned quantum physicist, Stephen Hawking, sided with Bertrand Russell. He said we do not need God to light the blue touch paper, i.e. kick off the Big Bang.

The Cosmological argument should not be confused with the Cosmological Constant. This is a term Einstein used when inserting a figure into his equations to fit in with his “steady-state” theory of the universe. Steady-state was dropped when universal expansion became irrefutable, proving yet again that even the greatest minds can get it wrong.

In science, the origin of our universe begins with the Big Bang. Cosmologists cannot yet say what preceded the Big Bang, although the Multiverse concept looms larger and larger. There is certainly no hard, compelling evidence, for the Cosmological argument. It is not a proof of God’s existence.

2. Ontological Argument

Anselm of Canterbury in 1078 put forward this idea. It requires thinking of the greatest being you can possibly conceive of. Then, Anselm says, because this thought is in the mind there must be a greater being, one who exists in reality as well as the mind.

Anselm’s thought was certainly original and at first glance a bit weird. Maybe at second and third glance too for many people. This idea had great support from René Descartes and Gottfried Leibnitz.

The ever-pragmatic David Hume rejected it saying in effect: “where’s the evidence?” David argued that we humans are capable of imagining anything, including a God.

p.23

He went on to say that just because we can imagine it does not mean the thing exists in reality. Aquinas also dismissed the concept (points scoring? - see philosophers’ chapter) so did Immanuel Kant. The ontological argument has survived nearly 1,000 years. It continues to be debated right up to the present day. It was discussed on BBC Radio 4 programme “In Our Time” in 2012.

3. Teleological Argument

This is also known as argument by design. In essence, it is claimed that the world and every plant and animal is so perfect it could only have come about by the expressed design of God. William Paley (1743-1805) put the case quite succinctly in his book Natural Theology.

Paley imagined someone finding a watch and reasoned:

• The existence of the watch is proof of a watchmaker; • The watch could not have come about by accident (unlike a rock or stone); • The universe is intricate in its design (e.g. molecular structure of ice, the position of the Earth from the Sun etc.); • The universe could not come about by accident; • The universe itself is proof of an intelligent designer (but needs a bit more of a super designer than the watch).

The teleological argument is a favourite of the Intelligent Design people. Plants and animals, including humans, are perfectly adapted to their environment, through God’s design. Thus, claim the IDers. David Hume argued that the apparently ordered world could come about by chance. Darwin, assisted by Mendel, put meat on the bone by showing that the adaptation of flora and fauna has come about through hundreds of millions of years of evolutionary honing.

p.24

And in respect of inanimate objects, many can be designed by humans and made from artificial material like plastic. They are only perfect if their human designer and maker is of sufficient skill and the materials worked on are also up to the job. Richard Dawkins, evolutionary biologist, puts it like this: “Paley’s argument is made with passionate sincerity and is informed by the best biological scholarship of the day, but it is wrong, gloriously and utterly wrong… All appearances to the contrary, the only watchmaker in nature is the blind force of physics … Natural selection, the blind unconscious, automatic process which Darwin discovered, and which we now know is the explanation for the existence and apparently purposeful form of all life, has no purpose in mind. … If it can be said to play the role of watchmaker in nature, it is the blind watchmaker.” Both Fred Hoyle and Professor of Christian Religion, Richard Swinburne were proponents of intelligent design. Richard Dawkins, in his book: The Blind Watchmaker said in answer to these two: “Evolution has no long-term goal. There is no long-distance target, no final perfection to serve as a criterion for selection… The criteria for selection are always short-term, either simply survival, or, more generally, reproductive success.” Richard Dawkins followed up these ideas in his aptly titled book: “The Selfish Gene.”

5. Anthropic Principle

This argument is relatively modern. It’s closely connected to the teleological, design proof of God’s existence. Where it differs is it is much broader in scope and more basic than the design idea. It focuses on our entire universe; looking back to its very beginning. The Anthropic Principle says that the underlying physical laws of our universe are so finely tuned that they eventually enable us humans to exist. In their 1989 book John Gribbin and Martin Rees made this argument, saying: “The conditions in our Universe really do seem to be uniquely suitable for life forms like ourselves.” Very tiny differences in the laws of physics could mean that the strong nuclear force was not sufficiently strong to enable elementary particles to form atoms, and perhaps even for any form of organic complexity.

Other laws, of gravity and quantum mechanics could also have been different, causing mayhem with the structure of our universe and nullifying the possibility of any life. Ergo, with different physics, after the Big Bang 13.8 billion years ago, elementary particles would have remained just that. No atoms would have formed. That means there would be no matter as we know it. Stars, Planets and Galaxies would not have been created. We would not have any place from which we could evolve into being. Closely connected to the Anthropic Principle is the purported Goldilocks Zone. This is the zone in which planet Earth is perfectly located. The Earth is just the right distance from the sun so that it is neither too hot (like Mercury and Venus) or too cold like (Mars, the gas and ice giants). That means water can exist in liquid form on Earth.

There’s Jupiter, a giant planet positioned just far enough away to be able to scoop up, with its massive gravity, enough comets and other space debris so that Earth would not be blasted into lifelessness in the early days of its 4.6-billion-year existence. But Jupiter is not so close that it attracted all such comets. Many of them were water bearing. Earth needed these to form the oceans, lakes and rivers. Water is the base of all life, as we know it.

The Theists say the Anthropic Principle demonstrates that a guiding hand, i.e. God, exists. Countering this claim are two arguments.

p.26

The first simply says it’s a matter of observation. That is, we humans are bound to recognize what universe we are in and its physical laws, because we are here. The second contention says our universe may be one of countless trillions, one in a multiverse. This is a suggestion made by Martin Rees, Astronomer Royal and Emeritus Professor of Cosmology and Astrophysics at Cambridge University. Each of the universes in the countless trillions within the multiverse, so speculate some cosmologists, could have different physical laws. There is such a vast number of universes, one of them is certain to have the right laws and a Goldilocks Zone to allow our existence.

The multiverse idea is unproven but growing in popularity. It is an argument against both the Anthropic Principle and Cosmological claimed proofs.

6. Moralistic Theory

Religious theorists argue that since every person has awareness of some form of morality, that is what is right and what is wrong, this is a norm for all humans. The source of this moral normality, they say, can only be God. Therefore, God must exist, according to this moralistic theory.

Many philosophers, as well as theologians argue in favour of a moral or ethical lifestyle. But the philosophers do not necessarily bring God into the argument. From ancient eastern traditions Confucius was a great supporter of personal and governmental morality. He stressed the importance of sincerity and justice. Buddhism teaches ethical conduct as part of the eightfold path. Truth is an aspect of morality. Interestingly the existence in the Bible of lies, what is now termed “fake-news” has been pointed to by Pope Francis in 2018. He said that Eve was given fake-news when the snake encouraged her to give Adam an apple. French anthropologist, Pascal Boyer claims that moral sentiments exist perfectly well in societies that have to a large extent abandoned religion, such as those in Czech Republic and Scandinavia. He says: “Moral intuitions don’t come out of moral codes. They develop very early in children, who understand that moral questions are about co-operation, and about constructing coalitions.”

The early Socratic philosophers, Socrates, Plato and Aristotle, also pontificated on morality, mainly linking happiness to virtue. In particular Plato thought morality was knowable, a priori, through reason. Later, Hume said morality comes from human sentiment, i.e. we view other people’s actions against the moral and ethical standards we ourselves have.

p.27

He said: “Take any action allow’d to be vicious: Willful murder, for instance. Examine it in all its lights, and see if you can find that matter of fact, or real existence, which you call vice… You can never find it, till you turn your reflection into your own breast, and find a sentiment of disapprobation, which arises in you, towards this action.” - from Treatise on Human Nature.

Immanuel Kant disagreed and argued morality derived from human reason. He referred to it as the “categorical Imperative”.

John Stuart Mill claimed that “…actions are right in the proportion as they tend to promote happiness, wrong as they tend to produce the reverse of happiness. By happiness is intended pleasure, and the absence of pain; by unhappiness, pain and the privation of pleasure.” Perhaps John Stuart had in mind what some of those ancient Greeks had said. Friedrich Nietzsche said, in effect, morality is a load of baloney.

So, typical with philosophers, after the ancient Greeks, we have different approaches to the issue of human morality. Some think it’s based on reason. Hume says it’s down to our own emotional reaction. Mill thinks moral actions promote happiness and Nietzsche dismisses morality. However, the ideas of all have one thing in common - God is absent from their argument.

The issue of what is considered to be moral has raised an even more intriguing question, generally known as the Euthyphro Dilemma, from Plato’s dialogue. In essence what this asks is whether something is moral because God wills it, or are there moral standards independent of God. If the former, can God decide something is moral that most humans would consider definitely not? Aquinas thinks we can have it both ways, that there is a set of basic moral laws that even God has to accept and cannot change, like the Biblical 10 commandments. In addition, Aquinas thinks there are some moral decisions over which God can decide, particularly when making judgements of individual human behaviour. Those philosophers that offer serious criticism say if there are moral standards outside of God’s influence then He is bound by some greater force, does not have free will and is not omnipotent.

The question of God as the foundation stone of morality has been debated right up to modern times. In 2003 Agony Aunt and author Anne Atkins said it’s impossible for people to hold to strong ethical standards if they are faithless. She made this comment on the BBC radio programme “Thought for the Day”: “Without God, where do we find the absolutes of right and wrong? What is to stop a secular society sinking to depths of depravity that, as yet, we only dream of.”

p.28

Professor Alice Roberts, President of Humanists Association, responded: “In answer to her first question, how do we know right from wrong - a humanist would perhaps reply that ethical behaviour is best guided not by recourse to religion, but by employing empathy, self-control, a moral sense, and reason…”

What else?

Apart from the COTHAM six proofs, or more accurately attempted proofs, there is one other worth noting: The Principle of Sufficient Reason (nihil est sine ratione), or PSR for short) - the +1.

This one is a real poser, as well as a POSR. It claims that nothing happens without a reason or cause. Spinoza explained his thinking for this philosophical principle: “Since existing is something positive, we cannot say that it has nothing as its cause. Therefore, we must assign some positive cause, or reason, why (a thing) exists - either an external one, i.e., one outside the thing itself, or an internal one, one comprehended in the nature and definition of the existing thing itself.” Leibnitz also dabbled in the same water. He made the point we cannot always know the reason or cause: “……most of the time these reasons cannot be known to us.” There is much debate over PSR, but it has been used as an argument for God. HE being the reason or cause of all that exists, it is claimed. How can we know this? We cannot, there might be other reasons, aka Leibnitz.

What about evil?

A common argument against the existence of God is to say how can He, an all knowing and all powerful and all loving entity allow evil to exist? The main counter argument used by theists is that God has a morally sufficient reason for allowing evil.

Timothy Keller in his book, ‘The Reason for God’, says that just because people don’t see a reason why God allows evil it does not mean there isn’t one. In answer to this point two questions can be asked:

  1. Is the traditional God omnipotent, i.e. all powerful? If so, He can make everything conform to his will. The answer all Christians and other Abrahamic religions give is an unqualified “YES”, God is super-super capable.

  2. Question 2, does this God want evil to exist? Answer, “NO”, otherwise there would not be all the Biblical incitements to be 'good", also we would not have the constant threat of hell for those falling into evil ways.

So, because evil exists, either God is not all powerful and cannot do anything about evil, because evil is a more powerful force than God, or He is capable of doing something about evil and suffering - often the two go hand-in-hand - but chooses not to do so. If the latter is the answer, this puts a hole in the purported kind and loving God - a few examples of this, mostly New Testament, version of God: the Jesus, who cured a leper, the Jesus who raised Lazarus from the dead, the Jesus who fed the 5,000 because they were a bit peckish and the Jesus who kept the party going by turning water into wine. A rather cynical analysis of the devout was made by Nobel Prize winning French poet, Anatole France: “Devout believers are safeguarded in a high degree against the risk of certain neurotic illnesses; their acceptance of the universal neurosis spares them the task of constructing a personal one.”

Along similar lines to Anatole a psychological term could also be applied to some of this Godly thinking: “The Argument from Ignorance”. This simply says it is a fallacy to say something is true because it has not yet been proven false.

In essence, there are holes in all of the claimed proofs and in the FIFU defence. Additionally, the problems of evil and freewill remain just that - a problem.
