Philosophy (mostly, with a bit of religion and science)

For all of my younger years and most of my adulthood my personal inclination was to avoid philosophy and philosophers like the plague. Had it not been that I needed to understand their thoughts for the sake of this book, I certainly wouldn’t have troubled to research them. But needs must, so in hope but not expectation I’ve done some paddling, albeit shallowly, in the turgid waters of the polemicists. For much of this process all those deep, philosophical musings from some of mankind’s greatest minds left me cold and unimpressed. It’s mostly impenetrable, without some effort and help to untangle, and seemingly self-serving too. Only in the latter stages of my reading about the great philosophers did I begin to realise a little more clearly what they were getting at. It was with this growing understanding that I began to appreciate and admire the amazing thoughts of most of these people.

But it’s worth setting out my early, highly critical, reactions of this whole genre because I suspect these are what many people feel about philosophers and philosophy. I had some fun in the process. Here are my initial jaundiced views.

Difficult to understand: Philosophers have a common approach to the way they present their thoughts - with a few exceptions. The more complicated and involved the better, they think. All lengthy philosophical writings are the opposite of the Occam’s razor approach, where the simplest explanation is normally the right one. You think I’m being too harsh? Then try out Heidegger: “Temporality temporalises itself fully in each ecstasy, i.e. in the ecstatic unity of the complete temporalising of temporality there is grounded the wholeness of the structural complex of existentiality, factuality and fallenness, which comprises the unity of the care-structure” What…?

Why didn’t these great brains apply themselves more productively: dig their garden; concoct a new, nutritious recipe for baked beans on toast; solve both energy and transport problems; come up with a sensible way to prevent Climate Change. They could most usefully even come up with the surest method to stamp out super-bugs, like Covid 19? Instead we have from them diatribes of diarrhoeic output.

p.244

Unwise and obvious: Let’s have a look back to the beginning, almost, of philosophy’s story. We start with Socrates, a giant figure in the early period of Western philosophy. Now he’s got a lot to answer for. In Athens, 399 BCE he was charged with mocking the Athenian Gods and of the moral corruption of the city’s youth. His sentence, following conviction, required him to swig hemlock. Socrates voluntarily swallowed this nasty poison. The Oracle of Delphi said that there’s no wiser man than Socrates. Doesn’t sound very wise to me; the nutter. He could have made a run for it. Some Oracle too.

Courtesy of Plato, Jean-Leon has a little joke by painting in Diogenes doggy friends, reminding viewers that Plato called Dio a dog. It was Plato who used it as a nickname for Diogenes. Nice one Dio. You should instead be known as the originator of daftism, but maybe I’m barking up the wrong tree and being cynical here. Anyway, the prize for the philosopher with the most unique home, goes to Dio. He takes the biscuit.

Marcus Aurelius, a Roman Emperor no less, did a bit of philosophising in his spare time. He favoured stoicism; a belief that people should accept their lot in life. Marky, I may be overly suspicious here, but it strikes me that the widespread application of this philosophy would have been very convenient to you as Emperor. If you could convince those revolting Huns on your eastern borders to become stoics, then they wouldn’t be quite so revolting.

There’s Rene Descartes famous line: “I think therefore I am.”, or if you want to be a little superior: “Cogito ergo sum” (Latin, the language of the intelligentsia in Rene’s time), “Je pense donc je suis” (in Descartes’ native tongue). Rene, old chap, does this mean you didn’t exist when you were asleep? Dreams are not conscious thought. Another thing Rene, you might have “thought”, or is it “lived”, again had you seen The Matrix film (with help from Dr Who’s Telephone Box). Then again, who, exactly is the “I” Rene? Karl Marx gave us Dialectical Materialism, for all the use that’s been to anyone, particularly to communists of the old USSR. Hume pronounced on scepticism, but he need not have bothered - Richard Wilson gave a much more practical and entertaining demonstration of this ideology with his catch-phrase: “I don’t believe it.”, in the BBC TV series “One Foot in the Grave” (sorry David, I do in fact have a soft spot for your pragmatic way of thinking).

Kierkegaard gloried as the founder of existentialism - such contrivance. You can live a very full and happy life in complete and blissful ignorance of this slightly tortured concept.

We are told Nietzsche was both a nihilist and an existentialist. Does this mean he was a striker, with an unpronounceable name, who couldn’t score goals for Germany, even in extra time? Kant; well his musings were exactly that, a lot of cant: “Ideas do not conform to the external world, but rather the world can be known only insofar as it conforms to the mind’s own structure”. Not nearly as bad as Heidegger, but edging in that direction.

Egotistical certainty: Of course, these great thinkers were individualists. Most of them admired, or at least were extremely conscious of the weight of their own intellects, which were indeed supremely powerful in relation to those members of the common herd, like me. This meant they tended to say to themselves “I’ve thought this through with my immense mind; therefore, my conclusions must be superior to everybody else’s.” With such a scenario, you might think it hard to find any strands of commonality among philosophers to help enlighten on the G issue but, there are a couple of shared threads that have some relevance. For example, many philosophers exhibit an empirical approach that focuses on their own experience and direct knowledge. “If I haven’t experienced it, or am unable to conceptualise it directly from my life experiences, then it can’t exist.” This is the starting point for many (by no means all) of the philosophies that the great thinkers propound. It is the antithesis of Faith which is such a powerful factor in the God debate for those with a religious inclination.

Isimitis: This is a disease any serious philosopher must contract, if he/she wants to be included in the philosophers’ Premiership division. They must come out, ideally all over, in an ism. There’s Diogenes Cynicism, Plato’s Platonism, and Satre’s Existentialism, to name but a few.

Common views of philosophy and philosophers: Ordinary people (now there’s a statement that could launch a whole flotilla of argumentative philosophy books) may find Ps irritating or baffling. Others (probably quite a lot of them) possibly take a more straightforward and dim view of philosophy and philosophers. They dismiss both as far removed from the real world. Philosophers’ prognostications are seen to have no bearing or help with how people live their lives. These sceptics may see Ps philosophising in terms such as:

  • much ado about nothing;
  • hot air and even more hot air;
  • a scientific understanding of everything, without the science and without the understanding;
  • when Homo Sapiens becomes Homo Sap;
  • the common man has common sense, while the philosopher has nonsense;
  • definition of a philosopher; “a being who is trying to be superior but is superior at being trying.”

Now I really, must stop indulging my initial prejudices. It’s time to draw a line under these silly criticisms. There’s no shying away from it. If we are trying to determine whether or not God exists, which is the point of this book, it’s necessary to look at what some of mankind’s greatest brain boxes, the great philosophers, have thought. They did not necessarily ponder directly, or exclusively, the key question, “does G exist”. But, the breadth and depth of reasoning on the what, why and wherefore of mankind is fundamental background to the G question, whether or not they mention His name at all in their most renowned treatise.

The three Routes to Knowledge and Wisdom

p.247

Philosophy: This is a route down which we can travel to learn and understand more about life, the world and everything. Through study of philosophy and by following the rigour of philosophical thinking knowledge and wisdom can be gained apparently, although not necessarily correctly. But, arguably it’s only one of three routes to knowledge and wisdom, the other two being religion and science.

Religion in brief

Some people claim that only one of these three routes will lead us to true knowledge. On the other hand, there are sceptics who say we are not capable of knowing true reality; regardless of which route is taken. We cannot be sure of anything at all these doubters assert. We will investigate the reasoning behind such views later, but not now. In this chapter of the book, Hello, Hello, the focus is on philosophy but in other areas of the book religion and science get a much fuller examination. Before focusing on philosophy let’s have just a brief look at religion and science and their claims to be, if not the fount of all knowledge, at least the most secure base for true knowledge. Then we will return to philosophy.

Religion: In 1871, British anthropologist Sir Edward Burnett Tylor, defined the essential element of all religion quite simply as: “the belief in spiritual beings.” He thought animism was the earliest example of religion. Later Tylor’s elegant definition was expanded to recognise that religions commonly have a set of beliefs, rites and rituals specific to the particular religion. These usually fall under the umbrella of a church, which has a structure of expert practitioners who take the lead with the observance of the rites and rituals. They are also authoritative figures who can give answers to theological questions.

Many religions refer to a supernatural entity and a personal God. They typically claim there is a form of afterlife or alternate existence after death. Their beliefs are usually based on a particular holy book. The Abrahamic ones and some others tell of a supernatural basis for creation.

What’s the origin of religion’s claim to be the fount of all knowledge? Humans have always had religion of one sort or another, even back to pre-historic times. We know from the evidence of more than 30,000 years ago forms of spiritualism, usually shamanism or animism, were practiced by our hunter-gatherer forbearers.

p.248

These early forms of religion, closely identified with the natural and imagined spiritual worlds, had probably been in existence since the dawn of modern humans, around 300,000 years ago.

About 10,000 years ago agricultural societies began to replace hunter-gathering humans. Humans gathered in larger groups and with the more assured food supply that agriculture offered had more time on their hands. They were able to use this spare time to develop more complex religious beliefs. Since the dawn of agriculture religion has grown into a mighty and diverse industry, now dominated by the monotheism of the Abrahamic trio: Christianity, Islam and Judaism. But it also includes many others, predominately far-eastern ones, like Hinduism, Sikhism, Shinto and those that blur the line between religion and philosophy such as Buddhism, Taoism and Confucianism.

These institutionalised religions have in common their certainty of a type of reality. They claim to know how things have worked and will work out, in metaphysical terms, and they tell us of absolute truths, particularly about the consequences of different types of human behaviour and the relevance of good and evil. These religions are largely a form of revelatory knowledge, via various holy texts. It is a matter of faith that the content of the texts was revealed to the author(s) by some great power. The texts give all the necessary answers to how humans should live their lives and in what way the particular deity must be revered. The sacred texts must not be questioned. Believers are required to have faith in place of proof.

The Bible is stuffed full of examples of revelatory truth, one of very many, appropriately towards the beginning of the book, is the story of man’s origin. In Genesis, chapter 2, God says: “And the LORD God formed man of the dust of the ground, & breathed into his nostrils the breath of life; and man became a living soul” (https://www.kingjamesbibleonline.org/1611_Genesis-2-7))

We now know, thanks to Charles Darwin and Wallace, that man was the product of evolution. He was not formed out of dust, unless the Biblical author was thinking of stellar dust. Some believers think evolution happened too fast to be natural. It must have been speeded up by God they say. Other believers claim that Genesis is only intended to give a general indication, both of creation and man’s original defiance of God. It’s not meant to be taken literally.

p.249

There have also been several attempted logical proofs of the monotheistic God’s existence, but to date no one has yet managed this feat. These are described in detail elsewhere in this book. Nor has anyone shown conclusively that re-incarnation happens or that the state of nirvana can be achieved, as claimed by Buddhism. So perhaps religious revelatory truth is not truth at all. It may either be allegorical, just unsupported opinion, or even simple story telling.

However, many millions believe their brand of religion gives some knowledge about big issues, like the existence of a supreme being, the origin of the universe, man’s place in it and what is expected of him. The use of the term “man” is sometimes meant to be generic and include women, although in principle religions women may be a little ignored or treated as less important than men.

Religion therefore is a form of true knowledge for many people. Some believe so fervently that anyone with alternate beliefs can be subject to physical disapproval. The strength and intolerance of religious belief has led to much violent conflict. Charles de Montesquieu, a judge and political philosopher, thought this was the result of narrow-minded bigotry.

“Religious wars are not caused by the fact that there is more than one religion, but by the spirit of intolerance… the spread of which can only be regarded as the total eclipse of human reason.”

Charles de Montesquieu 1689 to 1755

Religion gets a much fuller airing in several other chapters of this book.

Science in brief

Science: Finally, we have science as the third source of knowledge. There are some similarities with philosophy. Both use rigorous argument in support of a particular conclusion. However, science attempts to offer proof though experimental evidence of a key factor. It is absent from the other two disciplines. Science tends to take a step-by-step approach along the route to true knowledge. Often the apparent truth of a previous step is overturned, amended or added to by a later step. It tries to prove its case through reason based on observation, experiment and testing. Statistics and mathematical equations can play a big part in the scientific approach, as can the basic laws of physics and chemistry.

In 2011, at a Herefordshire conference, Professor Stephen Hawking claimed that science was in the lead in the knowledge race: “Scientists have become the bearers of the torch of discovery in our quest for knowledge.”

p.250

Many scientists express doubt when the question of God and religious beliefs is asked: “If we are honest - and scientists have to be - we must admit that religion is a jumble of false assertions, with no basis in reality.” - said Paul Dirac, the great Quantum physicist, of Bristol and Cambridge fame. See more on science in Chapter 24.

Comparative Features of the three roads to knowledge

There are other features to note about the three roads to wisdom. Proponents of one or the other will sometimes champion their knowledge source as the only correct road to follow. Religion does stand out in contrast with the other two here. It is only religious zealots who can sometimes kill, maim or imprison if their religion’s version of the truth is not adhered to. Philosophers and scientists are not known to murder in defence of their ideas. They are more likely to dispute verbally or in writing, occasionally very forcefully. Pythagoras was a one-off here. He did break the philosophers duck by drowning a student for giving the game away on irrational numbers. And there is the milder incident where Wittgenstein is alleged to have waved a poker at Karl Popper. These occurrences though fail to get anywhere near religions propensity for murder on a mass scale.

Togetherness: Philosophy, religion and science do not always stand entirely alone, sometimes one route can blur into another. Philosophy is the only one of the three schools that can have strong links to the other two. Religion has been, especially in the past, anti-science to an extent and likewise science can be dismissive of religion. Some philosophers use philosophy to support religion; others see it as a separate, truer basis for knowledge than what they consider to be superstitious religious beliefs. Voltaire dramatically put it: “Superstition sets the whole world in flames; philosophy quenches them.”

Another connection - church approval or disapproval: Philosophy is sometimes closely linked to religion because theologians may identify a philosopher’s ideas as giving support to their religious beliefs. The two can also be connected for the opposite reason; the church strongly disapproves of a philosopher, and his philosophy.

Having one’s philosophy strongly approved by the dominant church allows a philosopher to get his name and ideas mentioned publicly. Aristotle was much admired by church theologians, mostly up to Galileo (1564-1642), but a bit beyond him too. Aristotle’s mistaken view that the Earth was the centre of the universe fitted in nicely with Roman Catholic beliefs for about 2,000 years.

Other philosophers may also get publicity, for the opposite reason. They may be strongly disapproved of by an established religion. Galileo is an example. He daringly went against Catholic certainty by saying the Earth was not the centre of the universe. It was only in 1992, 350 years after Galileo, that Pope John Paul II finally admitted the Catholic Church’s condemnation of him had been in error. A couple of hundred years after Galileo, Church creationists were not enamoured of Darwin’s evolutionary ideas. For many of them, the Biblical Book of Genesis was believed to be literally true. Spinoza got a huge amount of unintended free advertising. He was excommunicated and declared a non-Jew in 17th century Amsterdam. In addition, his works were banned by the Catholic church. Upsetting two churches did wonders in getting his name and philosophy bandied about. To this day, he is still to the fore because several efforts to have his excommunication rescinded have all failed. Jewish historian, Joseph Klausner tried unsuccessfully in 1927. Other attempts included one in 2012, put before a committee of four, which included the American philosopher Steven Nadler. This resulted in yet another refusal.

Believers and unbelievers comparative intelligence: There have been a number of surveys into the issue of whether there is an inverse relationship between the strength of religious belief and intelligence. In his 2006 book: The God Delusion, Prof. Richard Dawkins said: “Of 43 studies carried out since 1927 on the relationship between religious belief and one’s intelligence and/or educational level, all but four found an inverse connection. That is, the higher one’s intelligence or education level, the less one is likely to be religious or hold ‘beliefs’ of any kind.”

A 2005 Gallop international study surveyed 50,000 people in over 65 countries. It discovered that people with a higher level of education are less religious than those with either a basic education or no education. Another somewhat similar outcome came from a later study, in 2013. This was by Edward Dutton and Dimitri van der Linden, for Rotterdam University. They concluded that the results showed that on average religious people are less intelligent than atheists. It suggested the reason for this is that religion can be considered instinctive while intelligence is the ability to rise above this instinctive drive. All this is definitely a little unfair on religious people. There are innumerable highly intelligent people, past and present who have had some form of religious belief.

PHILOSOPHY - definitions, usage and origins

We are now going back to focus on philosophy.

Meaning: What exactly is philosophy? There are a lot of definitions, just try any dictionary or a Google search and you will see what I mean.

The Oxford Dictionary gives one definition: “The study of the fundamental nature of knowledge, reality, and existence, especially when considered as an academic discipline.” Not bad, but philosophy covers more than this, as will be explained. The great Plato had a more fundamental description: “Wonder is very much the affection of a philosopher; for there is no other beginning of philosophy than this.” Yes, it’s possible to wonder about anything. That’s at the base of all philosophy.

Plato’s brilliantly simple one-word description - wonder - needs to be fleshed out a bit more. It was the ancient Greeks who got philosophy well and truly on the road so it’s natural to look to them and their meaning of the term. Not surprisingly they were the first to formally coin the particular words, philo (love) and sophia (wisdom). When joined together these became philosophia meaning the love of wisdom. The term later gained a second and wider meaning; the logical exploration of ideas, virtually on any topic.

p.253

Their original definition - love of wisdom - points to the motivation of these Greek philosophers. They wanted to understand and gain knowledge, not just ponder the nature of knowledge.

The later meaning - the logical exploration of ideas - described more specifically what they did and how they did it. A meld of these two meanings is what philosophy is all about in a nutshell. But, given the extent and range of philosophical coverage over more than two and a half thousand years since the Greek originators, that shell comes from one mighty big nut! Philosophy is renowned for its argumentative and contentious nature, so there can be a lot of dispute about this statement.

Is there any point to philosophy?

A fair question. As touched on earlier, you can find lots of people sceptical or even contemptuous of philosophy. They don’t see that it has any relevance to their lives. Philosophy can be a complex subject, with its own quite difficult language and sometimes pedantic, tortuous reasoning. It can go into minute and seemingly pointless detail. Bertrand Russell puts it like this:

What is the value of philosophy and why ought it to be studied? It is the more necessary to consider this question, in view of the fact that many men, under the influence of science or practical affairs, are inclined to think philosophy is anything better than innocent but useless trifling, hair-splitting distinction, and controversies on matters concerning which knowledge is impossible… if the study of philosophy has any value at all…it must be only indirectly, through its effect on the lives of those who study it, it is in these effects therefore… that the value of philosophy can be sought.

Well, all interesting stuff Bertrand but surely philosophy has more of an impact than simply on the lives of those who study it?

Here’s another comment from Bertrand, in its favour, although he may still be thinking only about those who specialise in philosophy:

The point of philosophy is to start with something so simple as not to seem worth stating, and to end with something so paradoxical that no one will believe it.

(Bertrand Russell)

What’s it all about?

p.254

This was a question asked of Alfie by Cilla Black in her hit song of the same title. She went on: “Is it just for the moment we live?” Cilla might have been prompted to ask this question having read Jostein Gaarder’s brilliant book of philosophical history and guidance, “Sophie’s World”. Cilla’s were philosophical questions touching on ethics and metaphysics. Her’s is a simple example that philosophy can be part of normal life. It’s a road down which we can travel to learn and understand more about the human condition, the world and everything.

Philosophy is for all of us, not just for Alfie, because it:

  • has been transformational in human history;
  • has been the turning point from solely faith based religious explanations to a more reasoned approach;
  • helped found the scientific method;
  • anticipated scientific findings to some extent.

Great expertise is not a necessity. We don’t need to be capable of proceeding from the simple to the paradoxical, as Bertrand suggests. By just a little study of philosophy we can understand the necessity of questioning through critical and logical thinking. In the process, we learn to doubt and be suspicious of any claim of certainty. Freer thinking enables us to step away from what can be the stultifying influence of culture, place and inheritance.

None of us are likely to approach the skill of Socrates in doing this sort of analytical dissection, but we can try. These philosophical techniques can be useful to us in any sphere of human activity. By their application we can gain both knowledge and wisdom. This does not mean we can magically reach final and certain truths through philosophy. It’s just a way of inching in that direction. Often, the process reveals more questions than it does answers. If you want certainty, go for religion, not philosophy.

So, philosophy can help us think more effectively. It also has a use in aiding us to broaden and deepen our thinking in important areas of human activity and thought. This it does through the main branches it delves into. It presents many ideas that give us much to ponder. Here are philosophy’s main topic areas (there are others) in summary:

p.255
  • Epistemology - (the study of knowledge). Epistemology is relevant to every branch of philosophy;
  • Metaphysics - the origin and nature of existence;
  • Political philosophy - the argument for various political and justice systems;
  • Ethics - how we should act towards others and ourselves;
  • Religious philosophy - the use of philosophical reasoning by theologians to support certain religious beliefs;
  • Aesthetics - the relevance and importance of art, beauty, good taste and nature. Aesthetics and ethics are sub-divisions of what is known as axiology.

And in more detail:

Epistemology - We have to go to those ancient Greeks again for the base meaning of this term. It comes from “episteme,” meaning knowledge and “logos” which can mean to study. OK, so epistemology is the study of knowledge. Why study it? Everyone knows what knowledge is. It’s stuff you learn and understand, so what’s all the fuss about, why do we need to philosophise about it? You might reasonably surmise this. But we are talking about philosophy which requires a disciplined, critical and logical thinking approach to any topic. Epistemology, or more plainly knowledge, has been subjected to this rigorous analysis by philosophers. The result is that knowledge is a bit more complicated than one might think.

Under the broad heading that is epistemology, philosophers have first tried to determine the nature of knowledge; that is, what does it mean to say that someone knows, or fails to know, something? This is a matter of understanding what knowledge is, and how to tell the difference between instances in which someone knows something, or thinks they know something, and when someone does not know something. While there is some general agreement about aspects of this issue, we shall see that this question is much more involved than one might first imagine.

Second, they’ve considered the extent of human knowledge; that is, how much do we, or can we, know? How do we use our brains, consciousness and senses - especially sight and sound? In addition, how do we use the information provided by other people to acquire knowledge? Are there limits to what we can know? And the really big one, can we know anything at all?

One of the all-time greats, Socrates seemed to offer support to this latter view. He said: “I know that I know nothing”. Perhaps his exceptional dialectical analysis of just about anything led him to this comment. It’s a paradoxical claim of course, if Socs. knew that he knew nothing then that means he knows something!

p.256

Socrates pared away so finely at every apparent certainty, maybe he reached a level of doubt to an extent he could not be sure he knew anything. Or maybe his was a teasing remark, claiming false modesty. It’s perfectly possible, after all Socrates was known to have a sense of humour. He was also well aware that Athenians had high regard for his supreme intelligence.

Aristotle, in one of his many comments about knowing and knowledge said this: “What we know is not capable of being otherwise; of things capable of being otherwise we do not know, when they have passed outside our observation, whether they exist or not. Therefore, the object of knowledge is of necessity.” and, “To say of something which is that it is not, or to say of something which is not that it is, is false. However, to say of something which is that it is, or of something which is not that it is not, is true.” You have to read this a second time, quite slowly, to appreciate what he’s saying. But you may now have reached a stage where you want to close this book and watch some TV. We are starting to get into the area of complexity and nit-picking that makes many turn away from philosophy. However, both Socrates and Aristotle are worthy of noting. They have indicated that the extent of human knowledge is an important issue, so let’s carry on. So, back to the first point, ie., what is knowledge? In basic terms knowledge is an understanding of reality. Information of this reality is gained through a process of reason and experience or a combination of the two. For some religious claims of knowledge, it’s neither by reason or experience, it’s revelatory and faith based.

Traditionally, the type of knowledge we have been concerned with is known as propositional knowledge. This is the knowledge about the result or end point rather than knowledge of how to do something. In arithmetic, we know that 3+4=7. Knowing the answer, 7, is propositional knowledge. The knowledge of the addition process that is needed to arrive at the answer is not propositional. Epistemologists generally concentrate on what they consider to be propositional knowledge. When examining more deeply propositional knowledge, philosophers have come up with two types:

p.257
  • a priori - this is where knowledge is gained through reason and logic, not that based on experience. A priori knowledge comes before we have the experience. Those philosophers who emphasise the essential nature of this form of knowledge are known as rationalists;
  • a posteriori - is the opposite of above. It is knowledge gained through experience. It involves use of our senses to gain factual evidence of our world. Philosophers who emphasise the essential nature of this form of knowledge are known as empiricists.

To take these further epistemologists, generally, have identified three related conditions necessary to claim knowledge, whether it’s a priori or a posteriori knowledge. These conditions must all line up for someone to know something as “knowledge”:

truth; justification; belief.

It was Plato who originally came up with this argument. Basically, it means that something can only be known as knowledge if it is a belief that is based on absolute grounds as being true and this belief must be justified, i.e. subject to rigorous proof. Anything that failed this test is an opinion, not knowledge. This means that some beliefs or claims of knowledge can only be labelled as opinions. Edmund Gettier has tried to challenge this, but his views are not wholly accepted. Plato’s pupil, Aristotle took a slightly different approach. He said that to have correct knowledge of something we must know its cause. In fact he said there were four causes that we need to consider:

  • material cause: “that out of which something arises”, e.g., the paint of a painting;
  • formal cause: e.g. the form, or shape of the painting;
  • efficient cause: primary source of the change, e.g., the artist, the paint brushes, easel, the art tutor or advisor;
  • final cause: “the end, that for the sake of which a thing is done”, e.g., being clean is the end of a bath.

Since Plato and Aristotle, more than 2,300 years ago, there has been much discussion among philosophers about this question. The main issue examined is the extent of evidence needed to claim justified truth and hence knowledge.

One issue under debate, in the broad context of epistemology, has been causation; simply meaning what is the process that results in some or other effect. In the frame are horizontal and vertical causation.

Horizontal causation is a two-stage process, from a cause leading to an effect, or outcome. Vertical causation generally adds a third, preceding stage. It examines what is the underlying cause of the cause, the metacause. So, as an example of horizontal causation, rain - the cause, leads to wet ground - the effect; all in just two stages. One example of vertical causation is, a weather system - the metacause, leads to rain - the cause and finally wet ground - the effect; more than two stages. Horizontal and vertical causation is an issue in the philosophy of science.

Metaphysics:

This term comes, yet again, from the ancient Greeks. It simply means beyond or after physics. Specifically, it deals with questions about the nature of reality, e.g. what is existence; what is the cause of existence, sometimes referred to as “being” and “first cause”. But since the ancient Greek philosophers, like the pre-Socratics, the scope of metaphysics has widened. It now includes issues of space and time, e.g. is there such a thing as space, and if so does it have infinite size; does time have a beginning and an end?

Other disciplines also attempt to address questions of reality and existence, namely religion and science. Science, as indicated, takes an approach that relies on propositions that can be proven by rigorous experiment and observation, although science has inched towards greater understanding of the answers to metaphysical questions, it has not yet come up with a complete solution. The Great Theory of Everything remains an elusive goal.

As for religion, there is no end of explanations for the origin of existence. The vast range of religious beliefs give different specific answers but have two common, underlying themes. First, nearly all claim the entirety of existence is, down to an eternal God, or Gods. Second, none can offer unchallengeable proof.

Early Greek pre-Socratic philosophers more than 2,400 years ago first considered metaphysical problems, pondered the origin of all matter, and gave their reasoning for various answers.

Many philosophers have made clear their thoughts on ethical issues over the centuries since Socrates’ time. They’ve analysed ethics in so many ways. The result is that ethics is split up into several categories. We have:

p.261

  • Metaethics, this looks at how humans arrive at moral decisions and considers the origin of morality, i.e. can it exist independently of humans? This is an area that interest’s theologians. They want to know whether God is the origin of moral standards. One aspect of metaethics is known as Moral or Ethical Rationalism. This claims that general moral principles can be known through reason. Plato and Immanuel Kant were of this view. Later, the idea was rejected, partly, by David Hume and Friedrich Nietzsche. In more recent times some philosophers have backed the a priori concept purported by Plato. This form of ethics, whether called metaethics, moral or ethical rationalism, is more of an investigative, pondering approach. It does not immediately propose necessary action.

  • Normative ethics, this is a more practical approach than metaethics. It considers what moral standards or rules we should adopt and how these basic standards should be arrived at. Arguably (anything to do with philosophy is always arguable) the above are the main categories under which ethics is considered. But there is more terminology that is sometimes used to group together theories of ethics. For instance, consequentialism - this simply means that the morally correct action is the one that produces the most good. Bentham’s utilitarianism is a form of consequentialism. Then we have deontology. Deontology is nothing to do with teeth, although it does have some bite when it comes to opposing consequentialism. Proponents of deontological ethics say that it’s the action itself that must be morally right, not just the outcome. Kant can be seen as a proponent of this concept.

Aesthetics: This is the second strand of axiology, ethics is the first. Aesthetics is also known as the theory of beauty. It explores culture, nature and art generally.

The First Philosophers

It was Pythagoras, 570 BC - 495 BC, who expounded the term philosophia to refer to great thinkers and questioners as philosophers. Of course, Pythagoras is better known to generations of school children for his mathematical skills associated with right-angled triangles. Pythagoras was not the first Greek philosopher, just the first to call himself by that name.

About 2,600 years ago, in the freewheeling Greek coastal town of Miletus (now in Turkey), a man called Thales had new ideas about the nature of all reality. He dismissed the traditional religious views. These claimed everything came from the Gods; the Greek Gods. For Thales, the often cruel, selfish and plain silly antics of Greek anthropomorphic Gods made them unbelievable.

Being a mathematician, he applied rigorous thought to what constituted original matter. According to Thales there must be some basic substance that was the first matter ever to exist. He concluded that everything in nature had water, the Arche, as its base material. His reasoning was that water could be either a liquid, solid or vapour (water, ice or steam). Cicero said that Thales believed it was the mind of a supreme being that created all things from water, if this is so he clearly assumed there was some sort of God.

There are two more important things to note about Thales, his freedom and his school. He lived in a society where it was relatively safe, but not wholly so, to articulate non-conformist ideas. At this time Greece had a democratic government and people were allowed greater freedom of speech, especially if they lived some distance from Athens, as Thales did in Miletus. This freedom was quite unusual compared to many other ancient cultures of his time (although maybe not middle-eastern Akkad where the 2,500-year-old clay Cyrus cylinder suggests religious freedom was allowed).

Thales was not exiled, imprisoned or executed for his anti-religious opinions, as he might have been elsewhere than Greece. He founded the first school of philosophy, later called The Milesian School.

Following on from Thales were his pupil Anaximander and then Anaximenes. These two continued wrestling with the metaphysical problem of what was the base substance from which all matter was derived. These three Milesians did not get it right, but their ideas and freethinking example inspired many others after them.

It’s certain that the Milesian school trio got Western philosophy well and truly off the ground. Bertrand Russell says western philosophy began with Thales, but were these the original philosophers on planet Earth? No, they were not. Ptah Hotep, an Egyptian Grand Vizier from some 1,800 years before Thales, deserves that honour. He was the first philosopher that we have evidence of. Ptah wrote ethical philosophical statements. He did not say much though; this may have been because of the limitations posed by needing to have his thoughts chiselled on stone. And, as far as we know, he did not trigger a philosophical movement. Certainly, there is no sure record, on stone or papyrus, of other ancient Egyptian philosophers, although there is a very limited claim made of Imhotep.

But Egypt had an older culture than Greece. We know Thales received some instruction from an Egyptian priest and that he used geometry to measure the height of the pyramids. It’s likely therefore he was fully aware of Egyptian mythology. Perhaps he saw the Egyptian Gods in the same dismissive light as the Greek ones and so may have concluded that the Egyptians, for all their sophisticated culture, did not have the answer to the source of reality. He had to work it out for himself.

Other than Ptah Hotep, with his ethical philosophy, there is no one else known to have preceded Thales. In the far east the Chinese Laozi, founder of Taoism, weighed in just a few years after Thales. In India Siddhartha Gautama (Buddha) and back in China, Confucius came slightly later still. However, we are talking about who was the first philosopher in recorded history. By definition, we cannot know of those sages from pre-recorded, i.e. pre-historic times. There must have been some, like the fictional story of Noth the stone-age theologian/philosopher recounted in Chapter 5. Descartes, Rene

1596–1650

Rene Descartes was born in France and is called the “father of modern philosophy.” His breakthrough discovery in philosophy came in the year 1619. While serving as a mercenary soldier, Descartes spent a cold November night in Germany lying in an oven (which back then meant a small heated room). On that night, he experienced three vivid dreams which changed the course of his life. These dreams inspired him to dedicate his life to philosophy.

[IMAGE: Portrait of Rene Descartes] 55.

Descartes was impressed by the progress being made by the sciences, especially mathematics. He wanted philosophy to achieve the same sort of certainty that mathematics possessed. In order to achieve this goal, Descartes decided he needed to rebuild all philosophy on a firm foundation. To do this, he had to cast away anything that could possibly be doubted.

So Descartes used what is now called “Descartes’s method of doubt,” which is to doubt everything that can be doubted. Descartes pointed out that our senses can deceive us, so anything we believe based on sense experience can be doubted. He even realized that we cannot distinguish between dreams and reality, so the entire external world can be doubted. Descartes then asked: Is there anything that cannot be doubted?

p.327

After stripping away everything that could be doubted, Descartes was left with one certain truth: “I think, therefore I am” (in Latin: Cogito, ergo sum). Even if an evil demon were deceiving him about everything, Descartes reasoned, he would still have to exist in order to be deceived. The very act of thinking proves one’s own existence. This became the foundation of Descartes’s philosophy.

From this foundation, Descartes tried to rebuild knowledge, proving first that God exists, then that the external world exists. His “proof” for God’s existence is controversial and unconvincing to many. Nonetheless, Descartes’s method of systematic doubt was revolutionary and his Cogito argument remains one of the most famous in all philosophy.

Descartes was also a dualist. He believed that humans are made of two fundamentally different substances: mind (thinking substance) and body (physical substance). The mind and body interact with each other, though how they do this is mysterious and problematic. This view became known as “Cartesian dualism.” The mind-body problem continues to puzzle philosophers and scientists today.

Pascal, Blaise

Blaise Pascal was a French mathematician, physicist, and religious philosopher. He made significant contributions to mathematics and science before dedicating the last years of his short life to philosophy and theology. Pascal is famous for “Pascal’s Wager,” an argument for believing in God.

[IMAGE: Portrait of Blaise Pascal] 56.

Pascal’s Wager goes like this: Either God exists or God doesn’t exist. If you believe in God and God exists, you gain eternal happiness in heaven. If you believe in God but God doesn’t exist, you lose little (perhaps some time spent at church). On the other hand, if you don’t believe in God and God does exist, you face eternal damnation. If you don’t believe in God and God doesn’t exist, you gain little. Therefore, it makes sense to believe in God—you have everything to gain and little to lose.

Many philosophers have criticized Pascal’s Wager. Some point out that you cannot simply choose to believe something—belief is not voluntary. Others note that the argument assumes God rewards belief regardless of sincerity, which seems questionable. Still others argue that Pascal’s Wager could apply to any religion’s god, not just the Christian God. Despite these criticisms, Pascal’s Wager remains an interesting and influential argument.

p.329

Pascal also emphasized that “the heart has its reasons of which reason knows nothing.” He believed that faith and religious experience go beyond what reason alone can grasp. This reflects Pascal’s view that humans have both a rational and an intuitive side, and both are important.

Spinoza, Baruch

1632–1677

Baruch (or Benedict) Spinoza was born into a Jewish family in Amsterdam. His radical philosophical ideas led to his excommunication from the Jewish community at age 23. Spinoza earned his living grinding lenses for microscopes and telescopes while developing one of the most systematic and radical philosophies of his era.

[IMAGE: Portrait of Baruch Spinoza] 57.

Spinoza was a rationalist who believed that everything in the universe follows from logical necessity, like a geometric proof. He argued that there is only one substance in the universe, which he called “God or Nature” (Latin: Deus sive Natura). This made Spinoza a pantheist—someone who believes God and nature are identical.

According to Spinoza, everything that exists is part of this one substance. What we think of as individual things (including ourselves) are really just modes or modifications of the one substance. This means that everything is connected and that free will is an illusion. We think we have free will only because we are ignorant of the causes that determine our actions.

p.330

Spinoza believed that understanding the true nature of reality leads to freedom and happiness. By understanding that everything happens by necessity, we can accept things as they are rather than being upset by events beyond our control. This leads to what Spinoza called “the intellectual love of God”—a serene acceptance of reality.

Spinoza’s philosophy was considered highly controversial and dangerous in his time. His books were banned and he was widely attacked. However, his influence grew over time, and today he is recognized as one of the great rationalist philosophers of the early modern period.

Locke, John

1632–1704

John Locke was born in England and is considered the founder of British empiricism and classical liberalism. His ideas profoundly influenced the Enlightenment and the development of democratic government, especially in America. The American Declaration of Independence reflects many of Locke’s political ideas.

p.331

[IMAGE: Portrait of John Locke] 58.

In epistemology (theory of knowledge), Locke argued against the rationalist idea that we are born with innate ideas. Instead, Locke claimed that the mind at birth is a “blank slate” (Latin: tabula rasa). All our knowledge comes from experience—either from sensation (external experience of the world) or reflection (internal experience of our own mental operations).

Locke distinguished between primary qualities and secondary qualities of objects. Primary qualities (like shape, motion, solidity) actually exist in objects themselves. Secondary qualities (like color, taste, smell) are not in the objects themselves but are the way objects appear to our senses. For example, an apple is not really red—redness is how the apple’s surface affects our eyes.

In political philosophy, Locke argued that all people have natural rights to “life, liberty, and property.” Unlike Hobbes, Locke believed that even in the state of nature, people have rights that cannot be taken away. People enter into a social contract to better protect these rights, not to give them up. Government exists to serve the people, and if a government fails to protect people’s rights, the people have the right to overthrow it.

Locke’s political philosophy was revolutionary because it challenged the divine right of kings and placed sovereignty in the hands of the people. His ideas provided philosophical justification for liberal democracy and constitutional government.

Leibniz, Gottfried Wilhelm

1646–1716

Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz was a German philosopher and mathematician who made contributions to many fields. He independently invented calculus at the same time as Isaac Newton (which led to a bitter dispute over priority). In philosophy, Leibniz was a rationalist who believed that the universe is fundamentally logical and that everything happens for a reason.

[IMAGE: Portrait of Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz] 59.

Leibniz believed that the universe is made up of infinitely many simple substances called “monads.” Each monad is like a tiny mind or soul that perceives the universe from its own unique perspective. Monads have no physical extension and cannot interact with each other directly. Instead, God created them in a “pre-established harmony” so that they all develop in coordination, like synchronized clocks.

This may sound strange, but it was Leibniz’s solution to the mind-body problem raised by Descartes. If mind and body are fundamentally different, how can they interact? Leibniz’s answer: they don’t interact; they just run in parallel according to God’s pre-programmed harmony.

Leibniz also argued that we live in “the best of all possible worlds.” God, being infinitely good and powerful, would have created the best world possible. This doesn’t mean our world is perfect, but that it’s the best that could exist given certain logical constraints. This view was famously satirized by Voltaire in his novel Candide, where optimistic characters maintain that “all is for the best” even as terrible things happen to them.

Despite being mocked by Voltaire, Leibniz made lasting contributions to philosophy, particularly in logic and metaphysics. His work on possible worlds and necessary truths influenced later philosophy, especially modal logic.

Berkeley, George

1685–1753

George Berkeley was an Irish philosopher and Anglican bishop. He is known for defending a radical form of idealism—the view that reality consists only of minds and their ideas. Berkeley’s famous motto was “esse est percipi”—“to be is to be perceived.”

[IMAGE: Portrait of George Berkeley] 60.

Berkeley argued that material objects do not exist independently of perception. When we say that a table exists, we mean that it is perceived by someone (or could be perceived). When no human is perceiving the table, it continues to exist because God perceives it. In Berkeley’s philosophy, God’s constant perception of everything keeps the world in existence.

This seems like a bizarre view, but Berkeley had reasons for it. He wanted to defend against skepticism and atheism. If objects exist independently of minds, how can we ever be sure our perceptions match reality? By denying that material objects exist separately from perception, Berkeley thought he could avoid skepticism. And by making God necessary for objects to exist when unperceived, he thought he had proven God’s existence.

Critics pointed out an obvious problem: Berkeley’s view seems to contradict common sense. When we close our eyes, does the world disappear? Berkeley’s response: no, because God is always perceiving it. Dr. Samuel Johnson famously “refuted” Berkeley by kicking a large stone, exclaiming “I refute it thus!” Of course, this doesn’t really refute Berkeley, who would say that Johnson perceived the sensation of kicking, but that doesn’t prove there’s matter behind the sensation.

Despite seeming counterintuitive, Berkeley’s idealism is internally consistent and hard to refute. His work raised important questions about the relationship between perception and reality that continue to interest philosophers.

Voltaire

1694–1778

Voltaire was the pen name of Francois-Marie Arouet, a French Enlightenment writer, historian, and philosopher. He was famous for his wit, his criticism of Christianity and organized religion, and his advocacy of freedom of speech, freedom of religion, and separation of church and state. These positions put him in conflict with the Catholic Church and French government.

[IMAGE: Portrait of Voltaire] 63.

Voltaire was not a systematic philosopher in the academic sense. His philosophical ideas appeared in his many writings—plays, poems, novels, essays, historical works, and letters. His satirical novella Candide is his most famous work. It mocks Leibniz’s optimism that we live in “the best of all possible worlds.”

Born in Paris, Voltaire was a leading figure of the French Enlightenment. He became disconnected from his family, preferring to be called Voltaire, a name he invented that may have been an anagram of a Latinized version of his name. He befriended various philosophers and wrote pieces on religion, freedom of expression, and separation of church and state.

Rationalism

The label “rationalist” applies to a diverse group of figures from the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries whose thought was based on the idea that reason—rather than sensory experience—was the ultimate source of knowledge. That is, only through the processes of logic, deduction, mathematics, and so on, rather than through the unreliable testimony of the senses, could “necessary truth”—truth that was beyond doubt and would remain so—be achieved. René Descartes, Baruch Spinoza, Gottfried Leibniz, and Immanuel Kant are the most significant figures who have come to define this method, but for Descartes, above all, rationalism was a way of establishing a secure and enduring knowledge. For this reason, most of the seventeenth century’s work in philosophy is characterized by Cartesianism—so called because Descartes’ surname in Latin would have been Cartesius.

Descartes, René 1596-1650 (France)

Descartes is considered to be the father of modern philosophy. Educated by Jesuits, he became a soldier, but after a series of disturbing dreams he devoted himself to the search for truth—a project he pursued for much of the rest of his life. What he sought was a method of inquiry that would allow him to separate true ideas from false ones and build up a secure foundation for knowledge. Descartes is thus associated with epistemology, which is the philosophical investigation of the nature, source, and scope of knowledge.

His most famous work is the Meditations (1641), in which he attempts to find certain truth by doubting everything he can possibly doubt. Whatever remained after this radical “hyperbolic doubt” could be regarded as certain. Descartes set about doubting the evidence of his senses (because these had often misled him), his belief in the external world (which might be a dream or hallucination), and even mathematical truths (which a deceptive God or demon might make him believe). What did remain, however, was the fact of his doubting—for even if he doubted, he was at least thinking, and if he was thinking, there had to be an “I” who was thinking. This is the significance of his most famous statement, Cogito ergo sum—“I think, therefore I am.” From this insight he went on to prove the existence of God, and from this in turn to establish a whole philosophical system.

The method of radical doubt laid down by Descartes had a lasting effect on philosophy, and his dualism—according to which reality was split into two distinct types of substance, “thinking things” (res cogitans) and “extended things” (res extensa)—dominated thinking on the nature of mind and body for centuries to come. In his later work he also made groundbreaking contributions to mathematics, science, and optical theory, and he died in 1650.

Spinoza, Baruch 1632-77 (Netherlands)

Spinoza, a Jewish lens grinder from Amsterdam who changed his name to Benedictus when he converted to Christianity, was one of the most original of all rationalist thinkers. His great work, Ethics (1677), was published only after his early death from tuberculosis at the age of 44. A maverick who was excommunicated by the Jewish community for heresy, Spinoza produced a philosophy that can be described as monist and determinist—that is, reality consisted of only one substance (not Descartes’ two), which Spinoza called God or Nature. Furthermore, everything that happens is causally determined by the nature of this single substance; there can be no freedom of the will since everything flows with logical necessity from God or Nature.

p.333

Spinoza’s God has nothing to do with the God of traditional religion, being impersonal and identical to the universe itself. This led to charges of atheism and pantheism. Nevertheless, Spinoza’s philosophy has had a major influence on many later thinkers. He believed that ultimate reality was accessible through reason, not through faith or revelation, and that our goal should be to achieve blessedness and contentment through knowledge of God—that is, through understanding the rational order of the universe. His philosophy was uncompromisingly intellectual and rigorous, written in the style of Euclid’s geometry—starting with axioms, then deducing all knowledge from them. For Spinoza, emotions were to be restrained through the application of reason, and he claimed that emotions were themselves ideas, albeit confused ones. His ethical ideal was to move from the bondage of emotion to the freedom of rational understanding.

Leibniz, Gottfried Wilhelm 1646-1716 (Germany)

The last of the great rationalists, Leibniz was a polymath who made original contributions to mathematics, physics, technology, linguistics, and law as well as philosophy. He is credited with inventing calculus independently of Isaac Newton, which led to an ugly priority dispute. For Leibniz, reality consisted of an infinite number of immaterial, indivisible substances, which he called “monads.” These monads had no parts and no spatial dimensions—they were, in effect, point-like centers of force or energy. Unlike Descartes’ res extensa (physical substance), monads had no extension, and unlike Spinoza’s single substance, there were infinitely many of them.

The monad theory raises the problem of how these isolated substances, which Leibniz insisted had “no windows,” can interact with each other or relate to the external world. His answer was the theory of “pre-established harmony,” according to which God had created all the monads at the beginning of time so that their internal development synchronized perfectly, like a collection of perfect clocks. Thus each monad mirrors the entire universe from its own perspective, and what appears to be interaction is actually just this pre-established correspondence.

Leibniz was also famous for his theodicy—his attempt to reconcile the existence of evil with God’s goodness, power, and knowledge. He concluded that this must be “the best of all possible worlds,” a position that was later satirized by Voltaire in Candide. In his Monadology and Discourse on Metaphysics, he set out his belief that there were two kinds of truth—truths of reason (necessary truths such as those of logic and mathematics) and truths of fact (contingent truths about the world). For Leibniz, the ultimate explanation of why the world exists was to be found in necessary truths discoverable by reason, and the sufficient reason for everything was God.

Kant, Immanuel 1724-1804 (Germany)

Kant spent his entire life in Königsberg, Prussia (now Kaliningrad, Russia), never traveling more than 40 miles from his birthplace. He lived a strictly regimented life—so regular that, according to legend, his neighbors could set their clocks by his afternoon walk. Despite this, he wrote works that revolutionized philosophy and gave it the shape it largely retains today.

His Critique of Pure Reason (1781) sought to synthesize the insights of rationalism and empiricism by arguing that while all knowledge begins with experience, not all knowledge arises from experience. Kant argued that the mind imposes certain structures on experience—what he called the “categories of understanding”—which allow us to make sense of the chaotic stream of sensory input. Space and time themselves were not features of reality as it is in itself, but rather forms of sensory intuition through which we organize our experience.

This led to his famous distinction between phenomena (things as they appear to us) and noumena (things as they are in themselves). We can have knowledge only of phenomena—the world as structured by our mental categories—but we can never know things-in-themselves. This was Kant’s “Copernican Revolution” in philosophy: instead of assuming that our knowledge must conform to objects, he proposed that objects must conform to our ways of knowing.

In ethics, Kant developed his famous “categorical imperative,” a rational test for determining whether an action is morally right. His central idea was that morality was based on reason alone, not on consequences or desires. An action is right if it accords with a principle that could be universally applied—“Act only according to that maxim whereby you can at the same time will that it should become a universal law.” This made morality a matter of duty and respect for rational beings as ends in themselves, never merely as means.

His Critique of Practical Reason (1788) and Critique of Judgment (1790) completed his critical philosophy, which remains one of the most influential bodies of work in the history of thought.

Empiricism

British empiricism developed as a challenge to continental rationalism. While rationalists believed that genuine knowledge came from reason and was known to us innately or a priori (independent of experience), the empiricists argued that all knowledge ultimately derived from sensory experience—what we see, hear, touch, taste, and smell. The mind at birth was a tabula rasa, or blank slate, written upon by experience. This method was championed above all by John Locke, George Berkeley, and David Hume, who are the most significant of the British empiricists.

Locke, John 1632-1704 (England)

Locke is considered the founder of British empiricism and was also an important political philosopher. His most significant philosophical work is An Essay Concerning Human Understanding (1690), which begins with a sustained attack on the rationalist doctrine of innate ideas. Locke argued that there were no innate ideas—moral, logical, or otherwise—and that the mind at birth was like “white paper void of all characters.” All knowledge comes from experience of two kinds: sensation (our awareness of external objects through the senses) and reflection (our awareness of the operations of our own minds).

p.334

Locke also distinguished between primary qualities and secondary qualities of objects. Primary qualities—such as solidity, extension, shape, motion, and number—belonged to objects themselves and existed independently of any observer. Secondary qualities—such as color, sound, taste, and smell—were merely the power that objects had to produce sensations in us, and did not exist in the objects themselves. This distinction became important for later empiricists and for the development of modern science.

In political philosophy, Locke’s Two Treatises of Government (1689) argued for natural rights—life, liberty, and property—and for the social contract theory of government, according to which political authority derived from the consent of the governed. These ideas influenced the American and French Revolutions and continue to underpin liberal democratic thought.

p.335

Berkeley, George 1685-1753 (Ireland)

Berkeley was an Irish bishop who developed a radical form of empiricism known as immaterialism or idealism. His main works are A Treatise Concerning the Principles of Human Knowledge (1710) and Three Dialogues Between Hylas and Philonous (1713). Berkeley argued that if we took Locke’s empiricism seriously, we would have to conclude that we can never have knowledge of material objects existing independently of our minds. All we ever experience directly are our own ideas or perceptions—sensations, images, thoughts. To claim that these ideas are caused by material objects existing “out there” is to go beyond experience and is therefore unjustified.

p.336

Berkeley’s solution was to deny the existence of matter altogether. His famous formula was esse est percipi—“to be is to be perceived.” Physical objects exist only as collections of ideas in the minds of perceivers. A tree in the forest exists only when someone is perceiving it, and it continues to exist even when no human is present because God is always perceiving it. Thus God becomes necessary to Berkeley’s philosophy—He is the permanent perceiver who keeps the universe in existence.

p.337

This seems like a strange and counterintuitive view, and it was met with ridicule by many of Berkeley’s contemporaries. Samuel Johnson famously “refuted” Berkeley by kicking a stone, as if to prove that matter really existed. But Berkeley’s point was not that our experience is an illusion—rather, he was claiming that what we call “matter” adds nothing to our understanding and should be eliminated from our ontology.

Hume, David 1711-76 (Scotland)

p.338

Hume took empiricism to its logical extreme and arrived at a deeply skeptical philosophy that questioned many of our fundamental beliefs. His major work is A Treatise of Human Nature (1739-40), which he later rewrote in a more accessible form as An Enquiry Concerning Human Understanding (1748).

Hume began by dividing all mental contents into impressions (vivid, forceful perceptions such as sensations and emotions) and ideas (faint copies of impressions used in thinking). All ideas must ultimately be derived from impressions; if we cannot trace an idea back to its corresponding impression, then it is meaningless. This became known as Hume’s “fork”: all meaningful propositions are either relations of ideas (such as mathematics and logic) or matters of fact (empirical claims about the world).

Using this principle, Hume attacked many traditional philosophical concepts. He argued that we have no impression of causation—we observe only that one event follows another repeatedly, and from this habit of association we infer a necessary connection. But there is no logical necessity in causation; it is merely a psychological habit. Similarly, we have no impression of the self—what we call the “self” is just a bundle of perceptions with no underlying unity. And we have no rational justification for induction—the assumption that the future will resemble the past. We believe this only out of habit and custom, not because reason supports it.

p.339

In ethics, Hume argued that reason alone cannot motivate action—“Reason is, and ought only to be, the slave of the passions.” Moral judgments are based not on reason but on sentiment and feeling. We call actions “good” or “bad” based on whether they produce feelings of approval or disapproval in us.

Hume’s skeptical conclusions disturbed many of his readers, but his rigorous application of empiricist principles had a profound impact on subsequent philosophy. He famously “woke Kant from his dogmatic slumber,” prompting Kant to develop his critical philosophy as a response to Hume’s skepticism.

German Idealism

German Idealism arose in the late eighteenth and early nineteenth centuries as a response to Kant’s critical philosophy. The idealists accepted Kant’s insight that the mind plays an active role in structuring experience, but they rejected his claim that we can know nothing about things-in-themselves. Instead, they argued that reality itself is fundamentally mental or spiritual in nature. The leading figures were Fichte, Schelling, and Hegel.

Fichte, Johann Gottlieb 1762-1814 (Germany)

Fichte developed a philosophy based on the primacy of the self or ego. In his Foundations of the Science of Knowledge (1794), he argued that all philosophy must begin with the self-positing activity of the “I.” The “I” posits itself, and in doing so, it also posits a “not-I” (the external world) as something to push against. This dialectical process—thesis (I), antithesis (not-I), synthesis (the interaction between them)—became a model for later idealist thinking.

p.340

For Fichte, the external world existed only as something posited by the self for moral purposes. Reality was fundamentally active, creative, and purposive rather than static and mechanical. His philosophy emphasized freedom, moral striving, and the importance of practical reason over theoretical reason.

Fichte was also a nationalist who saw the German nation as having a special role in world history. His Addresses to the German Nation (1808), delivered during the Napoleonic occupation of Prussia, called for German unity and cultural renewal.

Schelling, Friedrich Wilhelm Joseph 1775-1854 (Germany)

Schelling developed a philosophy of nature that sought to overcome the dualism between mind and matter. In his early work, he argued that nature was not dead, mechanical matter but rather unconscious spirit—spirit in the process of becoming conscious. Nature and mind were two aspects of the same underlying reality, which he called the “Absolute.”

Schelling’s philosophy went through several phases. In his philosophy of nature, he emphasized the organic, dynamic, and purposive character of natural processes. In his philosophy of art, he argued that artistic genius and aesthetic intuition could grasp truths that rational philosophy could not. In his later philosophy of mythology and revelation, he turned increasingly to religious and mystical themes.

p.341

Though less systematic than Hegel, Schelling’s thought had a significant influence on Romanticism and on later existentialist thinkers. His emphasis on freedom, creativity, and the limits of rational understanding anticipated many twentieth-century philosophical movements.

Hegel, Georg Wilhelm Friedrich 1770-1831 (Germany)

Hegel was the most influential of the German idealists. His philosophy was ambitious in scope, attempting to provide a comprehensive account of reality, history, knowledge, art, religion, and the state. His major works include The Phenomenology of Spirit (1807), Science of Logic (1812-16), and Elements of the Philosophy of Right (1821).

Central to Hegel’s thought is the dialectical method—a process of development through contradiction and resolution. A concept or situation (thesis) generates its opposite (antithesis), and these are then reconciled at a higher level (synthesis), which itself becomes a new thesis. This process continues until it reaches the “Absolute”—the final, comprehensive truth in which all contradictions are resolved.

p.342

Hegel applied this method to the history of philosophy, arguing that each philosophical system represented a stage in the development of self-consciousness. He also applied it to world history, which he saw as the progressive realization of human freedom. History had a rational structure and purpose—it was the self-development of “Spirit” (Geist) or “Mind” coming to know itself.

In political philosophy, Hegel emphasized the importance of the state as the embodiment of ethical life. Individual freedom could be realized only within the context of a rational social order. His philosophy of history saw certain nations as having world-historical significance—bearers of Spirit at particular moments in time.

Hegel’s influence on subsequent thought was immense. His dialectical method influenced Marx, who “turned Hegel on his head” by applying it to material rather than spiritual development. His emphasis on history and development influenced evolutionary thinking in many fields. And his attempt to create a comprehensive philosophical system set the agenda for much nineteenth-century philosophy.

Utilitarianism

Utilitarianism is an ethical theory that holds that the right action is the one that produces the greatest happiness for the greatest number of people. It is a form of consequentialism—the view that actions should be judged by their consequences rather than by intentions or by conformity to moral rules. The theory was developed primarily by Jeremy Bentham and John Stuart Mill in nineteenth-century Britain.

p.343

Bentham, Jeremy 1748-1832 (England)

Bentham was a legal and social reformer who developed the first systematic version of utilitarianism. In his Introduction to the Principles of Morals and Legislation (1789), he argued that “nature has placed mankind under the governance of two sovereign masters, pain and pleasure.” These two, he claimed, determine what we ought to do as well as what we will do. The principle of utility—the “greatest happiness principle”—states that actions are right in proportion as they tend to promote happiness, wrong as they tend to produce the reverse of happiness.

Bentham attempted to make ethics scientific by developing a “felicific calculus”—a method for calculating the amount of pleasure and pain produced by any action. He identified seven dimensions of pleasure and pain: intensity, duration, certainty, propinquity (nearness), fecundity (likelihood of being followed by similar sensations), purity (likelihood of not being followed by opposite sensations), and extent (number of people affected). By measuring these factors, one could allegedly determine which action would produce the most happiness.

Bentham was a radical reformer who applied utilitarian principles to legal and social questions. He advocated for democratic government, legal reform, prison reform, animal rights, and women’s suffrage—positions that were progressive for his time. He argued that all pleasures were qualitatively the same, differing only in quantity—a view summarized in his statement that “quantity of pleasure being equal, pushpin is as good as poetry” (pushpin was a children’s game).

p.344

Mill, John Stuart 1806-73 (England)

Mill refined and defended utilitarianism in response to criticisms of Bentham’s theory. His short book Utilitarianism (1861) remains the classic statement of the theory. Mill accepted the basic utilitarian principle but made important modifications.

First, he argued that pleasures differ in quality as well as quantity. Some pleasures—intellectual, aesthetic, and moral pleasures—are higher and more valuable than mere physical pleasures. “It is better to be a human being dissatisfied than a pig satisfied; better to be Socrates dissatisfied than a fool satisfied.” This introduced a qualitative dimension that Bentham’s purely quantitative approach lacked.

Second, Mill emphasized that utilitarianism was concerned with long-term happiness and the general welfare, not just immediate pleasure. He argued that cultivating virtues like honesty and justice would, in the long run, promote the greatest happiness, even if in particular cases they might seem to conflict with utility.

p.345

Third, Mill defended individual liberty as essential to human happiness. In On Liberty (1859), he argued for the “harm principle”: the only legitimate reason for restricting individual liberty is to prevent harm to others. Society should not interfere with individual choices that affect only the person making them, even if society disapproves of those choices.

Mill also wrote on political economy, logic, and women’s rights. His The Subjection of Women (1869) was a pioneering work of feminist philosophy, arguing for complete legal and social equality between the sexes.

Materialism and Positivism

In the nineteenth century, many thinkers reacted against idealism by emphasizing the importance of material reality and empirical science. Materialism held that only matter exists, and that all phenomena—including consciousness and thought—can be explained in terms of material processes. Positivism held that knowledge must be based on observable facts and that metaphysical speculation should be abandoned.

Feuerbach, Ludwig 1804-72 (Germany)

Feuerbach was a left-Hegelian who rejected Hegel’s idealism in favor of materialism. In his most famous work, The Essence of Christianity (1841), he argued that religion was a projection of human nature onto an imaginary divine being. God was not a real entity but rather an idealized image of human qualities—love, wisdom, power—projected outward and worshiped. Religion thus involved self-alienation: humans attributed their own best qualities to God rather than recognizing them as their own.

p.346

Feuerbach’s materialism influenced Marx, who famously said that he had “turned Hegel on his head” but should more accurately have said he turned Hegel right-side up. Where Hegel saw history as the development of Spirit, Marx would see it as the development of material economic forces.

Comte, Auguste 1798-1857 (France)

Comte founded the philosophical movement known as positivism. In his Course of Positive Philosophy (1830-42), he argued that human thought progresses through three stages: the theological stage (explaining phenomena by reference to gods or spirits), the metaphysical stage (explaining phenomena by reference to abstract forces or essences), and the positive or scientific stage (explaining phenomena by reference to observable laws and regularities).

According to Comte, philosophy and all knowledge should now enter the positive stage, abandoning metaphysical speculation in favor of empirical science. He coined the term “sociology” and argued that society could be studied scientifically, using the same methods as the natural sciences. He even proposed a “religion of humanity” to replace traditional religion, complete with rituals and a hierarchy of “priests” (scientists and philosophers).

p.347

Though Comte’s specific proposals were often eccentric, his emphasis on scientific method and his rejection of metaphysics influenced later empiricist and scientific movements in philosophy.

Marxism and Historical Materialism

Karl Marx developed a comprehensive philosophy combining German idealism (particularly Hegel), French socialism, and British political economy. His thought focused on understanding and critiquing capitalism and envisioning a future communist society. Rather than merely interpreting the world, Marx wanted to change it.

Marx, Karl 1818-83 (Germany)

Marx’s philosophy, known as historical materialism or dialectical materialism, applied Hegel’s dialectical method to material rather than spiritual reality. While Hegel saw history as the development of ideas or Spirit, Marx argued that material economic conditions—the “mode of production”—determined social, political, and intellectual life. “It is not the consciousness of men that determines their existence, but their social existence that determines their consciousness.”

In Marx’s view, history progressed through a series of stages defined by different modes of production: primitive communism, slave society, feudalism, capitalism, and (in the future) socialism and communism. Each stage contained internal contradictions between the forces of production and the relations of production, leading eventually to revolutionary transformation.

p.348

Under capitalism, the fundamental contradiction was between the bourgeoisie (who owned the means of production) and the proletariat (who owned only their labor power). Capitalists exploited workers by paying them less than the value they produced, appropriating the surplus value as profit. This created class conflict that would eventually lead to revolution and the establishment of a socialist society in which the means of production would be collectively owned.

Marx analyzed capitalism extensively in his masterwork Das Kapital (Capital, 1867-94). He described how capitalism commodified labor, alienated workers from their activity and products, and created cycles of crisis. He predicted that capitalism would eventually collapse due to its internal contradictions—falling rates of profit, concentration of wealth, and increasing immiseration of workers.

Though Marx’s predictions about capitalism’s collapse have not come to pass in the way he envisioned, his analysis of capitalism, class, and ideology has had enormous influence on social theory, economics, history, and political movements worldwide.

Engels, Friedrich 1820-95 (Germany)

Engels was Marx’s lifelong friend, collaborator, and financial supporter. Together they wrote The Communist Manifesto (1848), one of the most influential political documents ever published. After Marx’s death, Engels edited and published the remaining volumes of Das Kapital from Marx’s notes.

p.349

Engels made important contributions to Marxist theory in his own right. His The Condition of the Working Class in England (1845) provided detailed empirical evidence of workers’ exploitation and poverty. His Anti-Dühring (1878) was a comprehensive exposition of Marxist philosophy, and his Dialectics of Nature (though unfinished) attempted to extend dialectical materialism to the natural sciences.

Engels also developed the concept of dialectical materialism more fully than Marx had, emphasizing three “laws” of dialectics: the transformation of quantity into quality (gradual quantitative changes lead to sudden qualitative leaps), the interpenetration of opposites (things contain contradictions within themselves), and the negation of the negation (development proceeds through contradiction and supersession).

Pragmatism

Pragmatism emerged in America in the late nineteenth century as a distinctively American contribution to philosophy. Pragmatists argued that the meaning and truth of ideas should be understood in terms of their practical consequences. An idea is true if it “works”—if believing it leads to successful action and useful results. The major pragmatists were Charles Sanders Peirce, William James, and John Dewey.

p.350

Peirce, Charles Sanders 1839-1914 (United States)

Peirce was a logician, scientist, and philosopher who founded pragmatism, though he later renamed his version “pragmaticism” to distinguish it from William James’s popularization. In his 1878 essay “How to Make Our Ideas Clear,” Peirce formulated what he called the pragmatic maxim: “Consider what effects, that might conceivably have practical bearings, we conceive the object of our conception to have. Then, our conception of these effects is the whole of our conception of the object.”

For Peirce, the meaning of a concept consists in its practical consequences. To understand what we mean by calling something “hard,” for example, we must consider what difference its hardness makes to our experience—that it will resist scratching, that it won’t be easily dented, and so on. The sum of these practical implications is what we mean by “hardness.”

Peirce also developed a theory of truth as the opinion that would be reached by scientists if inquiry were pursued to its ideal limit. Truth is not correspondence to an independent reality or internal coherence, but rather what inquiry would converge upon in the long run. This made truth something achieved through community inquiry rather than individual insight.

p.351

James, William 1842-1910 (United States)

James popularized pragmatism and extended it in ways that went beyond Peirce’s more cautious formulation. In his Pragmatism: A New Name for Some Old Ways of Thinking (1907), James argued that an idea is true if believing it has beneficial consequences. Truth is not static but is “made” in the course of experience.

James’s pragmatism was more psychological and humanistic than Peirce’s. He emphasized the role of will, emotion, and individual temperament in shaping beliefs. In The Will to Believe (1897), he argued that in some cases—particularly regarding religious beliefs—it is legitimate to believe something for which we lack sufficient evidence, if the belief has practical benefits and if failing to believe would itself be a loss.

James also made important contributions to psychology. His Principles of Psychology (1890) was a landmark work that influenced the development of psychology as an empirical science. He developed a theory of emotion (the James-Lange theory) according to which emotions are the perception of bodily changes—we don’t run because we’re afraid; rather, we’re afraid because we run.

p.352

His later work on radical empiricism and pluralism challenged traditional metaphysical assumptions. He argued for a pluralistic universe rather than the monistic universe of traditional philosophy, and he defended a view he called “neutral monism” according to which both mind and matter are constructed from the same fundamental “pure experience.”

Dewey, John 1859-1952 (United States)

Dewey applied pragmatism to education, ethics, politics, and social reform. He was the most influential American philosopher of the first half of the twentieth century and had a profound impact on American educational theory and practice.

Dewey rejected traditional dualisms—mind/body, theory/practice, thought/action—arguing that these were artificial separations. Thinking is not separate from doing but is a form of action directed at solving problems. Knowledge is not abstract contemplation but rather a tool for dealing with practical situations. This led to his theory of “instrumentalism”—the view that ideas, theories, and concepts are instruments for solving problems rather than representations of reality.

p.353

In education, Dewey advocated for progressive education focused on experience, experimentation, and problem-solving rather than rote memorization. His Democracy and Education (1916) argued that education should prepare students for democratic citizenship and meaningful participation in social life.

In ethics, Dewey rejected the search for fixed moral principles, arguing instead that ethical thinking should be experimental and context-sensitive. The task of ethics is not to discover eternal truths but to help us deal intelligently with the moral problems we face. His Ethics (with James Tufts, 1908) and Human Nature and Conduct (1922) developed these themes.

Dewey was also a political activist and public intellectual who wrote on democracy, liberalism, and social reform. He argued for participatory democracy and believed that democracy was not just a form of government but a way of life requiring active, intelligent participation by all citizens.

Phenomenology

Phenomenology is a philosophical method and movement that seeks to describe human experience as it is actually lived, without imposing theoretical assumptions. Rather than beginning with theories about reality or consciousness, phenomenologists try to describe phenomena (appearances) as they present themselves to consciousness. The method was developed by Edmund Husserl and was taken up by later thinkers including Martin Heidegger, Jean-Paul Sartre, and Maurice Merleau-Ponty.

p.354

Husserl, Edmund 1859-1938 (Germany/Austria)

Husserl founded phenomenology as a rigorous philosophical method. Trained as a mathematician, he sought to make philosophy a rigorous science by returning to “the things themselves”—that is, by describing phenomena as they are directly given in experience, before any theoretical interpretation.

His method involved “bracketing” or “epoché”—suspending judgment about the existence of the external world and focusing purely on the content and structure of conscious experience. By doing this, Husserl believed we could discover the essential structures of consciousness and of the various types of objects that can appear to consciousness.

Central to Husserl’s phenomenology is the concept of intentionality—the idea that consciousness is always consciousness of something. Every mental act is directed toward an object: believing is believing something, desiring is desiring something, perceiving is perceiving something. The object need not exist—we can think about unicorns or hallucinate objects that aren’t there—but the intentional structure remains.

p.355

Husserl’s phenomenology went through several phases. His early work, Logical Investigations (1900-1901), focused on the structures of meaning and knowledge. His later work, particularly Ideas (1913) and Cartesian Meditations (1931), developed transcendental phenomenology, which sought to describe the structures of the transcendental ego—the consciousness that constitutes the world of experience.

Though sometimes criticized as overly abstract and technical, Husserl’s phenomenology provided the foundation for twentieth-century continental philosophy and influenced existentialism, hermeneutics, and poststructuralism.

Existentialism

Existentialism is a philosophical movement emphasizing individual existence, freedom, choice, and responsibility. Existentialists argue that humans are not defined by any fixed nature or essence; rather, we create ourselves through our choices and actions. “Existence precedes essence”—we first exist, and only then do we define what we are through our decisions. Key existentialist themes include freedom, anxiety, authenticity, bad faith, and the confrontation with death and meaninglessness.

Kierkegaard, Søren 1813-55 (Denmark)

p.356

Kierkegaard is often called the father of existentialism, though he wrote before the term existed. A Christian thinker who reacted against Hegel’s rationalistic system, Kierkegaard emphasized the importance of individual existence and subjective experience over abstract philosophical systems.

He distinguished three “stages on life’s way”: the aesthetic (living for pleasure and immediate satisfaction), the ethical (living according to moral duty and social norms), and the religious (living in a direct personal relationship with God). Movement from one stage to another requires a “leap of faith”—a decision that cannot be justified by reason alone.

For Kierkegaard, anxiety or dread (Angst) is a fundamental feature of human existence. It arises from our awareness of freedom and possibility—the recognition that we must choose and that our choices define who we are. Authentic existence requires confronting this anxiety and making genuine commitments, rather than losing oneself in conformity to social expectations.

His concept of “truth as subjectivity” challenged the idea that truth is objective and universal. Religious truth, in particular, cannot be grasped by detached rational analysis but only through passionate personal commitment. His famous statement “truth is subjectivity” meant that the most important truths—about how to live, what to value, how to relate to God—are discovered through personal engagement, not through objective inquiry.

Nietzsche, Friedrich 1844-1900 (Germany)

p.357

Nietzsche was one of the most influential and controversial philosophers of the nineteenth century. Though not always classified as an existentialist, his emphasis on individual authenticity, his critique of traditional values, and his concern with how to live a meaningful life in a godless world make him a key figure in existentialist thought.

His most famous declaration is “God is dead”—not a claim about God’s literal death, but rather the claim that traditional religious and moral frameworks have lost their authority in modern culture. With the death of God comes nihilism—the threat that life has no meaning or value. Nietzsche saw this as both a crisis and an opportunity.

His response was to call for a “revaluation of all values.” Traditional Christian morality, which he called “slave morality,” emphasized humility, compassion, and self-sacrifice. Nietzsche argued for an alternative “master morality” based on strength, creativity, and affirmation of life. The ideal human type was the “Übermensch” (overman or superman)—one who creates their own values rather than conforming to herd morality.

p.358

Nietzsche also developed the concept of “eternal recurrence”—the idea that one should live as if every moment would recur infinitely. This serves as a test: would you be willing to live your life over and over again exactly as you’ve lived it? If not, you need to change how you live. The person who can affirm eternal recurrence, who says “yes” to life in its entirety, embodies Nietzsche’s ideal.

His works are written in an aphoristic, literary style quite different from traditional academic philosophy. Major works include Thus Spoke Zarathustra (1883-85), Beyond Good and Evil (1886), and On the Genealogy of Morals (1887).

Heidegger, Martin 1889-1976 (Germany)

Heidegger studied with Husserl but developed phenomenology in a more existential direction. His masterwork Being and Time (1927) seeks to answer the question “What is the meaning of Being?”—a question Heidegger thought philosophy had neglected.

He begins by analyzing human existence, which he calls “Dasein” (literally “being-there”). Dasein is characterized by its awareness of its own existence and its concern about that existence. We don’t simply exist; we take a stand on our existence, we care about it, we interpret it.

Heidegger identifies several fundamental structures of Dasein. We are always “thrown” into a particular situation (historical period, culture, language) that we didn’t choose. We are always “projecting” ourselves into the future through our choices and possibilities. And we are always “falling” into everyday routines and conformity to social expectations, losing touch with our authentic possibilities.

p.359

Authentic existence requires confronting death—not death as a biological event, but death as the ultimate possibility that gives definiteness and urgency to our existence. When we authentically grasp our “being-toward-death,” we can free ourselves from conformity and live in accordance with our own possibilities.

Heidegger’s later philosophy took a different direction, focusing on language, poetry, and the history of Western thought. He argued that modern technology and scientific thinking had led to a forgetting of Being, and he looked to art, poetry, and meditative thinking as ways to recover a more authentic relation to Being.

Heidegger’s involvement with Nazism in the 1930s has remained controversial and has led to ongoing debates about the relationship between his philosophy and his politics.

Sartre, Jean-Paul 1905-80 (France)

Sartre was the most famous existentialist philosopher and did the most to popularize existentialism in the mid-twentieth century. His philosophical work Being and Nothingness (1943) combined phenomenology and existentialism to develop a comprehensive account of human existence.

p.360

Sartre distinguished between “being-in-itself” (en-soi)—the mode of being of objects, which simply are what they are—and “being-for-itself” (pour-soi)—the mode of being of consciousness, which is always directed toward something and is never simply identical with itself. Consciousness introduces “nothingness” into the world: it can negate, imagine alternatives, and project possibilities that don’t yet exist.

This leads to Sartre’s claim that “existence precedes essence.” Unlike a paper knife, which is designed with a specific purpose (its essence) before it exists, humans have no predetermined nature. We first exist, and only then do we create our essence through our free choices. We are “condemned to be free”—we cannot escape the responsibility for choosing who we are.

Bad faith is a central concept in Sartre’s ethics. It consists in denying one’s freedom and responsibility by pretending that one’s identity is fixed or that one’s actions are determined by external factors. The waiter who over-identifies with his role, the woman who pretends she can’t control how others treat her—these are examples of bad faith.

Sartre’s existentialism was explicitly atheistic. Without God, there are no objective values or predetermined human nature. We must create our own values and meaning through our choices. This is both liberating and terrifying—it gives us complete freedom but also complete responsibility.

p.361

Later in his life, Sartre attempted to reconcile existentialism with Marxism in Critique of Dialectical Reason (1960). He was also a playwright, novelist, and political activist who wrote extensively on literature, politics, and social issues.

Camus, Albert 1913-60 (Algeria/France)

Though Camus rejected the label “existentialist,” his philosophical concerns aligned closely with existentialism. His central theme was the absurd—the confrontation between our human need for meaning and the indifferent, meaningless universe.

In The Myth of Sisyphus (1942), Camus uses the Greek myth of Sisyphus—condemned forever to roll a boulder up a mountain only to watch it roll back down—as a metaphor for human existence. Like Sisyphus, we seek meaning in a meaningless world. The question is whether this absurdity makes life worth living or justifies suicide.

Camus argues that suicide is not the answer. Instead, we must acknowledge the absurd and continue living in spite of it—or rather, because of it. Sisyphus, aware that his task is meaningless yet continuing anyway, represents the human condition. “One must imagine Sisyphus happy,” Camus concludes. Happiness comes not from denying the absurd but from embracing it with defiance.

p.362

In The Rebel (1951), Camus explored the political implications of absurdism. He rejected nihilism and political violence, arguing instead for revolt—ongoing resistance to oppression and injustice without hope of final victory. This put him at odds with Sartre, who defended revolutionary violence in certain circumstances.

Camus was also a major novelist and playwright. Works like The Stranger (1942) and The Plague (1947) dramatized his philosophical themes in compelling narratives that reached a wide audience.

Analytic Philosophy

Analytic philosophy emerged in the early twentieth century, primarily in Britain and later in the United States. It emphasized clarity, precision, logical analysis, and attention to language. While continental philosophy (phenomenology, existentialism) focused on lived experience and interpretation, analytic philosophy focused on logical structure and conceptual analysis. Major figures include Frege, Russell, Wittgenstein, and the logical positivists.

Frege, Gottlob 1848-1925 (Germany)

Frege is considered the founder of modern logic and analytic philosophy. His work in logic and philosophy of language laid the foundation for much twentieth-century philosophy. In his Begriffsschrift (Concept-Script, 1879), he created a formal logical system more powerful than anything that had existed before, introducing quantifiers, variables, and a notation for expressing complex logical relations.

p.363

Frege’s work in philosophy of language distinguished between sense (Sinn) and reference (Bedeutung). The reference of a term is the object it refers to, while its sense is the way that object is presented or the mode of presentation. For example, “the morning star” and “the evening star” both refer to the same object (the planet Venus), but they have different senses—different ways of picking out that object.

This distinction solved puzzles about identity statements. “The morning star is the evening star” is informative because the two terms have different senses, even though they have the same reference. Frege’s analysis influenced philosophy of language throughout the twentieth century.

He also developed a logicist project, attempting to show that mathematics could be reduced to logic. Though this project ultimately failed due to Russell’s paradox, it profoundly influenced the development of mathematical logic and set theory.

Russell, Bertrand 1872-1970 (United Kingdom)

Russell made major contributions to logic, philosophy of language, epistemology, and ethics. With Alfred North Whitehead, he wrote Principia Mathematica (1910-13), an attempt to derive all mathematics from logical principles. Though the project was incomplete, it demonstrated the power of formal logic for philosophical analysis.

p.364

Russell’s theory of descriptions (1905) showed how logical analysis could dissolve philosophical problems. The sentence “The present King of France is bald” seems puzzling: if there is no King of France, is it true, false, or meaningless? Russell’s analysis showed that the sentence should be understood as making three claims: (1) there is at least one King of France, (2) there is at most one King of France, and (3) whatever is King of France is bald. Since the first claim is false, the whole sentence is false, not meaningless.

This method of logical analysis became central to analytic philosophy. By revealing the logical form beneath grammatical form, Russell believed philosophy could solve problems that arose from linguistic confusion.

Russell was also a prominent public intellectual, pacifist, and social critic. He was imprisoned for his pacifism during World War I and remained politically active throughout his long life. His History of Western Philosophy (1945) introduced philosophy to a broad audience, and he was awarded the Nobel Prize for Literature in 1950.

p.365

Wittgenstein, Ludwig 1889-1951 (Austria/United Kingdom)

Wittgenstein wrote two masterpieces that profoundly influenced twentieth-century philosophy, though they advanced very different views. His first book, Tractatus Logico-Philosophicus (1921), developed a picture theory of language. According to this theory, language represents reality by picturing it—the structure of a meaningful sentence mirrors the structure of the fact it represents. Only propositions that picture facts have meaning; traditional philosophical questions about ethics, aesthetics, and metaphysics try to say what can only be shown. “Whereof one cannot speak, thereof one must be silent.”

The Tractatus influenced the logical positivists, though Wittgenstein himself became dissatisfied with its conclusions. In his later work, particularly Philosophical Investigations (published posthumously in 1953), he developed a very different view. Language is not primarily a picture of reality but rather a tool we use for various purposes. Meaning is determined not by correspondence to reality but by use—“meaning is use.”

Wittgenstein introduced the concept of “language games”—the various rule-governed activities in which we engage using language. There is no single essence of language, but rather a family of overlapping uses. Philosophical problems arise when we misunderstand the grammar of our language or try to use words outside their proper language games. The task of philosophy is therapeutic: to dissolve confusions by clarifying how language actually works.

p.366

“Philosophy is a battle against the bewitchment of our intelligence by means of language,” he wrote. Rather than constructing theories, philosophy should show how philosophical problems arise from misunderstandings and can be dissolved through conceptual clarification.

Logical Positivism

Logical positivism (or logical empiricism) was a philosophical movement that emerged in Vienna in the 1920s and 1930s. The Vienna Circle, as the group was known, sought to make philosophy scientific by eliminating metaphysics and grounding all knowledge in empirical observation and logical analysis.

Ayer, A.J. 1910-89 (United Kingdom)

Ayer brought logical positivism to the English-speaking world with his book Language, Truth and Logic (1936). Written when he was only 26, it presented a forceful and accessible account of positivist philosophy.

The central claim was the verification principle: a statement is meaningful only if it can be verified by observation or if it is a tautology (true by definition, like mathematical and logical truths). Any statement that cannot be verified is literally meaningless—including most traditional metaphysics, theology, and ethics.

p.367

This led to an emotivist theory of ethics: moral statements like “murder is wrong” do not state facts that could be true or false. Rather, they express emotions or attitudes—saying “murder is wrong” is like saying “murder—boo!” Moral language is meaningful only in expressing and influencing feelings, not in describing reality.

Though the verification principle faced serious objections (including the problem that it itself couldn’t be verified), logical positivism had an enormous influence on mid-twentieth century philosophy. It shaped the scientific worldview that still dominates much analytic philosophy and helped establish philosophy of language and philosophy of science as central areas of philosophical inquiry.

Postmodernism and Poststructuralism

Postmodernism and poststructuralism emerged in France in the 1960s and 1970s as reactions against traditional philosophy, particularly the search for universal truths and foundations. These movements emphasized the role of language, power, and social construction in shaping knowledge and reality. They were skeptical of “grand narratives” and universal claims, emphasizing instead plurality, difference, and the instability of meaning.

p.368

Foucault, Michel 1926-84 (France)

Foucault studied how power operates through knowledge and social institutions. Rather than seeing power as something possessed by individuals or governments, he analyzed how power is dispersed throughout society in networks of relationships and practices.

His early work examined how societies define and treat madness, illness, and criminality. Madness and Civilization (1961) showed how the concept of mental illness was historically constructed and used to control deviant behavior. The Birth of the Clinic (1963) analyzed how modern medicine created new ways of seeing and classifying bodies. Discipline and Punish (1975) examined how modern societies control behavior through surveillance and normalization rather than through physical punishment.

A key concept is “power/knowledge”—the idea that power and knowledge are intertwined. Knowledge is not neutral but is shaped by power relations, and power operates through the production and circulation of knowledge. Systems of knowledge (like psychiatry or criminology) define what counts as normal and abnormal, rational and irrational, creating categories that enable social control.

Foucault’s later work focused on sexuality and ethics. The History of Sexuality (1976-84) challenged the idea that sexuality is a natural, biological given, showing instead how it has been constructed through discourse and power relations. His last works explored ancient ethics and “technologies of the self”—practices through which individuals work on themselves to become certain kinds of persons.

p.369

Derrida, Jacques 1930-2004 (France)

Derrida developed deconstruction, a method of reading texts that reveals their internal contradictions and the instability of meaning. Traditional philosophy, Derrida argued, has been based on “logocentrism”—the assumption that meaning is fixed and determinate, centered in an authoritative origin (whether God, consciousness, or the author’s intention).

Deconstruction shows that meaning is never fully present or stable. Every sign gets its meaning from its difference from other signs, and this process of differentiation never comes to an end. Derrida coined the term “différance” (combining the French words for “difference” and “deferral”) to capture this idea that meaning is endlessly deferred, never fully present.

He also analyzed the binary oppositions that structure Western thought: speech/writing, presence/absence, nature/culture, male/female. These oppositions are not neutral but hierarchical—one term is privileged over the other. Deconstruction shows how these hierarchies are unstable and how the subordinated term always haunts and disrupts the privileged one.

p.370

Derrida’s style is deliberately difficult and playful, using wordplay, neologisms, and unconventional arguments. His major works include Of Grammatology (1967), Writing and Difference (1967), and Margins of Philosophy (1972). Though initially controversial, deconstruction profoundly influenced literary theory, cultural studies, and postmodern thought.

Iris Murdoch was a highly successful novelist and moral philosopher. She was also an accomplished academic who taught at Oxford University for many years. Albert Camus was a French-Algerian philosopher, author, and journalist. Though he is often associated with existentialism, he rejected that label and preferred to be seen as writing about the absurd. The absurd, for Camus, is the fundamental conflict between our human need for meaning and the meaningless, indifferent universe.

100. Foucault, Michel 1926 to 1984 Post-Modernism

Michel Foucault was a French philosopher and historian of ideas who had an enormous influence on late twentieth-century thought. His work challenged conventional ways of thinking about power, knowledge, sexuality, madness, and the human subject.

Foucault’s approach was genealogical and archaeological. He studied the historical emergence of concepts, practices, and institutions, showing how things that seem natural or inevitable actually have specific histories and serve particular power relations. His major works include Madness and Civilization (1961), The Birth of the Clinic (1963), The Order of Things (1966), Discipline and Punish (1975), and The History of Sexuality (multiple volumes, 1976-1984).

p.383

One of Foucault’s key insights was that power is not simply repressive—it doesn’t just say “no.” Power is also productive; it creates subjects, bodies of knowledge, and ways of living. Modern societies, he argued, exercise power not primarily through violence or threat, but through more subtle mechanisms of surveillance, normalization, and the internalization of discipline.

Foucault was particularly interested in how modern institutions—prisons, hospitals, schools, factories—produce “docile bodies” through systems of observation and discipline. The panopticon, Jeremy Bentham’s design for a prison where guards could observe all inmates without being seen themselves, served as a model for understanding modern power: we discipline ourselves because we might be being watched.

p.384

His work on sexuality challenged the “repressive hypothesis”—the idea that modern society had repressed sexuality. Instead, he argued, modernity had created an explosion of discourse about sexuality, transforming it into a central component of personal identity.

Foucault died of AIDS-related illness in 1984, one of the first prominent French intellectuals to die of the disease.

101. Lorde, Audre 1934 to 1992 Feminism

p.385

Audre Lorde was an American writer, feminist, womanist, librarian, and civil rights activist. She was a self-described “black, lesbian, mother, warrior, poet” who dedicated her life and creative talent to confronting and addressing injustices of racism, sexism, classism, and homophobia.

Lorde’s work emphasized the importance of recognizing difference—particularly among women and among feminists. She argued that difference should not be a source of separation and distrust, but rather a source of power and creativity. Her famous essay “The Master’s Tools Will Never Dismantle the Master’s House” challenged white, middle-class feminism to recognize its own limitations and exclusions.

She introduced the concept of the “mythical norm”—the idea that society posits a standard of what is normal (white, thin, male, young, heterosexual, Christian, financially secure) against which everyone else is measured and found wanting. Those who do not fit this norm, she argued, are expected to conform to it or be marginalized.

p.386

Lorde also wrote powerfully about anger as a legitimate and necessary response to oppression, and about the erotic as a source of power and knowledge for women. Her work has been foundational for intersectional feminism, black feminism, and queer theory.

[IMAGE: Portrait photograph of Konrad Lorenz]

102. Lorenz, Konrad 1903 to 1989 Behavioral Sciences

Konrad Lorenz was an Austrian zoologist, ethologist, and ornithologist who shared the Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine in 1973 with Nikolaas Tinbergen and Karl von Frisch for their discoveries concerning organization and elicitation of individual and social behavior patterns in animals.

Lorenz is best known for his discovery of the principle of imprinting—the process by which young animals form attachments to the first moving object they see during a critical period shortly after birth or hatching. His famous image, waddling along followed by a line of goslings who had imprinted on him, became iconic.

p.387

His work helped establish ethology—the scientific study of animal behavior—as a legitimate field. He emphasized studying animals in their natural environments rather than in laboratory settings, and he argued that many behaviors are innate rather than learned.

However, Lorenz’s reputation has been complicated by his involvement with the Nazi party and his writings supporting racial hygiene theories during the 1940s. While he later expressed regret for these views, they remain a controversial part of his legacy.

104. Gopnik, Alison C. (Mrs.) 1955 to Present

Alison Gopnik is a professor of psychology and affiliate professor of philosophy at the University of California, Berkeley. She is an internationally recognized leader in the study of children’s learning and development.

Gopnik’s research focuses on how children learn about the world and other people’s minds. She has studied how even very young children are able to learn about cause and effect, to understand that other people have different beliefs and desires, and to imagine alternative possibilities.

Her work challenges the traditional view of children as irrational or pre-rational. Instead, she argues that children are actually remarkably sophisticated thinkers and learners—in some ways, better learners than adults. Children’s play, she suggests, is a form of scientific experimentation.

p.389

Gopnik has written several popular books, including The Philosophical Baby (2009) and The Gardener and the Carpenter (2016), which bring her research to a general audience. She argues that modern parenting has become too focused on “carpentry”—trying to shape children into specific predetermined forms—rather than “gardening”—creating conditions in which children can flourish.

[IMAGE: Portrait photograph of Philippa Foot]

105. Foot, Philippa 1920 to 2010 Virtue Ethics

Philippa Foot was a British philosopher best known for her work in ethics, particularly her revival of virtue ethics and her invention of the famous “trolley problem” thought experiment.

Foot was part of a mid-twentieth-century movement to revive Aristotelian virtue ethics as an alternative to the then-dominant approaches of consequentialism and deontology. She argued that virtues like courage and justice are not simply good in themselves, but are connected to human flourishing—what Aristotle called eudaimonia.

p.390

In her 1967 paper “The Problem of Abortion and the Doctrine of Double Effect,” Foot introduced what became known as the trolley problem: Would it be morally permissible to divert a runaway trolley onto a track where it will kill one person instead of five? What if instead of diverting the trolley, you had to push someone in front of it to stop it? These scenarios have generated enormous philosophical discussion about the difference between doing and allowing, intended and foreseen consequences, and using someone as a means versus as a side effect.

Foot’s most important work, Natural Goodness (2001), published when she was 81, argues that moral evaluation is a species of natural evaluation—that we can evaluate humans in the same way we evaluate other living things, in terms of how well they realize their natural functions.

p.391

[IMAGE: Portrait photograph of Martha Nussbaum]

106. Nussbaum, Martha Craven 1947 to Present

Martha Nussbaum is an American philosopher and the current Ernst Freund Distinguished Service Professor of Law and Ethics at the University of Chicago. She has made major contributions to ancient philosophy, political philosophy, feminism, and ethics.

Nussbaum is best known for her capabilities approach to development and justice, developed in collaboration with economist Amartya Sen. Rather than focusing solely on resources or subjective well-being, the capabilities approach asks what people are actually able to do and be. It focuses on creating conditions that enable people to live flourishing lives.

Nussbaum has identified ten central capabilities that should be secured to all people as a matter of basic justice: life; bodily health; bodily integrity; senses, imagination, and thought; emotions; practical reason; affiliation; other species; play; and control over one’s environment (political and material).

p.392

Her work on emotions has challenged the traditional philosophical view that emotions are irrational disturbances. Instead, she argues that emotions embody judgments and evaluations and are therefore essential to ethical life.

Nussbaum has also written extensively on literature and philosophy, arguing that reading novels and engaging with literature is not a distraction from serious philosophical work, but can itself be a form of ethical reflection and moral education.
