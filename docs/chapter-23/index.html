Philosophy (mostly, with a bit of religion and science)

For all of my younger years and most of my adulthood my personal inclination was to avoid philosophy and philosophers like the plague. Had it not been that I needed to understand their thoughts for the sake of this book, I certainly wouldn’t have troubled to research them. But needs must, so in hope but not expectation I’ve done some paddling, albeit shallowly, in the turgid waters of the polemicists. For much of this process all those deep, philosophical musings from some of mankind’s greatest minds left me cold and unimpressed. It’s mostly impenetrable, without some effort and help to untangle, and seemingly self-serving too. Only in the latter stages of my reading about the great philosophers did I begin to realise a little more clearly what they were getting at. It was with this growing understanding that I began to appreciate and admire the amazing thoughts of most of these people.

But it’s worth setting out my early, highly critical, reactions of this whole genre because I suspect these are what many people feel about philosophers and philosophy. I had some fun in the process. Here are my initial jaundiced views.

Difficult to understand: Philosophers have a common approach to the way they present their thoughts - with a few exceptions. The more complicated and involved the better, they think. All lengthy philosophical writings are the opposite of the Occam’s razor approach, where the simplest explanation is normally the right one. You think I’m being too harsh? Then try out Heidegger: “Temporality temporalises itself fully in each ecstasy, i.e. in the ecstatic unity of the complete temporalising of temporality there is grounded the wholeness of the structural complex of existentiality, factuality and fallenness, which comprises the unity of the care- structure” What…?

Why didn’t these great brains apply themselves more productively: dig their garden; concoct a new, nutritious recipe for baked beans on toast; solve both energy and transport problems by genetic manipulation to evolve furry, flying humans, how to permanently and cheaply dump shed loads of carbon to avert Climate Change. They could most usefully even come up with the surest method to stamp out super-bugs, like Covid 19? Instead we have from them diatribes of diarrhoeic output.

The three Routes to Knowledge and Wisdom

<u>Philosophy:</u> This is a route down which we can travel to learn and understand more about life, the world and everything. Through study of philosophy and by following the rigour of philosophical thinking knowledge and wisdom can be gained apparently, although not necessarily correctly. But, arguably it’s only one of three routes to knowledge and wisdom, the other two being religion and science.

p.247

Some people claim that only one of these three routes will lead us to true knowledge. On the other hand, there are sceptics who say we are not capable of knowing true reality; regardless of which route is taken. We cannot be sure of anything at all these doubters assert. We will investigate the reasoning behind such views later, but not now. In this chapter of the book, Hello, Hello, the focus is on philosophy but in other areas of the book religion and science get a much fuller examination Before focusing on philosophy let’s have just a brief look at religion and science and their claims to be, if not the fount of all knowledge, at least the most secure base for true knowledge. Then we will return to philosophy.

<u>Religion:</u> In 1871, British anthropologist Sir Edward Burnett Tylor, defined the essential element of all religion quite simply as: “the belief in spiritual beings.” He thought animism was the earliest example of religion. Later Tylor’s elegant definition was expanded to recognise that religions commonly have a set of beliefs, rites and rituals specific to the particular religion. These usually fall under the umbrella of a church, which has a structure of expert practitioners who take the lead with the observance of the rites and rituals. They are also authoritative figures who can give answers to theological questions.

Many religions refer to a supernatural entity and a personal God. They typically claim there is a form of afterlife or alternate existence after death. Their beliefs are usually based on a particular holy book. The Abrahamic ones and some others tell of a supernatural basis for creation.

What’s the origin of religion’s claim to be the fount of all knowledge? Humans have always had religion of one sort or another, even back to pre-historic times. We know from the evidence of more than 30,0000 years ago forms of spiritualism, usually shamanism or animism, were practiced by our hunter-gatherer forbearers.

p.248

These early forms of religion, closely identified with the natural and imagined spiritual worlds, had probably been in existence since the dawn of modern humans, around 300,000 years ago.

About 10,000 years ago agricultural societies began to replace hunter-gathering humans. Humans gathered in larger groups and with the more assured food supply that agriculture offered had more time on their hands. They were able to use this spare time to develop more complex religious beliefs. Since the dawn of agriculture religion has grown into a mighty and diverse industry, now dominated by the monotheism of the Abrahamic trio: Christianity, Islam and Judaism. But it also includes many others, predominately far-eastern ones, like Hinduism, Sikhism, Shinto and those that blur the line between religion and philosophy such as Buddhism, Taoism and Confucianism.

These institutionalised religions have in common their certainty of a type of reality. They claim to know how things have worked and will work out, in metaphysical terms, and they tell us of absolute truths, particularly about the consequences of different types of human behaviour and the relevance of good and evil. These religions are largely a form of revelatory knowledge, via various holy texts. It is a matter of faith that the content of the texts was revealed to the author(s) by some great power. The texts give all the necessary answers to how humans should live their lives and in what way the particular deity must be revered. The sacred texts must not be questioned. Believers are required to have faith in place of proof.

The Bible is stuffed full of examples of revelatory truth, one of very many, appropriately towards the beginning of the book, is the story of man’s origin. In Genesis, chapter 2, God says: “And the LORD God formed man of the dust of the ground, & breathed into his nostrils the breath of life; and man became a living soul.” (https://www.kingjamesbibleonline.org/1611_Genesis-2-7/) We now know, thanks to Charles Darwin and Wallace, that man was the product of evolution. He was not formed out of dust, unless the Biblical author was thinking of stellar dust. Some believers think evolution happened too fast to be natural. It must have been speeded up by God they say. Other believers claim that Genesis is only intended to give a general indication, both of creation and man’s original defiance of God. It’s not meant to taken literally.

There have also been several attempted logical proofs of the monotheistic God’s existence, but to date no one has yet managed this feat. These are described in detail elsewhere in this book. Nor has anyone shown conclusively that re-incarnation happens or that the state of nirvana can be achieved,

p.249

as claimed by Buddhism. So perhaps religious revelatory truth is not truth at all. It may either be allegorical, just unsupported opinion, or even simple story telling.

However, many millions believe their brand of religion gives some knowledge about big issues, like the existence of a supreme being, the origin of the universe, man’s place in it and what is expected of him. The use of the term “man” is sometimes meant to be generic and include women, although in principle religions women may be a little ignored or treated as less important than men.

Religion therefore is a form of true knowledge for many people. Some believe so fervently that anyone with alternate beliefs can be subject to physical disapproval. The strength and intolerance of religious belief has led to much violent conflict. Charles de Montesquieu, a judge and political philosopher, thought this was the result of narrow-minded bigotry.

Religious wars are not caused by the fact that there is more than one religion, but by the spirit of intolerance…the spread of which can only be regarded as the total eclipse of human reason.

Charles de Montesquieu 1689 to1755

Comparative Features of the three road to knowledge

There are other features to note about the three roads to wisdom. Proponents of one or the other will sometimes champion their knowledge source as the only correct road to follow. Religion does stand out in contrast with the other two here. It is only religious zealots who can sometimes kill, maim or imprison if their religion’s version of the truth is not adhered to. Philosophers and scientists are not known to murder in defence of their ideas. They are more likely to dispute verbally or in writing, occasionally very forcefully. Pythagoras was a one-off here. He did break the philosophers duck by drowning a student for giving the game away on irrational numbers. And there is the milder incident where Wittgenstein is alleged to have waved a poker at Karl Popper. These occurrences though fail to get anywhere near religions’ propensity for murder on a mass scale.

<u>Togetherness:</u> Philosophy, religion and science do not always stand entirely alone, sometimes one route can blur into another. Philosophy is the only one of the three schools that can have strong links to the other two. Religion has been, especially in the past, anti-science to an extent and likewise science can be dismissive of religion. Some philosophers use philosophy to support religion; others see it as a separate, truer basis for knowledge than what they consider to be superstitious religious beliefs. Voltaire dramatically put it: “Superstition sets the whole world in flames; philosophy quenches them.”

<u>Another connection - church approval or disapproval:</u> Philosophy is sometimes closely linked to religion because theologians may identify a philosopher’s ideas as giving support to their religious beliefs. The two can

also be connected for the opposite reason; the church strongly disapproves of a philosopher, and his philosophy.

Having one’s philosophy strongly approved by the dominant church allows a philosopher to get his name and ideas mentioned publicly.

Aristotle was much admired by church theologians, mostly up to Galileo (1564 - 1642), but a bit beyond him too. Aristotle’s mistaken view that the Earth was the centre of the universe fitted in nicely with Roman Catholic beliefs for about 2,000 years.

Other philosophers may also get publicity, for the opposite reason. They may be strongly disapproved of by an established religion. Galileo is an example He daringly went against Catholic certainty by saying the Earth was not the centre of the universe. It was only in 1992, 350 years after Galileo, that Pope John Paul 11 finally admitted the Catholic Church’s condemnation of him had been in error. A couple of hundred years after Galileo, Church creationists were not enamoured of Darwin’s evolutionary ideas. For many of them, the Biblical Book of Genesis was believed to be literally true. Spinoza got a huge amount of unintended free advertising, He was excommunicated and declared a non-Jew in 17th century Amsterdam. In addition, his works were banned by the Catholic church. Upsetting two churches did wonders in getting his name and philosophy bandied about. To this day, he is still to the fore because several efforts to have his excommunication rescinded have all failed. Jewish historian, Joseph Klausner tried unsuccessfully in 1927. Other attempts included one in 2012, put before a committee of four, which included the American philosopher Steven Nadler. This resulted in yet another refusal.

PHILOSOPHY - definitions, usage and origins

We are now going back to focus on philosophy.

<u>Meaning:</u> What exactly is philosophy? There are a lot of definitions, just try any dictionary or a Google search and you will see what I mean.

The Oxford Dictionary gives one definition: “The study of the fundamental nature of knowledge, reality, and existence, especially when considered as an academic discipline.” Not bad, but philosophy covers more than this, as will be explained. The great Plato had a more fundamental description: “Wonder is very much the affection of a philosopher; for there is no other beginning of philosophy than this.” Yes, it’s possible to wonder about anything. That’s at the base of all philosophy.

Plato’s brilliantly simple one-word description – wonder - needs to be fleshed out a bit more. It was the ancient Greeks who got philosophy well and truly on the road so it’s natural to look to them and their meaning of the term. Not surprisingly they were the first to formally coin the particular words, philo (love) and sophia (wisdom). When joined together these became philosophia meaning the love of wisdom. The term later gained a second and wider meaning; the logical exploration of ideas, virtually on any topic.

p.253

Their original definition - love of wisdom - points to the motivation of these Greek philosophers. They wanted to understand and gain knowledge, not just ponder the nature of knowledge.

The later meaning - the logical exploration of ideas - described more specifically what they did and how they did it. A meld of these two meanings is what philosophy is all about in a nutshell. But, given the extent and range of philosophical coverage over more than two and a half thousand years since the Greek originators, that shell comes from one mighty big nut! Philosophy is renowned for its argumentative and contentious nature, so there can be a lot of dispute about this statement.

Is there any point to philosophy?

A fair question. As touched on earlier, you can find lots of people sceptical or even contemptuous of philosophy. They don’t see that it has any relevance to their lives. Philosophy can be a complex subject, with its own quite difficult language and sometimes pedantic, tortuous reasoning. It can go into minute and seemingly pointless detail. What’s it all about?

This was a question asked of Alfie by Cilla Black in her hit song of the same title. She went on: “Is it just for the moment we live?” Cilla might have been prompted to ask this question having read Jostein Gaarder’s brilliant book of philosophical history and guidance, “Sophie’s World”. Cilla’s were philosophical questions touching on ethics and metaphysics. Her’s is a simple example that philosophy can be part of normal life. It’s a road down which we can travel to learn and understand more about the human condition, the world and everything.

Philosophy is for all of us, not just for Alfie, because it:

  • has been transformational in human history;
  • has been the turning point from solely faith based religious explanations to a more reasoned approach;
  • helped found the scientific method;
  • anticipated scientific findings to some extent.

Great expertise is not a necessity. We don’t need to be capable of proceeding from the simple to the paradoxical, as Bertrand suggests. By just a little study of philosophy we can understand the necessity of questioning through critical and logical thinking. In the process, we learn to doubt and be suspicious of any claim of certainty. Freer thinking enables us to step away from what can be the stultifying influence of culture, place and inheritance.

None of us are likely to approach the skill of Socrates in doing this sort of analytical dissection, but we can try. These philosophical techniques can be useful to us in any sphere of human activity. By their application we can gain both knowledge and wisdom. This does not mean we can magically reach final and certain truths through philosophy. It’s just a way of inching in that direction. Often, the process reveals more questions than it does answers. If you want certainty, go for religion, not philosophy.

So, philosophy can help us think more effectively. It also has a use in aiding us to broaden and deepen our thinking in important areas of human activity and thought. This it does through the main branches it delves into. It presents many ideas that give us much to ponder. Here are philosophy’s main topic areas (there are others) in summary:

  • <u>Epistemology</u>: - (the study of knowledge). Epistemology is relevant to every branch of philosophy;
  • <u>Metaphysics</u> - the origin and nature of existence;
p.255
  • <u>Political philosophy</u> - the argument for various political and justice systems;
  • <u>Ethics</u> - how we should act towards others and ourselves;
  • <u>Religious philosophy</u> - the use of philosophical reasoning by theologians to support certain religious beliefs;
  • <u>Aesthetics</u> - the relevance and importance of art, beauty, good taste and nature. Aesthetics and ethics are sub-divisions of what is known as axiology.

And in more detail:

<u>Epistemology</u> - We have to go to those ancient Greeks again for the base meaning of this term. It comes from “episteme," meaning knowledge and “logos” which can mean to study. OK, so epistemology is the study of knowledge. Why study it? Everyone knows what knowledge is. It’s stuff you learn and understand, so what’s all the fuss about, why do we need to philosophise about it? You might reasonably surmise this. But we are talking about philosophy which requires a disciplined, critical and logical thinking approach to any topic. Epistemology, or more plainly knowledge, has been subjected to this rigorous analysis by philosophers. The result is that knowledge is a bit more complicated than one might think.

Under the broad heading that is epistemology, philosophers have first tried to determine the nature of knowledge; that is, what does it mean to say that someone knows, or fails to know, something? This is a matter of understanding what knowledge is, and how to tell the difference between instances in which someone knows something, or thinks they know something, and when someone does not know something. While there is some general agreement about aspects of this issue, we shall see that this question is much more involved than one might first imagine.

Second, they’ve considered the extent of human knowledge; that is, how much do we, or can we, know? How do we use our brains, consciousness and senses - especially sight and sound? In addition, how do we use the information provided by other people to acquire knowledge? Are there limits to what we can know? And the really big one, can we know anything at all?

One of the all-time greats, Socrates seemed to offer support to this latter view. He said: “I know that I know nothing”. Perhaps his exceptional dialectical analysis of just about anything led him to this comment. It’s a paradoxical claim of course, if Socs. knew that he knew nothing then that means he knows something!

p.256

Socrates pared away so finely at every apparent certainty, maybe he reached a level of doubt to an extent he could not be sure he knew anything. Or maybe his was a teasing remark, claiming false modesty. It’s perfectly possible, after all Socrates was known to have a sense of humour. He was also well aware that Athenians had high regard for his supreme intelligence.

Aristotle, in one of his many comments about knowing and knowledge said this: “What we know is not capable of being otherwise; of things capable of being otherwise we do not know, when they have passed outside our observation, whether they exist or not. Therefore, the object of knowledge is of necessity.” and, “To say of something which is that it is not, or to say of something which is not that it is, is false. However, to say of something which is that it is, or of something which is not that it is not, is true.” You have to read this a second time, quite slowly, to appreciate what he’s saying. But you may now have reached a stage where you want to close this book and watch some TV. We are starting to get into the area of complexity and nit-picking that makes many turn away from philosophy. However, both Socrates and Aristotle are worthy of noting. They have indicated that the extent of human knowledge is an important issue, so let’s carry on. So, back to the first point, i.e., what is knowledge? In basic terms knowledge is an understanding of reality. Information of this reality is gained through a process of reason and experience or a combination of the two. For some religious claims of knowledge, it’s neither by reason or experience, it’s revelatory and faith based.

Traditionally, the type of knowledge we have been concerned with is known as propositional knowledge. This is the knowledge about the result or end point rather than knowledge of how to do something. In arithmetic, we know that 3+4=7. Knowing the answer, 7, is propositional knowledge. The knowledge of the addition process that is needed to arrive at the answer is not propositional. Epistemologists generally concentrate on what they consider to be propositional knowledge. When examining more deeply propositional knowledge, philosophers have come up with two types:

  • <u>a priori</u> - this is where knowledge is gained through reason and logic, not that based on experience. A priori knowledge comes before we have the experience. Sometimes intuition is thought to be a priori knowledge. Those philosophers who claim a priori knowledge to be of prime importance are called rationalists;
  • <u>a posteriori</u> - is the opposite of above. It is knowledge gained through experience. It involves use of our senses to gain factual evidence of our world. Philosophers who emphasise the essential nature of this form of knowledge are known as empiricists.
p.257

To take these further epistemologists, generally, have identified three related conditions necessary to claim knowledge, whether it’s priori or a posteriori knowledge. These conditions must all line up for someone to know something as “knowledge”:

<u>truth; justification; belief.</u>

It was Plato who originally came up with this argument. Basically, it means that something can only be known as knowledge if it is a belief that is based on absolute grounds as being true and this belief must be justified, i.e. subject to rigorous proof. Anything that failed this test is an opinion, not knowledge. This means that some beliefs or claims of knowledge can only be labelled as opinions. Edmund Gettier has tried to challenge this, but his views are not wholly accepted. Plato’s pupil, Aristotle took a slightly different approach. He said that to have correct knowledge of something we must know its cause. In fact he said there were four causes that we need to consider:

  • <u>material</u> cause: “that out of which something arises”, e.g., the paint of a painting;
  • <u>formal</u> cause: e.g. the form, or shape of the painting;
  • <u>efficient</u> cause: primary source of the change, e.g., the artist, the paint brushes, easel, the art tutor or advisor;
  • <u>final</u> cause: “the end, that for the sake of which a thing is done”, e.g., being clean is the end of a bath.

Since Plato and Aristotle, more than 2,300 years ago, there has been much discussion among philosophers about this question. The main issue examined is the extent of evidence needed to claim justified truth and hence knowledge. One issue under debate, in the broad context of epistemology, has been causation; simply meaning what is the process that results in some or other effect. In the frame are horizontal and vertical causation.

Horizontal causation is a two-stage process, from a cause leading to an effect, or outcome. Vertical causation generally adds a third, preceding stage. It examines what is the underlying cause of the cause, the metacause. So, as an example of horizontal causation, rain - the cause, leads to wet ground – the effect; all in just two stages. One example of vertical causation is, a weather system – the metacause, leads to rain – the cause and finally wet ground – the effect; more than two stages. Horizontal and vertical causation is an issue in the philosophy of science.

Metaphysics:

This term comes, yet again, from the ancient Greeks. It simply means beyond or after physics. Specifically, it deals with questions about the nature of reality, e.g. what is existence; what is the cause of existence, sometimes referred to as “being” and “first cause”. But since the ancient Greek philosophers, like the pre-Socratics, the scope of metaphysics has widened. It now includes issues of space and time, e.g. is there such a thing as space, and if so does it have infinite size; does time have a beginning and an end?

Other disciplines also attempt to address questions of reality and existence, namely religion and science. Science, as indicated, takes an approach that relies on propositions that can be proven by rigorous experiment and observation, although science has inched towards greater understanding of the answers to metaphysical questions, it has not yet come up with a complete solution. The Great Theory of Everything remains an elusive goal.

As for religion, there is no end of explanations for the origin of existence. The vast range of religious beliefs give different specific answers but have two common, underlying themes. First, nearly all claim the entirety of existence is, down to an eternal God, or Gods. Second, none can offer unchallengeable proof.

Early Greek pre-Socratic philosophers more than 2,400 years ago first considered metaphysical problems, pondered the origin of all matter, and gave their reasoning for various answers.

Political philosophy:

In western politics for example, philosophers like John Locke, Thomas Paine and Karl Marx have had a huge impact. The former two influence the USA’s Declaration of Independence document and to some extent the British constitutional model. Jean-Jacques Rousseau’s ideas - Liberté, Egalité - were fundamental to the French revolution. The democratic processes inspired by Locke, Paine and Rousseau, with their emphasis on freedom and equality, have been copied throughout the western world.

Marx helped establish the communist system, which is still around, sort of, in China and Cuba. John Adams, the second President of the USA was a great admirer of Cicero’s political philosophy. Other philosophers have had a say about politics and been studied by thoughtful politicians such as: Confucius, Thomas Hobbes, Machiavelli, Bacon, Marx and Hannah Arendt. It’s fair to say political philosophers have had an impact on the lives of hundreds of millions of people right up to the present day.

“War is politics by other means”, said Carl von Clausewitz. This topic may not be something that philosophy can be proud of, i.e. giving philosophical pointers to the best way to conduct war. In war situations, some military strategists have probably paid close attention to what Sun Tzu had to say about 2,000 years ago. Basically, his advice was to try diplomacy first and if that doesn’t work be ruthless and use overwhelming force to intimidate your opponent into submission. Apart from Sun Tzu, others of the great, famous and not-so-famous have pontificated about war. Religious Philosophy:

Theologians over the centuries have sought to use the pontifications of philosophers to help give credence to, and prop up their religion. However, they have to be quite selective in which philosophers they choose to support their case. Lots of philosophers do not believe in a deity, or, like Protagoras, say they do not know whether or not Gods exist. Plato and Aristotle were the fond favourites of many theologians, up to at least the middle ages. Both P and A believed we had a soul, as did their great mentor Socrates. Plato’s imagined place of Perfect Forms has some similarity to the idea of heaven or paradise. Plato also suggested there should be a perfect, non-physical love. This might have led to the Roman Catholic Church insisting its priests be celibate.

Some philosophers could also be described as theologians – Augustine, Anselm, Aquinas and Averroes particularly.

Ethics and Morals:

These topics are not an exclusive religious preserve. They may be taught to children by parents at home or in school.

p.260

Ethics and morals are a major subject for some great philosophers. Ethics comes from the Greek ethos, meaning customs and morals is derived from the Roman moros, also meaning customs.

The Egyptian vizier, Ptah Hotep 4,500 years ago laid down virtuous dictums, focusing on the importance of honesty, openness and, kindness. Some 2,000 years after Ptah the great Socrates was interested in moral issues that affect human lives. In his awkward, questioning way, he would ask a Judge “What is justice.” And a Priest would be faced with “What is piety.”

To lead a good life, Socrates thought, moral integrity was more important than physical well-being. Many philosophers have made clear their thoughts on ethical issues over the centuries since Socrates’ time. They’ve analysed ethics in so many ways. The result is that ethics is split up into several categories. We have:

  • <u>Metaethics</u>, this looks at how humans arrive at moral decisions and considers the origin of morality, i.e. can it exist independently of humans? This is an area that interest’s theologians. They want to know whether God is the origin of moral standards. One aspect of metaethics is known as Moral or Ethical Rationalism. This claims that general moral principles can be known through reason. Plato and Immanuel Kant were of this view. Later, the idea was rejected, partly, by David Hume and Friedrich Nietzsche. In more recent times some philosophers have backed the a priori concept purported by Plato. This form of ethics, whether called metaethics, moral or ethical rationalism, is more of an investigative, pondering approach. It does not immediately propose necessary action.
  • <u>Normative ethics</u>, this is a more practical approach than metaethics. It considers what moral standards or rules we should adopt and how these basic standards should be arrived at It also looks at what our behaviour means for other people. The character excellence sought by Virtue ethics falls under the normative ethics umbrella. This is slightly different to the rules-based approach. Some philosophers think that there are certain defined rules of behaviour that constitutes morality. These behaviours could be basic negatives like “don’t steal” or “don’t commit murder”. However, there are other philosophers who say this approach is too specific and lacks general moral guidance. Aristotle was probably the earliest of this sort. These philosophers support <u>Virtue ethics</u>. For them morality should be broader based and encourage positive good behaviour, like “be kind to others”. If you are being kind to others you won’t kill them or steal from them. See Bernard Mandeville and his argument that might suggest vice can be a virtue;
p.261
  • <u>Applied ethics</u> scrutinises controversial concerns like abortion; animal rights; human rights; capital punishment; homosexual, lesbian and transgender issues. It also looks at nuclear war - a topic on which the long-lived philosopher Bertrand Russell had something to say. He was strongly against nuclear weapons.

Arguably (anything to do with philosophy is always arguable) the above are the main categories under which ethics is considered. But there is more terminology that is sometimes used to group together theories of ethics. For instance, <u>consequentialism</u> – this simply means that the morally correct action is the one that produces the most good. Bentham’s utilitarianism is a form of consequentialism. Then we have <u>deontology.</u> Deontology is nothing to do with teeth, although it does have some bite when it comes to opposing consequentialism. Proponents of deontological ethics say that it’s the action itself that must be morally right, not just the outcome. Kant can be seen as a proponent of this concept.

<u>Aesthetics</u>: This is the second strand of axiology, ethics is the first. Aesthetics is also known as the theory of beauty. It explores culture, nature and art generally.

The First Philosophers

It was Pythagoras, 570 BC – 495 BC, who expounded the term philosophia to refer to great thinkers and questioners as philosophers. Of course, Pythagoras is better known to generations of school children for his mathematical skills associated with right-angled triangles. Pythagoras was not the first Greek philosopher, just the first to call himself by that name.

About 2,600 years ago, in the freewheeling Greek coastal town of Miletus (now in Turkey), a man called Thales had new ideas about the nature of all reality. He dismissed the traditional religious views. These claimed everything came from the Gods; the Greek Gods. For Thales, the often cruel, selfish and plain silly antics of Greek anthropomorphic Gods made them unbelievable.

p.265

Being a mathematician, he applied rigorous thought to what constituted original matter. According to Thales there must be some basic substance that was the first matter ever to exist. He concluded that everything in nature had water, the Arche, as its base material. His reasoning was that water could be either a liquid, solid or vapour (water, ice or steam). Cicero said that Thales believed it was the mind of a supreme being that created all things from water, if this is so he clearly assumed there was some sort of God.

There are two more important things to note about Thales, his freedom and his school. He lived in a society where it was relatively safe, but not wholly so, to articulate non-conformist ideas. At this time Greece had a democratic government and people were allowed greater freedom of speech, especially if they lived some distance from Athens, as Thales did in Miletus. This freedom was quite unusual compared to many other ancient cultures of his time (although maybe not middle-eastern Akkad where the 2,500-year-old clay Cyrus cylinder suggests religious freedom was allowed).

Thales was not exiled, imprisoned or executed for his anti-religious opinions, as he might have been elsewhere than Greece. He founded the first school of philosophy, later called The Milesian School.

The Milesian School of Philosophy What is the base substance of everything?

p.266

Following on from Thales were his pupil Anaximander and then Anaximenes. These two-continued wrestling with the metaphysical problem of what was the base substance from which all matter was derived. For Anaximander, it was not water but something indeterminate, which he called the Apeiron and Anaximenes had yet another opinion, it’s air he said. These three Milesians did not get it right, but their ideas and freethinking example inspired many others after them.

It’s certain that the Milesian school trio got Western philosophy well and truly off the ground. Bertrand Russell says western philosophy began with Thales, but were these the original philosophers on planet Earth? No, they were not. Ptah Hotep, an Egyptian Grand Vizier from some 1,800 years before Thales, deserves that honour. He was the first philosopher that we have evidence of. Ptah wrote ethical philosophical statements. He did not say much though; this may have been because of the limitations posed by needing to have his thoughts chiselled on stone. And, as far as we know, he did not trigger a philosophical movement. Certainly, there is no sure record, on stone or papyrus, of other ancient Egyptian philosophers, although there is a very limited claim made of Imhotep.

But Egypt had an older culture than Greece. We know Thales received some instruction from an Egyptian priest and that he used geometry to measure the height of the pyramids. It’s likely therefore he was fully aware of Egyptian mythology. Perhaps he saw the Egyptian Gods in the same dismissive light as the Greek ones and so may have concluded that the Egyptians, for all their sophisticated culture, did not have the answer to the source of reality. He had to work it out for himself.

Other than Ptah Hotep, with his ethical philosophy, there is no one else known to have preceded Thales. In the far east the Chinese Laozi, founder of Taoism, weighed in just a few years after Thales. In India Siddhartha Gautama (Buddha) and back in China, Confucius came slightly later still. However, we are talking about who was the first philosopher in recorded history. By definition, we cannot know of those sages from pre-recorded, i.e. pre-historic times. There must have been some, like the fictional story of Noth the stone-age theologian/philosopher recounted in Chapter 5. However, without written records of some sort, their real thoughts are lost to us.

1. Ptah, Hotep

2414 to 2375 BCE Egyptian 5th dynasty, Ethics

Some writers on philosophy are a bit snobbish in not recognising Ptah Hotep as the first philosopher that we know of. This is with a passing nod to another ancient Egyptian, Imhotep. He preceded Ptah by a couple of centuries. But it is not quite so clear what it is that Imhotep wrote, of a philosophical nature. He mainly acted as chancellor to the Pharaoh Djoser and was a medical specialist with an interest in architecture. Seemingly he designed the first pyramid. Coming back to Ptah. Here we have someone who was not a Greek, or of western heritage. He too was from Egypt. But, as historian Will Durrant said, Ptah was probably the first philosopher. He’s certainly, the first we have clear, written evidence of.

Most books on the history of philosophy will start with the pre-Socratic Greeks. These amazing people were mainly interested in metaphysical questions about the base substance underpinning everything – us, the whole world and the universe. Modern philosophy scribes normally quote the Greek, Thales of Miletus, as the first philosopher. But philosophy covers a broad field of ideas, not just metaphysics. Certainly, what Ptah had to say to his son comes within the bounds of ethical philosophy, so he should be counted as the first philosopher in recorded history.

Ptah was an Egyptian Grand Vizier of the 5th Dynasty. His main job might have been as a Grand Vizier, but he clearly made lasting philosophical utterings. Ptah gave advice to his son and to all other people after his time, because he had his thoughts inscribed on enduring stone. He said his was the wisdom of ancestors and that they got their wisdom from Gods. Taken from the Prisse Papyrus, this is some of the advice he gave: “Do not gossip in your neighbourhood, because people respect the silent.”; “Listening benefits the listener.”; “…he who listens becomes he who understands.”; “A perfect word is hidden more deeply than precious stones. It is to be found near the servants working at the mill-stone.”; “Only speak when you have something worth saying.”; “A woman with a happy heart brings equilibrium.”; “May your heart never be vain because of what you know. Take counsel from the ignorant as well as the wise…”;

p.271

“Think of living in peace with what you possess, and whatever the Gods choose to give will come of its own accord.” and “Do not repeat a slanderous rumour, do not listen to it.”. Ptah did not appreciate getting old: “Old age has struck, age has descended, feebleness has arrived, weakness is here again. Sleep is upon him in discomfort all day. Eyes are grown small, ears deaf, mouth silent, unable to speak, heart emptied, unable to recall yesterday, bones ache his whole length…”

Apart from his grumblings about the effect of old age, what he wrote on stone, 4,500 years ago, has moral, ethical and governmental implications. In essence, he said that certain things were most important: truthfulness; self-control; avoidance of dispute; openness; kindness and justice. His fatherly guidance could easily have been read, interpreted and re-written in a slightly different style by the human writer of the biblical ten commandments. Who knows, the biblical scribe might have plagiarised and adapted Ptah.

Good on yer Ptah Hotep. Your advice should be taught in all schools today. If followed it would result in a kinder, more tolerant and peaceful world.

2. Thales of Miletus (in modern day Turkey)

624 to 546 BCE Metaphysics, WATER, pre-Socratic, Milesian

Bertrand Russell claimed: “Western philosophy begins with Thales.” He was a businessman who also dabbled in philosophy, astronomy, mathematics and politics. In the Archaic period when Thales lived, Greek city-states were the norm. His home, Miletus, is now Milet in modern Turkey but at the time of Thales Miletus was part of Ionian Greece. Exactly what he wrote is debated, Diogenes Laertius, some 800 years after Thales, said: “according to others [Thales] wrote nothing but two treatises, one On the Solstice and one On the Equinox.” It seems he took an interest in navigation as well as philosophy. His main metaphysical idea was that water was the basis for all living things. This is an amazingly accurate idea. We now know that water is needed to start life on planet Earth. It’s possibly an essential for life on other planets too. Thales cryptically explained his idea of the fundamental nature of water: “If there is a change, there must be something that changes, yet does not change.”

p.272

And he was a great believer that eventually, given enough time, all will be known: “Time is the wisest of all things that are; for it brings everything to light.”

Thales is quoted as saying: “All things are full of Gods.” He did not explain what he meant. It could be that he believed in Gods, alternatively it might be a complaint. He knew that many occurrences could be explained without the need to resort to Gods. Known as one of the Seven Sages of Greece by Diogenes Laertus and Plato,

Thales scored several of “firsts”: • the first known Greek philosopher; the first that we know of anyway; first to question accepted mythical and religious explanations for the origin of everything. Here he was immediately followed by two others of the Milesian hotbed of early philosophy, Anaximander and Anaximenes. Thales example was taken up later by other great Greek thinkers. They firmly established philosophy as a discipline and science. Thales is sometimes also referred to as the father of science although this claim is more usually made of Democritus.

3. Anaximander

610 to 546 BCE Metaphysics, pre-Socratic, BOUNDLESS, Monism, Milesian

Anaximander like Thales lived in Miletus. He was a student of Thales but did not agree with his teacher’s view that water was the basis of all matter, although he did say the first living creatures were born in moisture. He attempted to formulate a view of the cosmos that was not based on mythology and he is sometimes known as the father of astronomy. Anaximander also wrote on geography and biology. The first map of the world was drawn by Anaximander; understandably not very accurate given the lack of geographical knowledge in his time. For Anaximander, the Earth was flat and shaped like a drum.

It is also claimed he was the first person to write a book of prose. Another first for him was his claim that the Earth floated in space. However, his biggest metaphysical idea was that the universe had no boundaries and held an infinite number of worlds. He said: “The Apeiron is neither water nor any other one of the things called elements, but the infinite is something of a different nature, from which came all the heavens and the worlds in them.”

p.273

The Apeiron translates as the indefinite or infinite. He also thought that man originated from an animal, perhaps a fish. Well, he almost got two out of three if allowing the vast universe with its 100 billion galaxies each averaging a 100 billion suns is to all intents boundless. Not bad for someone who lived 2,600 years ago in a time when unproven mythology was in place of science.

4. Lao-Tzu or Laozi

600 to ? BCE Chinese, Taoism (also known as Daoism)

Taoism is a form or ethical philosophy. It tells people how they should behave and live their lives. Laozi probably did not originate Taoism but he certainly gave it a huge boost, when he disputably wrote “Tao Te Ching” or the “Daode Jing” (The Way and Its Power). This is a fundamental Taoist text that has been intrinsic to Chinese culture, philosophical and religious thought. It influenced both Confucianism and Chinese Buddhism. It’s even been used by poets, artists and gardeners. The essence of this ancient text is that the Dao is the source of all existence and the base of everything. The aim of Taoism is to achieve harmony with the Dao through meditation and thoughtful action.

Taoism, sometimes known as Daoism, says that nature is supremely important. Lao Tzu thought humans did harm to the harmony of the natural world and that knowledge was worthless. This tradition says that 10,000 manifestations make up the world, with humans just one of these. Taoism is individualistic and mystical it suggests all things are formless with no identifiable beginning or end. It generally puts stress on the need for compassion, humility and serenity - the Three Treasures. Taoism advises acting in accordance with nature, without planning, ambition or desire. Laozi stressed the importance of truly understanding oneself and the viewpoint of other people. He said: “Knowing others is intelligence, knowing yourself is true wisdom.” He also gave specific advice associated with his Three Treasures. This is somewhat similar to Ptah Hotep’ guidance to his son 1700 years earlier: “Simplicity, patience, compassion. These three are your greatest treasures. Simple in actions and thoughts, you return to the source of being. Patient with both friends and enemies, you accord with the way things are. Compassionate toward yourself, you reconcile all beings in the world.” Taoism encourages adherents to be vegetarians. It is mostly practised today in China, where 173 million are said to follow Taoist practices. Other followers are mainly in Korea, Japan and Vietnam.

5. Anaximenes

585 to 528 BCE Metaphysics, pre-Socratic, AIR, Monism, Milesian

Anaximenes was the third member of the Milesian school of philosophy, after Thales and Anaximander. Like the other two, he was interested in the origin of the world. He differed from both Thales, who thought water was the fundamental substance of all creation, and he did not like what his mentor, Anaximander claimed. In the latter’s view, there was an infinite number of substances. Not for Anaximenes though. His thought was that everything came from air. The soul is air, fire is rarefied air, when condensed, air becomes first water, then if further condensed, earth, and finally stone. Consequently, all differences between different substances are quantitative, depending entirely upon the degree of condensation, so argued Anaximenes. His foray into cosmology was much, much further from the truth than the attempts of Anaximander. Extrapolating from his theme that air was the base of everything Anaximenes said the Earth was a flat disc that floated in space and was supported by air. We can give him one out of three. We do float in space, sort of. Nice try though Anaximenes, you lacked the astronomical and other instrumentation we have had over the last several hundred years, otherwise you would have got it right. He clearly had some moral conscience saying: “To what purpose should I trouble myself in searching out the secrets of the stars, having death or slavery continually before my eyes.”

6. Xenophanes

570 to 475 BCE Eleatic, Monism

He could be the philosopher most admired by today’s travel and tourism industry. Xenophanes spent 67 years of his life travelling throughout the Grecian world. Both Plato and Aristotle said he was the founder of the Eleatic school of philosophy although this claim is also made of the later Parmenides. The Eleatics thought there was one true supreme Being and that knowledge obtained via the senses was illusory, as was motion. Xenophanes was both a poet and philosopher. His comments cover a wide range: knowledge, religion and the natural world. He was critical of the unpredictability, immorality and anthropomorphic nature of the Greek Gods.

p.275

Quotes: “Men create the gods in their own image.” and “Ethiopians say that their gods are snub-nosed and black and Thracians that theirs have blue eyes and red hair.” "Homer and Hesiod have attributed to the gods everything that is blameworthy and disgraceful among human’s; theft and adultery and mutual trickery.”

Xenophanes might be the first monotheist because he said: “There is one God - supreme among gods and men - who is like mortals in neither body nor mind.” Xenophanes concluded the one God was a source of divine goodness. In his epistemological comments, he said all dogmatic claims of particular knowledge, especially religious ones, should be viewed sceptically. A clever chap, he also studied fossils and this led him to believe that water and earth were the basic constituents of all nature. Perhaps he had in mind what Thales said about the role of water and the views of other pre-Socratics who argued in favour of various basic constituents of all matter.

7. Pythagoras of Samos

570 to 495 BCE Metaphysics, pre-Socratic, NUMBERS

A great ancient Greek mathematician - every schoolboy knows, because of Pythagoras, that the square of the hypotenuse is equal to the sum of the squares on the other two sides. He claimed that we could understand the cosmos though numbers. Pythagoras said everything was based on numbers, in some form of perfect symmetry. His discovery of odd numbers like Pi and square roots put the boot into his own beliefs. It’s claimed he drowned a student, poor Hipparsus of Tarentum. This student had broadcast the existence of irrational numbers, thus disavowing the perfect arithmetical world claimed by his master. Hence, foolish Hipparsus was dispatched to a watery grave.

Pythagoras studied the Moon and concluded it was round and ergo, he thought, the Earth must also be round. He was the first we know of to suggest a circular Earth. He said the soul resides in the brain, and is immortal. On reincarnation, it moves to another human or sometimes from a human into an animal. The process of transmigration continued until it becomes pure, so said Pythagoras. This is Buddhist like thinking, although Pythagoras preceded Gautama. Pythagoras forbid the eating of meat. His loyal followers became the first vegetarians we know of. They also engaged in superstitious practices, including a refusal to eat beans.

p.276

The reason for the latter is not known; perhaps it’s connected to beans’ gaseous quality and possibly lack of stringless varieties? His philosophy with its element of mysticism became a cult for centuries. Pythagoras made some eminently quotable sayings: “Reason is immortal, all else mortal” and “There is geometry in the music of the strings, …” He also proposed that the movement of heavenly bodies created music, hence much later the “Music of the Spheres” concept. Was he unknowingly a very early proponent of String Theory?

He also believed in the importance of friendship: " Friends are as companions on a journey, who ought to aid each other to persevere the road to a happier life."

His comments contrasting men and women would not make him a favourite of those seeking equality between the sexes: “There is a good principle which created order, light and man, and an evil principle which created chaos, darkness and woman.” Pythagoras’s number would definitely be up if he were to be transported forward in time to meet people like Mary Wollstonecraft and Emily Pankhurst.

8. Gautama, Siddhartha (later known as Buddha)

563 to 483 BCE Indian, Ethics, Buddhism

Gautama was the founder of Buddhism; in fact, he was the original Buddha (the awakened one). Buddhism might be bracketed with religions, after all it was made the state religion in India in the 3rd century BCE, but its main tenets are philosophically based. They are associated with ethical philosophy.

In essence, Buddhism says the problems of life are caused through suffering. In itself suffering, so Buddhism says, is generated by desire. Therefore, when desire is conquered suffering ends. How to achieve this? Well, Buddhism says it’s through a process of obtaining wisdom, meditating and foregoing of earthly pleasures. Gautama was born to a wealthy family. He had a wife and a son. None of the pleasures that this privileged, family situation brought him were enough to bring him true happiness. He was acutely aware he could suffer disease and death, just like everyone else.

p.277

Gautama is famously believed to have attained enlightenment after meditating for 49 days under a Banyan tree. Now this is known as the Bodhi Tree - in Bodh Gaya, India. For a week after becoming enlightened, Gautama remained sitting under the Bodhi Tree, enjoying the happiness of freedom and peace, also one would think, suffering with a very sore bum and being a bit thirsty. He decided to give up his way of life and develop a philosophy and means of living that sought to detach himself from earthly desires.

Some people could criticise Gautama for abandoning his wife and son. It is doubtful that his family were helped on the road to happiness by Gautama’s action. Gautama, apparently, wandered the land preaching his thoughts and living on hand-outs from people. He challenged the established religious Hinduism of his time which was based on Vedism, from the sacred Veda texts. He concentrated on what had to be done to achieve happiness, virtue, and how to conduct a good life. His defining manner epitomised calmness and serenity. His reasoning concluded in a method he developed that could move people from suffering to happiness. Firstly, the Four Noble Truths were identified. These are:

  1. that suffering is universal;
  2. that desire is the cause of suffering;
  3. that suffering can be avoided by eliminating desire;
  4. the Eightfold path is the way to follow to eliminate desire.

Then he defined The Eightfold Path. key points are:

  1. Right Mindfulness;
  2. Right Action;
  3. Right Intention;
  4. Right Livelihood;
  5. Right Effort;
  6. Right Consciousness;
  7. Right Speech;
  8. Right Understanding.

Basically, Gautama said, avoid egotistical thoughts or actions, seek wisdom - he called it enlightenment. If you work hard at it you eventually achieve a state of Nirvana, or non-self. This may take several re-incarnations (re-incarnation is an essential feature of Buddhism).

The Buddhist road to Nirvana is influenced by his karma. Karma means wilful action. Past actions, or karma, influence the present and future while present

p.278

actions influence the future. Interestingly, although the state of Nirvana is similar to the extreme holiness achieved in the Christian heaven, Gautama avoids mention of God or Gods.

What we know of Gautama and his teachings comes from the Tipitaka (known as the three baskets which include the Sutta-pitaka). This was written 400 years after his death. In fact, it was written before the birth of Christ. Such a huge gap in time suggests there can be question marks over its accuracy. It’s debateable whether the oral tradition could result in a perfect re-telling after four centuries.

Another curious issue is that although Gautama stressed the equality of all men his followers have not treated him as an equal. Some revere him and give him godlike status. One can speculate that Gautama’s focus on wisdom, as claimed by the author of the Tipitaka, may have been influenced by the writings of Plato. Plato’s pupil was Aristotle. He was the tutor of Alexander the Great. Alexander’s empire stretched from the Adriatic Sea to the Indus River by about 330 BCE. It’s possible that via Aristotle, Plato’s dialogues reached India well before the Tipitaka was written. Both Socrates (whom Plato wrote of) and Plato thought the pursuit of wisdom was an important virtue just as it is in Buddhism. Who knows, perhaps the Buddha’s ideas, or those of his scribe, were influenced in this way.

Today there are nearly 400 million Buddhists. They are a member of one or other different sects. Theravada is biggest with most supporters in Cambodia, Sri Lanka, Thailand, Laos and Burma. Next up is Mahayana, this is popular a bit further east in China, Mongolia, Taiwan, Japan and Korea. Then there is Nichiren which, unlike other sects, seeks to gain converts. There’s also Tendai, Lamaism – Tibetan, and Zen, which is popular in the USA.

The best-known Buddhist leader is the Dalai Lama of Tibet. The current one (as at November 2017) has quite a pragmatic world view saying: “People take different roads seeking fulfilment and happiness. Just because they are not on your road does not mean they have gotten lost." and “It is unrealistic to think the future of humanity can be achieved only on the basis of prayer, what we need is to take action.” If only everyone followed these two dictums.

9. Confucius or Kong Fuzi (master Kong)

551 to 479 BCE Chinese, Ethical, Confucianism

Confucianism has had a huge impact on China, arguably even up to the present day. His was a system of social, political and personal ethics that stressed family, loyalty and righteous ness. His beliefs were a basis for good government. He did not deal in good and evil. Nor did he promote a God or Gods. Confucius philosophy was intended to emphasise the importance of living a virtuous life. He was a great believer in looking to the past for inspiration. Ancestors were important as was ritualisation.

His path to virtue was through faithfulness and sincerity. He believed these two qualities could be achieved partly by the use of appropriate rituals and ceremonies. He thought this type of morality had nothing to do with God. He said: “The superior man does what is proper to his station in which he is; he does not go beyond this.” This emphasis on sticking to the status quo is anti-revolutionary. It keeps people quietly in their place - very helpful to governments who want a compliant populace. The lack of ambition so proposed has some similarities with the ideas of Greek and Roman stoicism although quite at odds with the contemporary development of democracy in Greece. He is reputed to have many other wise and noble sayings. Here are a few: in forays into epistemology: “Real knowledge is to know the extent of one’s ignorance.”; “By three methods we may learn wisdom: First, by reflection, which is noblest; Second, by imitation, which is easiest; and third by experience, which is the bitterest." This interest in knowledge and learning contrasts sharply with the Taoist teachings of his Chinese predecessor Lao-Tzu who thought knowledge was of no value. and in terms that Wittgenstein might appreciate said: “when language has no object nothing can be done properly… Where language is concerned, precision is of the utmost importance.” Probably the best-known ethical Confucianism is the Golden Rule, which may or may not have originated from Confucius:

Golden Rule Do unto others as you would have them done to you

10. Sun Tzu

544 to 496 BCE Chinese, Political

Sun Tzu or Changqing lived in what is known as the Spring and Autumn period of ancient Chinese history alternatively called the Warring States period. He wrote “The Art of War”, a philosophical, strategic and tactical treatise on how to conduct a war. It also stresses the need for diplomacy and good relationships with other nations. This text has been used by politicians and in business. Sun Tzu said: “All warfare is based on deception. Hence, when we are able to attack, we must seem unable; when using our forces, we must appear inactive; when we are near, we must make the enemy believe we are far away; when far away, we must make him believe we are near.” and “The supreme art of war is to subdue the enemy without fighting." In other words, Sun Tzu said try politics first, war should be a last resort. But Tzu was a very tough cookie and prepared to be utterly ruthless if diplomacy failed.

One story illustrates Sun Tzu’s cold-blooded logic. He was about to be employed by the King of Wu. The king wanted to test Sun Tzu’s practical skills of command. He asked him to train his harem of 180 concubines. Sun divided them up and appointed the king’s two favourite concubines as commanders. The concubines giggled when he gave them commands, so he had the two favourite concubines executed on the spot; to the king’s dismay. Then he appointed new commanders. This time, when he repeated his original orders, they were carried out flawlessly. Sun Tzu probably laid down the path for others, like Machiavelli, to follow.

11. Heraclitus

535 to 475 BCE Metaphysics, pre-Socratic, FIRE and FLOW Monism

Heraclitus was another of the pre-Socratic Greek philosophers who lived in Ephesus (close to modern Kuşadası, Turkey). Perhaps Turkey ought to claim ownership of Heraclitus and other ancient Greeks philosophers who lived in what is now Turkish territory. Sometimes Heraclitus is known as the weeping philosopher, in contrast to the laughing

p.281

It maybe Heraclitus was easily moved to tears, or perhaps the weeping tag flows from his river analogy. The fundamental driving force of the cosmos was divine logos, according to Heraclitus. Logos, or reason, was a universal law. through which everything came into being and by which the elements of the universe were kept in balance. Heraclitus thought that fire is the original element. He believed fire to be the origin of all matter. Well, he could not choose either water or air if he wished to be original because these ideas were the properties of Thales and Anaximenes, his predecessors. Everything flows he said. “You cannot step into the same river twice, for fresh waters are ever flowing in upon you.” With this concept of constant change Heraclitus held an opposite view to the Milesians Thales and Anaximenes as well as Parmenides. For Heraclitus reality is just a number of changing positions; completely the opposite to yet another Greek philosopher, his contemporary Xenophanes. The latter was renowned for travelling throughout Greece and elsewhere so the two philosophers probably knew each other. It would have been interesting to eavesdrop on their discussions. Heraclitus told people that nothing is the same now as it was before, and so nothing what is today will be the same tomorrow. Some people will think this is stating the blindingly obvious. However, with this concept he planted the idea of impermanence into Greek thought. After Heraclitus, Greek philosophy was not the same anymore.

Like Anaximander, Heraclitus sees a cosmic balance in the struggle of the elements, water, air, fire, earth. Due to the eternal transmutation of forms, which are made of the elements, no single element ever gains predominance. Heraclitus thinks of fire as a non-destructive; but merely transforming power. The process of transformation does not happen by chance, but is, according to Heraclitus, the product of God’s reason which he called logos. When Heraclitus speaks of God, he doesn’t mean the many Greek gods, neither does he refer to a personal entity. Instead he thinks that God exists in every soul and even in every material thing on earth. God is everywhere, according to Heraclitus. The fiery element is the expression of God in everything, thus he is in every sense a pantheist and a helpful referee by later Christian and other Abrahamic theologians. He had a respectful view of war: “War is father of all, king of all. Some it makes gods, some it makes men, some it makes slaves, some free.” And: “We must realize that war is universal, and strife is justice, and that all things come into being and pass away through strife.”

Friedrich Nietzsche was somewhat attracted to Heraclitus’ views, although for him the God issue had moved on a bit in the 2,300 years since the Ephesus great.

12. Parmenides of Elea

515 to 450 BCE pre-Socratic, Eleatic, Monism, Metaphysics

Parmenides was another in the frame for the title of the most important pre-Socratic philosophers. He’s considered to be the founder of metaphysics. Others before him made metaphysical statements but perhaps in not quite so analytical a fashion as Parmenides. He was admired for his willingness to consider the seemingly implausible and for his rigorous deductive analysis. He influenced Socrates, Aristotle and Plato especially. Later on Spinoza, Nietzsche, and Heidegger took note of what he said. He wrote a poem with a famous section "The Way of Truth”. His main ideas were that everything was part of one unchanging whole, with no beginning and no end, just an eternal Being. But this was not a concept of a God, just a material, eternal and unchanging Being. This was the opposite of Heraclitus. Apparent movement and change were, he thought, just illusions. Past, present and future were all one, so Parmenides said and that there was no such thing as a void. Zeno was a supportive student.

Interestingly Parmenides’ ideas on time - that everything is now - coincides to some degree with that of the great 20th century scientist Einstein. The latter showed that space/time was linked and that time could be warped.

13. Anaxagoras

500 to 428 BCE pre-Socratic, Atomist, Pluralism

Anaxagoras was a pre-Socratic Greek from the city of Clazomenae, now Izmir in Turkey. In 462 BCE, he moved to the cultural hub that was Athens; becoming the first philosopher to take residence there. Here he influenced the playwright Euripides and the great statesman, orator and general, Pericles. Anaxagoras’ great idea was that all substances contained a portion of everything else and so everything was made up of lots of very small things. These small things he thought were eternal. They mixed then separated and mixed again. These minute substances never came into being and never died. They were always there, forever. The concept of something eternal has close correlation with his predecessor, Anaximander’s Apeiron. He also gave a strong hint to the later Democritus which might have led to Democritus’ atomist ideas.

Anaxagoras’ non-religious views on origin of the cosmos got him into trouble with the authorities who were keen on promoting orthodox religious explanations. Eventually he was sentenced to death in Athens for claiming the sun was a flaming rock. He said: “Everything has a natural explanation. The moon is not a god but a great rock and the sun a hot rock.” Not a bad surmise for someone who did not have the benefit of a telescope or any other scientific instrument some 2,500 years ago. He had other interesting ideas which had relevance more than 2,000 years later. Anaxagoras postulated an all-encompassing element which he called the aether. This, he said, was in constant motion and within it existed all the celestial bodies. He did not refer directly to a God but he did think that there was a directing natural intelligence. He called this Nous (mind). Nous, he thought, ensured order instead of chaos in the universe. Nous he meant as “the Mind of the Cosmos”. This he thought is infinite and absolute; possessing perfect knowledge, exerting the greatest power over everything. Nous dominates all living things, from the smallest to the largest.

As the original Panspermian, Anaxagoras predicted other worlds contained the seeds of life. In 1973, 2,200 years after Anaxagoras Francis Crick and Leslie Orgel won the Nobel prize for their theory of the intentional spreading of the seeds of life to other planets by super-intelligent extra-terrestrials. By rights Anaxagoras should be the philosopher of choice for today’s Seti investigators.

14. Empedocles

490 to 430 BCE pre-Socratic, FOUR ELEMENTS, Pluralism

A Greek who lived in Sicily (in his time Sicily was part of Greece). Like other pre-Socratics Empedocles tried to determine what was the source for all matter. He concluded that all matter was based on four elements: earth, air, fire and water. Sounds a bit familiar? Yes, others before him have paddled in similar seas although not entirely the same: for Thales the base was water; Anaximander – infinite substances; Anaximenes – air. Empedocles took a slightly different tack. Fire and air were outwardly reaching elements, reaching up and out, whereas earth and water turned inward and down. For him the interaction between these four (fire, air, earth and water) was influenced by the two basic energies of love and strife: “But come, hear my words, for truly learning causes the mind to grow. For as I said before in declaring the ends of my words: Twofold is the truth I shall speak; for at one time there grew to be the one alone out of many, and at another time it separated so that there were many out of the one; fire and water and earth and boundless height of air, and baneful Strife apart from these, balancing each of them, and Love among them, their equal in length and breadth.”

In his “Tetrasomia”, or “Doctrine of the Four Elements”, Empedocles described these elements not only as physical manifestations or material substances, but also as spiritual essences. He associated these elements with four Greek gods and goddesses - air with Zeus, earth with Hera, fire with Hades, and water with Nestis (the safe, alternate name for Persephone, Queen of the Dead, whose name was taboo). Empedocles, we now know was wrong. Modern science tells us that his 4 substances are not elemental. We understand there are more. But he had the correct basic ideas in that there are fundamental substances with forces interacting with them. We have, so far, identified 12 matter particles and 4 force particles in the Standard Model of particle physics (with a nod to the Higgs boson and the elusive graviton among others). As with other Greek philosophers, to some extent Empedocles’ basic ideas were given a sounder scientific basis in modern times. However, forces like the strong and weak nuclear force can only be extremely imaginatively linked to Empedocles’ forces of love and strife.

On balance though Empedocles thinking was not bad for a man who lacked a telescope, electron microscope and a Large Hadron Collider, among other scientific instruments. If he really intended to suggest a multiverse this is an astonishing perception.

15. Zeno of Elea

490 to 430 BCE pre-Socratic, Eleatic, MOTION AN ILLUSION, Monism

Zeno was born and died in Elea, what is now southern Italy. This Zeno is not to be confused with his namesake who came from Citium. The best information we have about Zeno of Elea comes from Plato’s work called Parmenides. He is renowned for his paradoxes which were intended to support his mentor and friend, Parmenides. Zeno wrote forty paradoxes. In his paradoxes he tried to demonstrate that motion is an illusion. The two best known are: Achilles and the tortoise, claiming that Achilles can never catch the tortoise in a race because the tortoise has always moved forward slightly by the time Achilles reaches it. In ‘The Arrow Paradox’ Zeno said the arrow always occupies a position in space at every given moment therefore does not move when fired. Zeno said: “The truth is, that these writings of mine were meant to protect the arguments of Parmenides against those who make fun of him.” Even the great Aristotle was impressed by Zeno’s paradox of motion. Zeno also turned his thinking to how humans should live their lives. He thought we should keep in tune with the natural world: “The goal of life is living in agreement with nature.” Poor Zeno had unhappy dealings with several tyrants, although this is not clear. He seriously fell out with the local tyrant, Nearchus and bravely refused to recant. He stoically faced his death and was fatally pounded into non-existence, but not before he had bitten off the tyrant’s ear.

16. Protagoras

490 to 420 BCE SUBJECTIVE JUDGEMENT, Sophism

A great travelling Greek teacher and orator. Unlike many of his predecessor Greek philosophers Protagoras was less interested in metaphysical issues of reality. He wanted to put humans centre stage. He said that human judgement was subjective and therefore impacted on how humans understand the world. What he meant was that there are only opinions, which can vary. There is no absolute truth.

Viewpoints depended on different perspectives. For example, a white skinned northern European may say that an Arab was dark skinned. Someone from western Africa would say Arabs were light skinned. Both descriptions are true.

p.286

Protagoras said: “Man is the measure of all things.” This idea was taken up by Michel Foucault some 2,400 years later.

“Concerning the Gods, I have no means of knowing whether they exist or not or of what sort they may be. Many things prevent knowledge including the obscurity of the subject and the brevity of human life.”

17. Socrates

470 to 399 BCE Epistemology, Rationalism

Socrates was born in Athens to very ordinary parents. His father was a stonemason and his mother a midwife. Later he married Xantippe, who sold vegetables and was frequently irritated by her husband’s vagueness.

The great Socrates lived in Athens at a time when Greek Gods were still largely thought to have the answers to every cosmological or ethical question. Socrates gave a huge boost to Western philosophy, partly because his probing, questioning approach can be applied to any area of philosophy. On top of this he was also mentor to the immensely influential Plato, and Plato tutored the great Aristotle. Here we have a terrific, closely linked, philosophical triumvirate. Cicero said Socrates was: “the first to call philosophy down from the heavens.” The Oracle of Delphi concluded there was no one wiser than Socrates. Socrates wrote nothing himself, as far as is known. What we know of his philosophy comes a little from the writings of Xenophon but mostly from Plato, through his dialogues, especially Republic, Crito and the Apology. Plato clearly had great respect and admiration for Socrates. He saw him as a brilliant, convivial and good-humoured man.

He differed from what are known as the pre-Socratic philosophers. These were mostly interested in the origin and composition of all things. Socrates looked at how people should conduct their lives. He scorned the Greek Gods and was an advocate of leading a moral and just life. He thought wisdom led to virtue and that seeking after truth was critically important. Said (according to Plato): “The unexamined life is not worth living. “and “I know nothing except the fact of my ignorance." This was the basis of his unique, for the time, method of argument through rational discussion. His form of dialogue is known as dialectical method. Question everything was Socrates approach. He compared himself to a horsefly whose duty was to wake up Athens from the influence of materialism and the dodgy Sophists.

p.287

He was a brilliant debater and able to tie up any opponent in knots. Living with someone like Socrates must have been difficult. Xantippe maybe found it so, if Socrates did indeed say this: “By all means marry; if you get a good wife, you’ll become happy; if you get a bad one, you’ll become a philosopher.”

Socrates thought well of his fellow man saying that evil or wrong doing was simply because of a lack of knowledge. In his view, all wrong-doing was because of ignorance, and that no one who is aware of right and wrong will choose what is wrong, to do so would be to engage in deliberate self-harm. According to Plato he said: “There is only one good, knowledge, and one evil, ignorance.” In Plato’s dialogue between Socrates and Meno, Socrates says there is a relationship between knowledge and opinion. He ends up claiming that true knowledge equals correct opinion and truth.

Charismatic and ethical he had an absolute integrity. Unfortunately, he was not careful enough in his choice of friends. Critias led the 30 tyrants who conducted a campaign of murder in Athens to retain their power and influence. Socrates had some association with Critias. Eventually the 30 tyrants were overthrown. This cost Socrates his life when he was condemned to death on a trumped-up charge of corrupting the youth of Athens. He tried to console his friends when on his death bed, after he taken hemlock, by saying this was not the end. He believed he had a soul which would survive bodily death, although he made clear that he did not believe in the Greek Gods.

18. Democritus

460 to 370 BCE Atomist

Democritus was born in Abdera. This was a town in Thrace, northern Greece. Democritus was a cheerful chap who lived to the very ripe old age of 90 (some say he was over 100 when he died). According to Diogenes Laertius, Democritus was interested in and wrote on many fields. These included ethics, cosmology, physics, mathematics, and even music. It’s claimed he was instructed in astronomy and theology by Magi (wise men).

These were provided by the Persian Emperor Xerxes in thanks for the welcome provided by Democritus’ wealthy father when the Emperor passed through Abdera. Democritus is known as the laughing philosopher and used irony and ridicule to support his arguments. His mentor was

p.288

Leucippus. These two did not accept the hypothesis of the Eleatics, i.e. Parmenides and Zeno principally, that everything was one Being. Maybe expanding on the ideas of Anaxagoras Democritus and Leucippus proposed that if you could keep paring away at a substance eventually you would reach something that could not be further divided. This something they called the atom. The atom would be the smallest indivisible particle of matter, nothing smaller could be made than an atom. It was Democritus who did the heaviest thinking here, so he gets most of the credit. The atomic idea was an astonishingly accurate thought, two thousand four hundred years before the scientific proof of an atom by Rutherford and Bohr. Of course, the atom is now known not to be the smallest particle of matter. Democritus though, and other ancient Greeks had the first concept that there would be some particle so small it could not be further divided. Now in particle physics, there is a continuing hunt for what is known as fundamental particles. He is quoted as saying: "The first principles of the universe are atoms and empty space; everything else is merely thought to exist. " Getting ahead, into Bishop Berkeley’s territory here.

19. Antisthenes

446 to 366 BCE First Cynic, Socratic ethics

This ancient Greek, was a warrior, then a student and a finally a teacher and writer of philosophy, focusing on Socratics, to which he added some ideas of his own. Antisthenes fought at the internecine dispute between Athens and Thebes/Tanagra in 425 BCE .He was just a young man then, aged 21. Much later he was fighting again, this time when Thebes and Sparta warred at the battle of Leuctra (371 BCE). In bewteen these two he adopted a simple lifestyle. This attracted poorer people to his school, ‘gymnasium’ located just outside Athens near the Temple of Heracles. He regularly wore a cloak and carried a staff and a bag. His symbolic attire was adopted by many of his followers. His devotion to Socrates wsaw him focus of the Socratic tenets of the key importance of virtue and dialectic questionioning – accept nothing at face value.

For Antisthenes virtue, not pleasure was most important. In his work “Lives of Eminent Philosophers” Diogenes Laertius told of Antisthenes stress on the importance of virtue: “He would prove that virtue can be taught; and that nobility belongs to none other than the virtuous. And he held virtue to be sufficient in itself to ensure happiness, since it needed nothing else except the

p.289

strength of a Socrates.”, and “…the wise man will be guided in his public acts not by the established laws but by the law of virtue…”

Antisthenes was a brilliant man with talents as an incisive thinker, orator, teacher of all (Diogenes of Sinope was one of his students) and author, (he wrote sixty texts). His attachment to Socrates, although they might have had a dispute or two, was shown by his attendance at his death, according to Plato. Antisthenes, following Socrates lead, was clearly suspicious of many apparent certainties. Because of this Diogenes Laertius regarded him as the first Cynic philosopher. He did not know this of course. If he did, would he see himself as an out and out cynic? His actions and focus on virtue do not make him wholly cynical in outlook

20. Xenophon

430 to 354 BCE Socratic, Political

He admired Socrates and recorded some of his sayings. Xenophon was a great soldier and a historian as well as a philosopher. He argued that the order in the world must have been conceived by Gods, a sort of early Creationist concept, and that animals were produced solely for human use. One of many quotes from his writings: “…men unite against none so readily as against those whom they see attempting to rule over them.” Although a soldier experienced in war, he recognised negotiation and persuasion was best: "It is only for those to employ force who possess strength without judgment; but the well advised will have recourse to other means.

Besides, he who pretends to carry his point by force hath need of many associates; but the man who can persuade knows that he is himself sufficient for the purpose." Xenophon was a soldier/philosopher like Sun Tzu; his Chinese predecessor.

Just as Sun Tzu advised, Xenophon also advocated the use of diplomacy before resorting to force.

21. Plato or Aristocles

427 to 347 BCE Socratic, Platonism

Plato was the nickname given to Aristocles. It meant broad, possibly because of his wide shoulders and wrestling ability. Whatever you call him he has always been counted as one of the greatest and most influential philosophers of all time. Philosophy writing and teaching is the career path he chose after failing to make the big time as a wrestler and poet. Plato had a dialectical approach to every philosophical question. This meant he delved into an issue by considering opposing, contradictory, sides. Though this synthesis he eliminated seemingly reasonable propositions and either arrived closer to a truth, or was able to ask a more pertinent question.

Plato raised a huge number of philosophical issues, which may be why the philosopher and mathematician A.N. Whitehead said that the whole of western philosophy was a series of footnotes to Plato saying in Process and Reality (1979): “The safest general characterization of the European philosophical tradition is that it consists of a series of footnotes to Plato. I do not mean the systematic scheme of thought which scholars have doubtfully extracted from his writings. I allude to the wealth of general ideas scattered through them. His personal endowments, his wide opportunities for experience at a great period of civilization, his inheritance of an intellectual tradition not yet stiffened by excessive systematization, have made his writing an inexhaustible mine of suggestion.”

Socrates was his much-admired mentor, another of the greats. Plato wrote a series of dialogues, after the fashion of Socrates analytic, verbal questioning approach. This was a method which presents philosophical beliefs through a supposed conversation between two people. There are quite a number of Plato dialogues. He is known as the supreme exemplar of this technique, with his greatest dialogue called “Republic”. In the Republic Plato explores the nature of justice, politics and epistemology. Some of his dialogues record the philosophical views of Socrates. These are sometimes known as the Socratic dialogues.

Plato himself, i.e. without any influence by Socrates, considered that there must be some unchanging perfect form of everything that exists, both material and immaterial objects and concepts. For instance, Plato would say there is a

p.291

perfect form of a deer and of the colour blue and the concept of justice. These Perfect Forms are the true reality he said. He imagined some sort of God. He called It the Demiurge. This Demiurge fashioned the raw matter, which was in a state of chaos, to make the matter of the universe as Plato understood it. There was no concept of galaxies, quasars, black holes etc in Plato’s time.

The world we live in is illusory, containing imperfect copies of the Ideal Forms, according to Plato. His Theory of Perfect Forms appears early in his dialogues. Interestingly, at a later stage he himself criticised the concept. But the idea carried on particularly into medieval times. The Perfect Forms could be seen by Christian theologians and other philosophers as a version of heaven. Plato also thought there was an immortal soul, separate from the body, which could reach the realm of Perfect Forms and he considered people had some innate knowledge, not learned from experience. This innateness was because we have immortal souls which have been around in a previous existence. These metaphysical concepts were later eagerly grabbed by Catholic theologians and used to bolster their Christian beliefs.

Plato’s domain of Perfect Forms is alleged, by some experts, to derive from his ‘unwritten doctrines’. In these Plato is supposed to have come up with two underlying doctrinal principles:

  • The One: the unity principle. This makes things fixed and determinate
  • The Indefinite Dyad: This is the opposite of ‘The One’. The Dyad is the source of ‘indeterminacy’ and ‘endlessness’.

There are some slightly different explanations of these two. They may provide underpinning for the place of Perfect Forms They can also, The One and The Dyad, apparently work together to deliver both unity in the universe and diversity. Later philosophers developed these two concepts. Some associated The One with ‘Good’ and Dyad with ‘Evil’ or to Plato’s God like entity, the Demiurge. Others, led by Plato’s nephew Speusippus, attached spheres of pure numbers and the body then introducing a third element, the soul. There are a number of experts who think there is insufficient evidence for the claim that Plato held these ‘unwritten doctrines.

His philosophy covered ethics, metaphysics and politics. On the latter, he said that reason and wisdom should govern. These two qualities he thought best resided in philosophers. Plato said government should be by philosopher kings. It’s understandable he would think this. In his experience, Athenian democracy was soured by the unjustified death sentence handed down to his revered teacher, Socrates.

p.292

In the field of ethics Plato famously expressed the idea of a perfect, spiritual, non-physical love. Platonic love is an expression we use 2,500 years after Plato, although the term itself was first iterated by William Davenant in 1635.

And we know from Plato’s Apology that Socrates linked wisdom with humility. Another Platonian idea, which is still quoted today is his Allegory of the Cave (often called Plato’s cave). This demonstrates that our limited experience and senses can give us an entirely false picture of reality. Einstein said something similar when he referred to a young child in a library.

He thought women were equal to men and should have access to both education and politics, just as men had. On metempsychosis (re-incarnation) the sexless soul could change genders.

Some philosophers and theologians who followed Plato, sometimes many centuries later, supported his views. These are known as Platonists. Platonism, in brief, is the idea that goodness is a part of reality and that physical objects are temporary and simply representations of the permanent ideas of these objects. Modern phenomenologists also appreciate this viewpoint of limited perception, so maybe Whitehead is right.

22. Diogenes of Sinope

404 to 323 BCE Cynicism

Diogenes was a Greek born in Sinope, now in Turkey. He is not to be confused with Diogenes Laertius. This second guy was a biographer of the ancient Greek philosophers, whom he recorded in his book “Lives of the Philosophers”.

His comments were usually brief but had a longer account of Epicurus, so Laertius might have been an Epicurean.

Our man, Diogenes of Sinope, was an unusual character. His rebellious and bolshie nature showed up in a number of cynical stunts, like carrying a lamp in daytime claiming to be looking for an honest man.

He also famously had an encounter with Alexander the Great. Alexander asked Diogenes what he desired. Diogenes replied: “Stand out of my light.”

p.293

Then he crossed swords, intellectually, with Plato. Plato disparagingly called Diogenes a cynic. This meant dog in Greek. Diogenes got his own back. Plato had referred to man as an animal, bipedal and featherless. Diogenes plucked a chicken and threw it at Plato’s feet saying: “Here is Plato’s man.” His home was often a ceramic jar, where he slept in the market place. He challenged local conventions, thinking society was corrupt. Plato did not like him because he disrupted his lectures and disputed his interpretation of Socrates. Diogenes had no respect for anyone, including Plato. Diogenes is said to have dogged the footsteps of Antisthenes (a student of Socrates).

He believed in living an ethical life. To achieve this, he thought we should live in tune with natural world by holding no possessions His view he sums up by saying: “He who has the most is most content with the least.” Like Socrates, he favoured expounding verbally rather than in writing. Not surprisingly, he was a renowned cynic, as we now understand the term. Diogenes practised what he preached. His life was quite long and quite eventful. Apart from unusual living quarters, and surviving being rude to the great Alexander, earlier in his life he was captured by pirates and sold as a slave before finally living in Corinth.

23. Aristotle

384 to 322 BCE Socratic, Empiricism

Like his mentor Plato, Aristotle was one of the greatest and most influential figures in the history of philosophy. In temperament and approach Aristotle differed from Plato. Both were brilliant and analytical but Plato tended to be attached to grand ideas. Aristotle liked to take a more step by step rational approach; much more scientific in his method. The Aristotelian method was used many centuries later by scientists of all kinds.

Charles Darwin said: " Linnaeus and Cuvier have been my two gods though in very different ways, but they were mere schoolboys to old Aristotle."

While Plato taught at the Academy Aristotle school was the Lyceum. Here lectures were free of charge and many were open to the general public, unlike at Plato’s Academy. Clever research students walked the Lyceum cloisters, called ‘peripatos’. These people became known as the Peripatetics.

p.294

Plato’s influence reigned supreme with early Christian theologians. It was quite some time after Boethius’ important commentary on Aristotle, in the 6th century, that Aristotle began to be taken seriously as a prop for Christian beliefs. He then had an impact on Abrahamic religions, particularly through medieval theologians. Aquinas simply referred to him as The Philosopher. Some medieval Muslim theologians called him the first teacher. He had a brilliant mind and developed the form of inductive argument that established firstly known truths based on experience then moved to a logical conclusion. He did not believe in innate knowledge, as did Plato, rather he was an early empiricist, and was followed by such as John Locke, George Berkeley and David Hume.

Although a student of the great Plato, Aristotle was someone who developed his own brand of ideas in logic, metaphysics, ethics and politics. He disagreed with Plato’s perfect Forms. He also disagreed with Plato over the soul. Plato said the soul and mind (body) were separate. They could exist after death; the mind would be re-incarnated in another body. Aristotle thought the two were linked. This could imply the soul died when the mind died on death of the body. Aristotle did not think that a God had kicked off the universe. He said the universe had always existed.

His ethical philosophy was mainly set out in Nicomachean Ethics (named after his father and son). In this work he stressed the importance of happiness. Like Epicurus he thought pleasure was one route to individual happiness, but he stressed two other components of happiness, the love of honour (closely connected to politics) and love of contemplation. Of the three Aristotle claimed contemplation was the most important because it involved using reason.

Aristotle thought that through contemplation and reasoning people would be led to a virtuous life. Courage was also an essential feature. These two, contemplation and courage, would themselves move people to some ultimate good and happiness (Eudaimonia), but they must keep to the virtuous path over their whole life - from Nicomachean Ethics: “Human good turns out to be the active exercise of the soul in conformity with excellence or virtue…. but this activity must take place throughout a complete lifetime for one swallow does not make a summer, any more than one fine day.” Plato gave the English language ‘platonic love” Aristotle “one swallow does not make a summer.

p.295

He said the happiness of the community was more important than that of an individual. In this he was an early utilitarian, no doubt Bentham heeded Aristotle’s lead. So, with Aristotle we have quite a list of ethical pointers with the end goal of goodness and happiness. And with his utilitarian stance he was suggesting we look out for what is best for the wider community.

Like his mentor Plato he thought everything, including us, had an essential essence that gives purpose. He held totally different views to Plato about the status on women seeing them as intellectually inferior to men and subject to them.

Aristotle was a polymath who made contributions in mathematics, physics, biology, botany, medicine, agriculture, politics, dance and theatre. He thought that the universe had always existed. He was the first to compartmentalise and identify specific areas of knowledge like ethics and mathematics. As the founder of the field of logic he was the first to develop a formalised system for reasoning. Through his enormous and largely original input across a wide range of subjects he is counted as one the most influential individuals in history.

He also thought that the Earth was the centre of the universe, a belief strongly taken up by the Christian church until, well after Galileo’s time, they eventually accepted that the Earth was just another planet and it orbited the sun, not the other way around. He had great interest in the state and how people should be governed: “The only stable state is the one in which all men are equal before the law.” and “If liberty and equality, as is thought by some are chiefly to be found in democracy, they will be best attained when all persons alike share in the government to the utmost.”

24. Phyrro

360 to 270 BCE Noncognitivism, Scepticism

Pyrrho was born in Elis, Greece, on the Ionian Sea. He started off as a painter before turning to philosophy having been influenced by Democritus. His philosophy has an Eastern influence too. He joined Alexander the Great on his expedition to India. In philosophical history, he might be the originator of Western

p.296

Scepticism, possibly importing it from India. His was a defeatist type of philosophy in regard to the gaining of knowledge and certainty. Nothing can be known, so Pyrrho thought. The answer then to life is to take a “ataraxia” attitude, which means freedom from worry. He said that people should ask themselves three questions: “Firstly we must ask what things are and how they are constituted. Secondly, we ask how we are related to these things. Thirdly, we ask what ought to be our attitude towards them.” Source of the phrase “pyrrhic victory”, i.e. winning is actually losing. Just not believing, Pyrrho thought, led to a tranquil state of mind.

25. Epicurus

341 to 271 BCE Epicureanism, Atomist

Epicurus was born on Samos, part of Greece. He set up a school in the garden of his house. The school was known as “the Garden”. Here, he taught all-comers, including women, children and slaves. This was a highly unusual demonstration of equality in his time. Epicurus, a vegetarian, ancient Greek philosopher was a gentle man who constantly tried to achieve a state of contentment and happiness no matter what his situation.

His philosophy was summed up by two Greek words: ataraxia, meaning freedom from worry, in a state of blissful peace, and aponia, the absence of pain. These two were essential elements of the Epicurean dictum ‘seek happiness’.

He lived out his own philosophy. Even as he was painfully dying he wrote a letter: " I have written this letter to you on a happy day to me, which is also the last day of my life. For I have been attacked by a painful inability to urinate, and also dysentery, so violent that nothing can be added to the violence of my sufferings. But the cheerfulness of my mind, which comes from the recollection of all my philosophical contemplation, counterbalances all these afflictions."

Epicurus’ philosophy was mainly about ethics and how people could gain peace of mind and be happy. He was what would be called today “a nice man”. People should not fear death, he said, reasoning: “Our unhappiness is caused by fear and our main fear is death. Death is the end of sensation so cannot be physically painful. Death is the end of consciousness so cannot be emotionally painful. Death is nothing to fear.”

p.297

He was not, as he was popularly thought, focused strongly on sensual pleasure. This comes from the more liberal and erotic version of Epicureanism adopted by the Romans.

Friendship, knowledge, leading a temperate life and not worrying about death, that’s the way to perfect happiness said the great man. He thought we had souls but that these faded out, were not sentient, once they were without the essential component to occupy - a body.

Not surprisingly, as a follower of Democritus, Epicurus was an atomist. For him the universe was eternal and everything in it was founded on the movement and composition of elemental particles, these Democritus, and his mentor Leucippus, referred to as atoms. These atoms, randomly got together to form everything that we know of, including modern humans and even Gods, so thought Epicurus. This randomness and indeterminacy smacks of Heisenberg’s uncertainty principle, which arrived some 2,300 years after Epicurus first came up with a somewhat similar idea. He also led the way for Darwinism and evolution.

Epicurus did not see that there was any directing presence - a God or Gods. He thought Gods, although they existed, had no influence or involvement with humans. This was a position completely at variance with the majority view in his time, where people made sacrifices to various Gods and prayed in temples asking for assorted favours. He thought Gods could be seen in the mind: “The Gods do indeed exist, since our knowledge of them is a matter of clear and distinct perception.” The eternal atoms and universe were not from the Gods. The deities had nothing to do with them. The Gods, as he saw them, were parked so far back on the side-lines, so disinterested and uninvolved with humans that Epicurus has been considered to be a type of atheist.

Epicurus had a simple, but compelling philosophy for a good, happy life: ataraxia, aponia, and friends. We must also remember the importance of the garden, where, like flowers, good ideas are planted and grow!

Interestingly, Epicurean focus on happiness is enshrined in the USA Declaration of Independence: “… life, liberty and the pursuit of happiness.” That makes all good Americans Epicureans!

26. Zeno of Citium

334 to 262 BCE Stoic, half cynic

This Zeno was born in Cyprus. He was first a wealthy merchant. This might have given the opportunity to sit back and think. The philosophy of the cynics attracted him at first. Following on from Cynicism Zeno split his philosophy into three areas Logic; physics and the divine nature of the universe; lastly ethics. Cicero was not impressed by Zeno’s attempts at logic., thinking others before him had a better handle on logic. Under ‘physics’ Zeno thought the universe is God where everything belongs to one whole. A super Gaia theory? His ethics said there was only one, simple good. He linked the soul with this true good and with virtue. He said that reason was a key element of virtuous good, that destructive emotions blur good judgement and pleasure and pain should be treated with indifference. He was not in agreement with Epicurus.

Later he began to teach in Athens at a place called the Stoa Poikile. Identifying with this location, his followers became known as Stoics and so, completely unbeknown to himself, Zeno of Citium became recognised as the first stoic.

27. Cicero Marcus Tullius

106 to 43 BCE Roman, Political

Cicero was a man of many talents: A Roman philosopher, politician, lawyer, political theorist and consul. A little like Epicurus, he liked a garden: “If you have a garden and a library, you have everything you need.” Perhaps Cicero ought to be the patron saint of both gardeners and librarians?

He was also a great orator and writer, so able to convey, convincingly his thoughts on these many areas. The start of the 14th century Renaissance is considered to be partly due to the revival of Cicero’s letters. He also had an influence on Enlightenment figures such as David Hume, John Locke and Montesquieu. Politics was his main love. He supported the return of republican government in Rome and became an enemy of Mark Anthony. This was a bad move and brought about his death.

p.299

His political philosophy has had huge bearing on the development of political ideas in the western world. John Adams (second President of the USA) said: “As all the ages of the world have not produced a greater statesman and philosopher united than Cicero, his authority should have great weight.” Thomas Jefferson (American founding father and main author of The Declaration of Independence) named Cicero as a major figure who contributed to a tradition “of public right”. This was a fundamental idea that underpinned his draft of the Declaration of Independence.

Camille Desmoulins (journalist and friend of Robespierre) said of the French republicans in 1789 that they were "mostly young people who, nourished by the reading of Cicero at school, had become passionate enthusiasts for liberty”. Jim Powell, author, begins his book on the history of liberty: “Marcus Tullius Cicero expressed principles that became the bedrock of liberty in the modern world.” What Cicero said was: “For there is but one essential justice which cements society, and one law which establishes this justice. This law is right reason, which is the true rule of all commandments and prohibitions. Whoever neglects this law, whether written or unwritten, is necessarily unjust and wicked.” In philosophy, he was a great admirer of Plato, saying if Zeus (Roman God) was to speak he would use the language of Plato found in his dialogues. Cicero encouraged religious toleration and was admired by Augustine of Hippo, Erasmus and Martin Luther.

28. Titus Lucretius Carus

99 to 55 BCE Metaphysics,

Lucretius was a Roman philosopher who was also a poet. Lucretius was author of the six-book epic: “De Rerum Natura”, (On the Nature of Things), a gigantic philosophical poem of 7,500 lines. This is thought by some to be the greatest masterpiece of Latin verse. In this work, he demonstrated his firmly held Epicurean views on attaining a tranquil mind and intellectual pleasure. Like Epicurus he thought death was to be accepted without fear: “Therefore death is nothing to us, it matters not one jot, since the nature of the mind is understood to be mortal.”

Lucretius was known as the Roman voice of Epicurus, but he also has some admiration for Empedocles. He distrusted religion: “Only religion can lead to such evil.” Lucretius made possibly the first great statement leading towards atheism: “Nothing can come from nothing.” (Ex nihilo, nihil fit).

p.300

His point was that matter exists, we know that by looking around us, but matter he claimed must be eternal and uncreated. Epicurus before him and Hobbes and Hume much later, held similar views.

His books give a natural explanation for the physical origin of the universe. Curiously though, he thought that Gods did exist, just as did Epicurus, but that they were not involved at all with humans, nor did they originate matter, hence the atheist tag. He also beats Darwin to the punch by suggesting the evolution of life forms, but he did not back this up with the same volume of supporting evidence and argument that Darwin used. Titus even put forward theories on the atomic structure of matter. Not surprisingly his work has been a source of inspiration for both scientists and philosophers in recent centuries. In his book, ‘The Swerve’, Stephen Greenblatt makes the point that Lucretius through ‘On the Nature of Things’ was an inspiration for the Renaissance, thanks to its re-discovery by Poggio Bracciolini in 1417 CE. Those who read and may have been inspired by it include: Niccolò Machiavelli, William Shakespeare, Michel de Montaigne, Charles Darwin, even Thomas Jefferson and Albert Einstein. This must mean two of the people who inspired Lucretius, Epicurus, mainly, and Democritus to an extent, also had, a bit of a hand in this, the Renaissance, a period of cultural and scientific rebirth from the 14th to the 17th century.

29. Philo of Alexandria

25 BCE to 50 CE Platonism

Had huge influence on later Christian thought and he possibly made an even earlier impact on Paul, who was a contemporary as well as the authors of the Gospel of John. Philo tried to gel together Hebrew mythology with Greek philosophy. His doctrine of logos was probably the most important aspect of his philosophy. Stoics used logos to refer to their idea of the universe as a living, organic entity. With Philo logos meant a transcendental, anthropomorphic being which was a mediator between God and men. He realised that the book of the Old Testament could not possibly contain literal truths. Philo justified these stories by claiming the content was mainly allegorical, using the Greek stories in the “Iliad” as an example.

He had great respect for Greek philosophy and tried to fuse Greek philosophy with Christian beliefs.

p.301

Philo thought Moses was the top man and had influenced Greek philosophers, especially Pythagoras. He held Plato in near divine reverence referring to him as: “The most holy Plato.”, but also admired Aristotle, Heraclitus and stoicism. His Platonism fused with elements of stoicism and Aristotelian logic and ethics in addition to some of Pythagoras’ ideas. His basic ethical philosophy was simple: “Be kind, for everyone you meet is fighting a great battle.”

Philo identified four virtues:

  • wisdom,
  • self-control,
  • courage,
  • justice.

Here is an early Jewish writer (he overlapped with Christ) who says, in effect, the biblical Old Testament stories are not literally true.

30. Seneca, Lucius Annaeus

4 BCE to 65 CE Stoic

Here he is, Lucius or Seneca the younger, but more commonly known as simply ‘Seneca’. He might be simply known but was in no way simple. Born to a low ranked provincial nobleman he came under the wing of an aunt who took him to Rome. Here he was educated in oratory and philosophy. The latter focus was on Stoicism and Neo-Pythagoreanism (revival of Pythagoras’s 500-year-old philosophy). Following on from his education he dabbled in politics and law, but found politics more interesting and rewarding. It took him a while to become recognised at the highest level of Roman politics, He became a Praetor (Roman official just below Consul) at 45 years old, in the time of Emperor Caligula. His rise was mainly due to his intellect. This, when combined with his renowned oratorical skill made him a formidable debater. His rhetorical expertise did not save him from a near death sentence when he crossed swords with Caligula. Seneca’s constant ill health persuaded Caligula that he was going to kick the bucket quite soon and did not need formally bumping off. Later, he manged to upset a second Emperor, Claudius, through a claimed dalliance with the Emperor’s niece, Julia.

His sentence was exile on the island of Corsica. He managed though to use his various skills to become extremely wealthy, owning several villa and estates around Italy. His lavish dinner parties demonstrated his love of a good time.

p.302

You might think he was too busy with many matters to have anytime for deep philosophical thought. He had huge estates and lots of investments to manage, then there’s the partying. He also wrote tragedies which must have taken up some of his time. Finally, his serious politicising and engagement with various Emperors would require much investment of hours. Despite all this Lucius did find time to slot in a bit of philosophising. Perhaps his delicate constitution meant he had to sit down and rest up quite frequently, giving him opportunity to ponder.

He was firmly attached to stoicism and obviously thought everyone could do better: “We are not given a short life but we make it short, and we are not ill-supplied but wasteful of it.” And this one is a bit Epicureanism: “The whole future lies in uncertainty: live immediately.” Two more quotes put him solidly with Epicurus on the question of fear of death: “The wise man will live as long as he ought, not as long as he can.” and “Death: There’s nothing bad about it at all except the thing that comes before it - the fear of it.”

In his letters ‘Epistulae Morales’ he discusses a range of topics from a philosophical viewpoint: wealth, grief, success, failure, and jealousy. His most famous text is ‘Letters to Lucilius’. From a position of stoicism, he argued that death, in yet another thought on this topic, is not an evil, just a necessity. Seneca also claimed that political power is not valuable and, curiously, given his riches, that wealth is not good. Seneca said he tried to follow his own philosophy but did not always succeed - too right.

His release from exile on Corsica, was at the behest of Agrippina, the mother of the future Emperor Nero. She wanted Seneca to tutor her son. This turned out to be a fatal job. In 65 CE Nero sentenced him to death, by suicide, for his alleged involvement in a plot to overthrow him. You might expect Seneca to have learnt from his bad experience of two previous Emperors. He should have known they are fairly easily annoyed and when this happens they take excessive action against the annoyer. How did such a clever, capable man, get into this position, why did he not depart to one of his estates and continued his writing?

p.303

31. Plutarch

45 to120 CE Platonism

Plutarch was born in Chaeronea, Greece. He had a huge literary output; his works totalled 227. Because of this he’s counted as the greatest literary figure of the first century CE. A section of his work was devoted to philosophical and other topics collectively known as Ethical Essays or Moralia. These essays were written in dialogue form as a series of advice on practical ethics. Cunningly, the philosophy he espoused was directed at the elite of his time. It was intended to make the upper class become even more upper class. At heart, he was a Platonist and was critical of what he considered was stoicism’s materialism and Epicurus’s atheism. Of the two, it was Epicurus’s philosophy he most firmly rejected. Despite being critical of stoicism, he did hold some respect for it. Plutarch believed in a divine entity God. For him, the Gods of other peoples and religions were just aspects of the same divine being. His divinity was closely allied to that of Plato, if Plato’s unwritten dialogues concerning The One - being the goodie and the Dyad - being the baddie. These sit together and interact as a form of dualism. They are responsible for the cosmos, with different perceptions from a good and an evil standpoint. The revelations of Plutarch’s deity were supremely important guide to humans. This is similar to the divination believed by stoics. His philosophy was closely linked to his religious beliefs.

An eternal soul was something else he believed in. It was not properly recalling of the ‘other world’ when in old age: “The soul, being eternal, after death is like a caged bird that has been released. If it has been a long time in the body, and has become tame by many affairs and long habit, the soul will immediately take another body and once again become involved in the troubles of the world. The worst thing about old age is that the soul’s memory of the other world grows dim, while at the same time its attachment to things of this world becomes so strong that the soul tends to retain the form that it had in the body. But that soul which remains only a short time within a body, until liberated by the higher powers, quickly recovers its fire and goes on to higher things."

Apart from his philosophy, Plutarch was also a biographer. He wrote about 48 famous Roman and Greeks, pairing the latter with the former; Pericles with Fabius Maximus and Alexander with Julius Caesar are two examples. All 48 were men, and men of power.

p.304

It can be speculated that Plutarch’s Moralia, with it’s philosophical guide aimed squarely at the elite of his day, could be one reason why he strongly rejected Epicurus. Epicurus was far more democratic and libertarian. He taught all comers, no matter their station in life. In addition, Plutarch’s deity was very much hands of while Epicurus’s was so far disconnected as to be almost invisible. I’m with Epicurus.

32. Epictetus

55 to 135 CE Stoicism

He was born a slave in Hierapolis, Phrygia (now Pamukkale, Turkey). Epictetus was a major figure of stoic ethics philosophy who also believed in a God who controls the universe. He was a follower of Socrates and Diogenes Laertius. See the latter’s “Lives and Opinions of Eminent Philosophers”.

Epictetus believed that a God gave humans free will. He thought people should not just discuss philosophy and stoicism but practice it. To this end he proposed three topoi, or areas to be studied:

  1. Desires (orexeis) and aversions (ekkliseis);
  2. Impulse to act (hormas) and not to act (aphormas);
  3. Freedom from deception, hasty judgement, and anything else related to assents (sunkatatheseis).

These three areas of training correspond to the three types of philosophical discourse referred to by earlier Stoics; the physical, the ethical, and the logical. He taught students stoic philosophy and spoke of his school as a hospital where students come to have their ills sorted out.

Quotes that demonstrate his broad-mindedness and stoical approach: “All religions must be tolerated… for every man must get to heaven in his own way.” He was also a great believer in the acquisition of knowledge and said: “Only the educated are free.” He supported a stoical approach and accepting one’s fate: “Happiness and freedom begin with a clear understanding of one principle: Some things are within our control, and some things are not. It is only after you have faced up to this fundamental rule and learned to distinguish between what you can and can’t control that inner tranquillity and outer effectiveness become possible.” Some similarity with his close namesake, Epicurus, here.

p.305

Epictetus said the way to achieve happiness was by living a life of reason; according to nature and free from passion. Being both rational and keeping your equilibrium was his shorthand for stoical living. Interestingly his philosophy was a help to James Stockdale, an American fighter pilot. In 1965 he was shot down in Vietnam then kept in captivity for seven and a half years. Stockdale quoted Epictetus in his later book: “The emotions of grief, pity and even affection are well-known disturbers of the soul. Grief is the most offensive.” Whether Epictetus actually said this could be open to some doubt. It certainly is in line with his stoic philosophy but, like Socrates, he did not write anything down. What we know of him is mainly through his student and historian, Arrian. He wrote down the teachings of Epictetus in the Discourses and Handbook. Topics covered by his master were ethics and advice on how to conduct oneself. They range over subjects such as getting and maintaining tranquillity; poverty; friendship; fear and illness.

33. Aurelius, Marcus

121 to 180 CE Roman, Stoicism

Here’s a philosopher who had a day job as a Roman Emperor. While perhaps not rated as an absolute top-rate philosophical thinker, his regular job as Emperor gave him a lot of clout as a philosopher. Marcus probably wrote his major work, “The Meditations” while campaigning in central Europe. “Meditations” was meant for his personal use as a series of philosophical exercises designed to change his life. Marcus tried to take not just a world view but a cosmological viewpoint.

He admired Epictetus and is thought to be a stoic although he did not make this clear in “Meditations”. Said Marcus: “When you arise in the morning think of what a privilege it is to be alive, to think, to enjoy, to love …”, quite Epicurean to consider that regardless of whether there were Gods, people should live a worthy life: " If there are Gods and they are just, then they will not care how devout you have been, but will welcome you based on the virtues you have lived by. If there are Gods but unjust, then you should not want to worship them. If there are Gods, then you will be gone, but will have lived a noble life that will live on in the memories of your loved ones." His strong stoical leanings are shown in this quote: “Accept the things to which fate binds you, and love the people with whom fate brings you together, but do so with all your heart.”

p.306

Another good one: “Waste no more time arguing what a good man should be. Be one.” Marcus believed action spoke louder than words.

34. Origen

185 to 253 CE Neo-Platonism

Another philosopher counted as Greek despite being born in Alexandria, Egypt. An important theologian and Neo-Platonism philosopher. Origen greatly influenced the early Christian church through his works: “Hexapla, On First Principles” and “Contra Celsus”. The former helped establish a clear doctrinal basis for the fragmented Christian church. It also countered the Gnostics.

The Contra argued against the views of the pagan philosopher Celsus and rebutted his attack on Christianity. These works helped the church greatly. His most renowned view was of universal salvation where, in the end evil is completely defeated and the devil repents. He believed in an allegorical interpretation of the scriptures These, he thought, had three levels of meaning: historical; moral and mystical. He said: “He who is anxious to attain a true understanding of holy Scripture, will discover the spiritual truths which are spoken by it to those who are called “spiritual,” by comparing the meaning of what is addressed to those of weaker mind with what is announced to such as are of acuter understanding, both meanings being frequently found in the same passage by him who is capable of comprehending it.”

He also supported the idea of a holy trinity, although he downgraded the Son of God to the role of a secondary God. The church in 533 CE condemned this idea, nearly 300 years after his death.

35. Plotinus

205 to 270 CE Neo-Platonism

May have been born in Lycopolis, Egypt and may have Invented Neo-Platonism. Of course, he knew nothing of the term Neo-Platonism was first coined in the 19th century. Plotinus was contemporary with Origen. Unlike Origen though, he specialised in the works of Plato.

p.307

He expounded his philosophy through leaning heavily on Plato’s works. Plotinus seemed to think he knew better than Plato what Plato’s ideas really meant and he set these out in the six Enneads. Enneads means nine in Greek. Porphyry, Plotinus’ pupil helped edit Plotinus’ writing. He intended the complete set to be in six groups of nine, although there turned out to be fewer than 54 finally, perhaps either Porphyry or Plotinus although very good with words were hopeless at simple arithmetic.

He said, in a similar fashion to Plato, that there are three founding principles of the cosmos: the One, the Intellect, and the Soul but Plotinus thought these three could be further sub-divided. So, he made a few slight variations on Plato ideas. Like Plato’s concept of Perfect Forms Plotinus thought there was some place that was the cosmic top of the tree. This was the source of everything - the One. Below this he envisioned four other layers representing: The Mind, the Soul, Nature and lastly the Material World. In Plotinus’ view true human happiness (Eudaimonia) was beyond anything physical. It was only attainable within consciousness, the mind. We could get closer to the One, he said, through good and appreciation of beauty. He, with some similarity with the Buddha, believed in re-incarnation and thought that through enlightenment people could achieve eventual union with the One, this also has some similarity with the aim of Taoists. They wanted to get close to the mighty Dao. Plotinus said when people achieved this level of enlightenment they would no longer be re-incarnated, claiming: “This All is universal power, of infinite extent and infinite in potency, a god so great that all his parts are infinite. Name any place, and he is already there.” Plotinus’s ideas made him attractive in later centuries to many different beliefs: apart from Buddhists these include Christian, Islamic, Jewish, pagans and mystics.

36. Hypatia of Alexandria

350? To 415 CE Woman, Platonism

Hypatia, wow, what a woman! She was the first major woman philosopher that we know of and rated the top philosopher of her life time, man or woman. Not only that she was also the world’s premier mathematician and astronomer, man or woman, during her adult life. There’s no other woman in history who can make the same claim to eminence in three fields of human endeavour. She was also an inventor and teacher. It is thought she may have invented the Astrolabe (used to measure the position of the Sun and stars). This is a claim made by one of her students, Synesius.

p.308

Born in Alexandria to Theon, a mathematician who must have been very free thinking about the status of women in his time. She was taught and worked with her father and he allowed her to study in Athens. She later became head of a school in Alexandria and taught philosophy to all comers, without fear or favour, no matter their religion or status. Hypatia was not a Christian or Jew. Both religions were competitively prominent in Alexandria in her time.

She was referred to as a pagan. Her main topics were the teachings of Plato, Aristotle and Plotinus, with a particular focus on the Neoplatonic view that everything came from the One. Her lack of Christian belief, eventually led to her demise, but Christians came to her school and some liked her, seeing her as a symbol of virtue. The Christian historian Socrates of Constantinople, (to avoid confusion with the great Socrates of Athens, this Socrates has a name that must not be shortened), wrote about her in glowing terms:

“There was a woman at Alexandria named Hypatia, daughter of the philosopher Theon, who made such attainments in literature and science, as to far surpass all the philosophers of her own time. Having succeeded to the school of Plato and Plotinus, she explained the principles of philosophy to her auditors, many of whom came from a distance to receive her instructions. On account of the self-possession and ease of manner which she acquired in consequence of the cultivation of her mind, she not infrequently appeared in public in the presence of the magistrates. Neither did she feel abashed in going to an assembly of men. For all men on account of her extraordinary dignity and virtue admired her the more.”

Hypatia was a deep thinker and, like Socrates (the great Athenian philosopher) was very hard to argue with. She championed a questioning approach to everything saying: “Reserve the right to think, for even to think wrongly is better than not to think at all.”

Hypatia became victim of a feud between Alexandria’s Christian Bishop Cyril and The Roman Governor Orestes. She was known to counsel Orestes so a Christian mob captured her and tortured her to death. Socrates (the Constantinople one) said about this barbaric act: " Surely nothing can be farther from the spirit of Christianity than the allowance of massacres, fights, and transactions of that sort." Some historians think Hypatia’s death was a turning point. Kathleen Wider suggested it marked the end of Classical antiquity (the ancient Greek and Roman period) and Stephen Greenblatt said

p.309

her murder signalled the downfall of Alexandrian intellectual life. Hypatia, it has been suggested, may have been Head Librarian at the Alexandria Library. One story says the Library was burned down at the time of her death. Certainly, the destruction of the intellectual underpinnings, represented by the 40,000 books burned with the Alexandria Library, acted as a brake on the flowering of knowledge. There are various accounts of when the mightiest Library of antiquity was burned down. The Hypatia link is only one of them. Another suggests it was after the Muslim conquest of Egypt, more than 200 years after Hypatia. At this time when his man Amyr asked the conqueror Caliph Omar what to do with the books Omar allegedly said: “If those books are in agreement with the Qur’an, we have no need of them; and if these are opposed to the Qur’an, destroy them.”

Hypatia’s influence outlived her. Voltaire mentioned Hypatia in his condemnation of the church and religion. In Charles Kingsley’s Victorian romance, she was the main subject. A well rated Spanish film about her called Agora was made in 2009. This was directed by Alejandro Amenábar with Rachel Weisz playing Hypatia.

Hypatia is to this day a feminist icon.

p.310

37. Augustine of Hippo

354 to 430 CE Dualism, Christian Platonism

Also, known as St Augustine and St Austin. He was born in that part of the Roman Empire now Algeria. Augustine was both a theologian and philosopher, but he was also very human and definitely a man. He said to his God: “Give me chastity and continence, but not just yet!” Augustine was always fearful of his mother’s reaction to the way he lived his life. Monica was a strict, devout Christian. Augustine took some time before he came around to accepting Christianity.

In his earlier years, he certainly enjoyed life, gaining a mistress and a son. Although he indulged his passions, he always regretted that he was not more like his mother and constantly chastised himself. He admired Cicero and like his teacher Ambrose saw Platonic ideas as a precursor to Christianity. Had huge influence on the early church and was known as “The father of the church”. He wrote “The Confessions” and “The City of God.” among many other works. In this he proposed the concept of the Catholic, spiritual City of God. The City of God, held the idea of a Trinity, as defined by the Council of Nicaea, 325 CE, was correct. He thought God had freely created the world and time from nothing. His views on time were original, thinking that both past and future are all in the mind; the past being remembered and the future anticipated Later both Kant and Einstein considered the question of ‘time’.

Augustine had a neat answer to the problem of God and the existence of evil, using some of Plato’s ideas. He thought that although God created everything in existence, He did not create evil. Augustine said that evil derives from lack or deficiency of good and is not a thing in itself. This concept was eagerly latched on to by Christian philosophers since Augustine’s time. But David Hume and others pointed out the problems of this line of reasoning, e.g. is illness simply an absence of good health?

The Roman Catholic doctrine of original sin is mainly down to Augustine. He was an advocate of “just war” and he viewed the soul as superior and separate from the body. Lastly, he was a supporter of the teleological argument for the proof of God’s existence. In essence, this argument says that the world and everything in it is so perfectly ordered that it must be because of a Godly creator.

38. Boethius, Anicius

480 to 524 CE Roman, Christian Platonism

He was born to a wealthy family in Rome at the time of the last Roman Emperor. He became a Roman senator and a consul. Boethius was a Roman philosopher who studied Plato but who also translated the works of Aristotle into Latin. His other influences were Augustine of Hippo and the Romans Cicero and Seneca. He also studied Porphyry - he who edited Plotinus’ Enneads. But his clear favourite among philosophers was Plato. This may be why Boethius’ most famous work followed the Platonic writing method of dialogue. He became an important government official but upset several powerful figures and was accused of treason. While in prison he wrote the “Consolation of Philosophy”. He was executed but not before he had finished his writing. In this work, he was helpful to Christians through coming up with ideas that addressed problems to do with free will. His major work consisted of a dramatic dialogue between himself and the Lady Philosophy. She argued that Earthly things like wealth, health and power were fickle. Lasting and true happiness could only be obtained through God. She also pronounced on evil and free will. There had always been concern by Christian thinkers over the issue of how can humans have free will if God knows exactly what we will do. Boethius argued that God was out of time - everything for God was like the present. All is now, no past or future. Boethius argued that we were free to choose but God knew what our choice was. He made this claim: “If God imposes order upon all things, there is no opportunity for random events.” During the Middle Ages Boethius’ work “The Consolation of Philosophy” was the most translated and copied of all secular works. He wrote influential treaties on education and music too. His fall from grace followed his brave defence of an ex-consul, Albinus, who was accused of treason. Boethius himself was also convicted of treason and executed.

39. Avicenna, or Ibn Sînâ

980 to 1037 CE Dualism

Avicenna’s first language was Persian, but he wrote in Arabic. Along with Averroes he was the greatest Islamic philosopher. He was a child prodigy and swiftly became supremely expert in many fields: philosophy, logic and medicine especially.

p.312

His book on medicine, The Canon of Medicine, was a standard textbook well into medieval times. Avicenna was determinedly a philosopher rather than an Islamic theologian. He thought that there was a soul and that it must be separate from the body. This was important because it allowed the possibility of the soul surviving on the death of the body. This was a Platonic view and at odds with Aristotle who said the soul and mind were linked so when one died (the mind) then the other, i.e. the soul could no longer exist. Avicenna tried logical argument to amend this Aristotelian view so that both mind and soul continued after death although not the body. For this Avicenna is known as a dualist, like Rene Descartes several hundred years later, although the two components of Rene’s dualism were different. His two were mind and body.

Avicenna emphasised the importance of prayer: “Prayer is that which enables the soul to realize its divinity. Through prayer human beings worship absolute truth, and seek an eternal reward. Prayer is the foundation-stone of religion; and religion is the means by which the soul is purified of all that pollutes it.” Avicenna’s views put him at odds with orthodox Islamic beliefs, where the whole body and mind is thought to be resurrected in an afterlife. He was attacked for his beliefs and called a heretic by Islamic theologian al-Ghazali.

40. Aztec Philosophy

14 and 15th CE South American Monism

The Aztecs were a group of people who dominated the central Americas for about 300 years, from the 14th to the 16th century. In 1521 the Aztec world changed forever through the devastating impact caused by the arrival of the Spanish conqueror, Cortes. Before Cortes and his cohorts, they had no contact with and no influence by the middle-eastern or western world. Christianity, Islam, the Greek and other philosophers were unknown to them. This makes the separate development of their religious and philosophical thoughts interestingly unique.

The Aztecs were known for ritual human sacrifice. Their religion required sacrificial homage to various Gods who had made the world and the sun. Other South American civilisations similarly practised human sacrifice: the Olmecs, Maya and Toltecs. However, the Aztec’s also had a distinct philosophy. This was mainly metaphysical in nature, focusing on the nature of reality but it also included ethics and knowledge. They called their philosophers tiamatinime (knower of things).

p.313

Aztecs thought there existed only one thing, ultimately. They called this teotl. The teotl was an eternally self-regenerating force at the heart of everything, the entire universe. It included such things as the sun, moon, rain, wind, mountains, plants and animals. All are considered as one with the sacred teotl. The oneness and eternal aspects are similar to the thinking of Anaximander.

Living a moral life was seen as a way of maintaining stability and balance in the ever changing teotl. Aztecs saw arts as a method of showing up the real nature of the elusive teotl. Poetry was used as an expression of the eternal but changing nature of teotl

"No one comes on this earth to stay Our bodies are like rose trees - They grow petals then wither and die. But our hearts are like grass in the springtime, They live on and forever grow green again."

Another major, ancient Empire in South America, was based on Peru. This was the home of the Incas and their Andean capital, Cusco. Like Aztecs and others of South America, they had a religion which included human sacrifice. Unlike the Aztecs teotl, for the Incas it was the God Viracocha who created all living things. Their moral code was not based on any Godly exhortation so it could be thought to be an ethical philosophy. Incas said: Do not steal; Do not lie; Do not be lazy.

41. Anselm, Saint of Canterbury

1033 to 1109 CE Medieval, Platonic Aristotelian

Anselm was born in Italy. He became a monk, studying under a great French logician, Lanfranc. His extreme cleverness helped him become Archbishop of Canterbury in 1093.

Some 600 years before Anselm Augustine had tried to argue proof of God. Anselm attempted his own weird sounding truth, an ontological explanation It goes like this: Think of some being which is the greatest that could exist; but this is in your mind only, something that exists in reality must be greater than in that in the mind; so, as God is the greatest conceivable entity he must exist both in the mind and in reality. It sounds a little daft but interestingly the idea has lasted. Rene Descartes 500 years after Anselm picked up the baton as did Spinoza. Later still it was Kant who gave this argument its title although he disagreed with it. Even in modern times philosophers ponder over it and some

p.314

cosmologists use a form of it to explain what came before the Big Bang. Anselm also considered the question of free will. But the ontological argument was only one of several that Anselm put forward as proof of God’s existence. He also claimed that there must be something that is supremely good through which all goodness stems.

42. Abelard, Peter

1079 to 1142 CE Medieval, Nominalism? Conceptualism?

Abelard apparently had everything going for him. He was born into nobility and was brilliant and charming. He was a poet, composer, theologian and philosopher. Abelard even invented an ism, without knowing it, i.e. nominalism. Under this doctrine the various objects labelled by the same general term have nothing in common but their name. This is at odds with Plato. The latter thought that such universal words had a unique and perfect form. However, Abelard said these universals are more individualistic. As an example, things called Cat have nothing in common but the name, according to Abelard. However, he seemed to vary his position and sometimes move towards the middle ground of conceptualism, where universals, like cat, have a may have no real existence, but exist in the mind. He was trying to make the point that we have to be very careful with use of language.

Abelard was more famous for his love of Héloise D’Argenteuil than for his philosophy. Sadly, this was a disaster. He had everything except that of his heart’s greatest desire, the lovely Héloise, but Abelard was not allowed to have Héloise. To make certain this was so he was cruelly castrated and she sent to reflect in a nunnery. This really spiked his guns. Poor Peter, he really should have stuck to philosophy.

Like other philosophers he considered the issue of how to obtain true knowledge: “The key to wisdom is this - constant and frequent questioning, for by doubting we are led to question and by questioning we arrive at the truth.” Socrates would approve.

Abelard’s interest in words and names was given a much more thorough analysis by Wittgenstein in his philosophy of ordinary language. No doubt he had a string of very vivid words to describe those who had treated him and Heloise so badly.

p.315

43. Averroes, or Ibn Rushd

1126 to 1198 CE Arabic, Aristotelianism

Averroes, sometimes known as The Commentator, led the way in challenging the fundamentalist views of his predecessor Al-Ghazali. The latter’s work, “Incoherence of Philosophers”, some 50 years before, had slated all efforts to link philosophy to Islam. Al-Ghazali was particularly critical of the writings of Avicenna. Everyone who tried to forge a link to ancient Greek philosophers was branded a heretic by Al-Ghazali. Then along came the exceptionally brilliant Averroes who offered some support for Avicenna.

His base, was in free-thinking Cordoba, Spain. At the time parts of Spain were under the rule of Arab conquerors. Fortunately for Averroes the ruling Almoahades caliphate was open-minded. This allowed him to ponder, relatively unhindered, thoughts that rigid Islamists might consider heretical.

Apart from philosophy his polymathic reach extended to medicine, physics, astronomy, law and psychology. Averroes thought that parts of the Qur’an could not be taken as the literal truth. Naturally, this made him an enemy for the rigid Islamic devotees. He made a great study of philosophy and tried to fuse the thinking of Aristotle into support for Islamic beliefs and the Qur’an in an acceptable way. His interpretation of Aristotle seemed to suggest he thought “The Philosopher” (as Aristotle was known) claimed people were immortal only in the sense of a shared intellect. Averroes considered that only the educated elite should study philosophy. It’s only this group that is capable of understanding how philosophy supports and is not in conflict with religious beliefs. For everyone else, said Averroes, they should simply accept the Qur’an as it was written and not get concerned about interpretation. Essentially, he claimed that some parts of the Qur’an should be interpreted as allegorical and philosophy should be used in their interpretation. He identified statements in the Qur’an as poetic truths and not to be taken literally. Naturally, this made him an enemy of the rigid Islamic devotees, who were mostly based in Bagdad.

What Averroes did was to make a great study of philosophy and look for ideas that could illuminate Qur’anic teaching. In the process, he tried to fuse the thinking of Aristotle especially, and Plato with the Qur’an so they supported Islamic beliefs in an acceptable way.

p.316

He wrote a paper: Decisive Treatise on the Agreement Between “Religious Law and Philosophy” (Faṣl al-Maḳāl) and “The Incoherence of the Incoherence” (Tahāfut al-Tahāfut). The latter was a direct refutation of Al-Ghazali work titled “The Incoherence of Philosophers”. Averroes detailed interpretation of Aristotle seemed to suggest he considered The Philosopher had claimed people were immortal only in the sense of a shared intellect. He clearly was quite dismissive of the stubborn religious establishment who would not accept anything other than literal application of their creed. He said of them: “The world is divided into men who have wit and no religion and men who have religion and no wit.” In Averroes opinion the ‘jumhur al Ulama’ (majority of Islamic scholars) should not claim that it was against the law set down in the Qur’an or Hadith to study philosophy: “After logic we must proceed to philosophy proper. Here too we have to learn from our predecessors, just as in mathematics and law. Thus, it is wrong to forbid the study of ancient philosophy. Harm from it is accidental, like harm from taking medicine, drinking water, or studying law.” For two years, his books were banned and he was sent into exile.

Averroes held some interesting views about the ability of women to understand him. He thought that women should be treated better: “Women should be treated as human beings, not as domestic animals.” This view of his did not extend to women being the equal of men. In some matters of Sharia Law, he considered a woman’s testimony was only worth half that of a man.

The three decades Averroes spent in analysing and commenting on the work of Aristotle did not have much impact at the time. Later he heavily influenced the Christian philosopher and theologian Thomas Aquinas who called Averroes “The Commentator”, in recognition of his detailed analysis of Aristotle.

44. Zhu, Xi

1130 to 1200 CE Rationalist, Neo-Confucian

A Neo-Confucian who trod a different path to other Neo-Cs. Confucius wrote a lot of philosophical advice and before Zhu Chinese scholars had considered Confucius’ contribution to the great I Ching was the most important to study. I Ching is the mysterious “Book of Changes” which expressed an ethical philosophy. Confucius contributed to it but it is not known who wrote the original.

p.317

Zhu however downplayed I Ching and put his emphasis on Confucius’ The Four Books: (1) Great Learning; (2) Doctrine of the Mean; (3) The Analects of Confucius (i.e. The moral code by which Confucius thought people should live) and (4) Mencius. Zhu’s interpretations were considered so important that The Four Books were the basis of Chinese classical education and of civil service exams up to 1905. In essence Zhu said that everything is brought into existence through two bases of reality: qi, the vital physical force and li the principle laws. The totality of li he referred to as Taiji which he thought also was at the core of human souls. Qi and li he said work together. He thought people were born basically good and only changed through a combination of family, and circumstances and how they employed their talents.

45. Maimonides, Moses

1138 to 1204 CE Jewish, Aristotelianism

Here was a man born in Spain, died in Egypt and has a tomb in Israel. Maimonides thought that the Torah was a source of philosophical wisdom and that included the creation and revelation elements. He wrote a treatise on Jewish law which is still in use. He was strong on advice in all areas and thought a logical, reasoned approach was the way: “Consequently he who wishes to attain to human perfection, must therefore first study Logic, next the various branches of Mathematics in their proper order, then Physics, and lastly Metaphysics.” He also warned about the danger of accepting something just because it is written down: “Do not consider it proof just because it is written in books, for a liar who will deceive with his tongue will not hesitate to do the same with his pen” On personal conduct he said: “We are obligated to be more scrupulous in fulfilling the commandment of charity than any other positive commandment because charity is the sign of a righteous man.” He said God was the creator but was indefinable.

46. Magnus, Albertus also Saint Albert the Great and Albert of Cologne

1206 to 1280 CE Scholasticism

German philosopher of the middle ages. Apparently, the Blessed Virgin Mary persuaded Albert to enter Holy Orders. He became a Dominican friar and later a Bishop.

p.318

Albert tried to reconcile the philosophy of Aristotle with Christian beliefs. This is very similar to Averroes who attempted to show Aristotle offered support for Islam. He also studied and was influenced by Plato. His intellectual reach extended beyond philosophy and theology. Albert had an interest in astrology, chemistry and music. He is credited with discovering arsenic. Hopefully, he did not use this on his opponents.

With music, he sought to explore ways in which music worked on the soul. Interestingly though, he rejected the Pythagorean idea of music of the spheres. Some claim he was the greatest German philosopher and theologian of the middle ages. It may be there was not a lot of competition in this period. In grateful recognition of his work Albert was beatified by the Catholic church in 1622 CE. 300 years later, he was upgraded to sainthood by his canonization in 1931 CE. In one of his works, “De Natura boni and De bono”, Albert explores justice and what it is to be good. Probably his most important argument was that knowledge should be obtained from two sources: 1. Revelation and faith, 2. Philosophy and science. Albert said these sources were not contradictory. For him though there were some mysteries that had to be accepted by faith alone. Albert believed in the immortality of individual souls. This was different to the more all-embracing concept of Averroes and Avicenna. His student, Aquinas proved to be another great philosopher in support of a monotheistic God.

47. Aquinas, Saint Thomas

1225 to 1274CE Christian, Aristotelianism

Aquinas was a Dominican friar born in Sicily (not born as a friar, that came later). He achieved such renown for his knowledge and reasoning skill that he became ad-hoc advisor, on behalf of the Dominican order, to both the pope and French King Louis V111. For several years he travelled in France and Italy lecturing alongside his friend, St Bonaventure. Thomas was made a saint himself by the Catholic church and held up as a model to those aspiring to the priesthood. But he did not reach sainthood without quite a debate in the church over his Aristotle linked teachings. First, in 1270, the Bishop of Paris issued an edict against all teachings derived either from Aristotle or Arabic philosophers such as Averroes. Then in 1277, after his death, the Bishops of both Paris and Oxford went further. They condemned Thomas’ writings as heretical. These, they said, did not follow the strict Augustinian interpretations beloved by the Catholic church, especially the idea

p.319

that humans could never fully understand God. He was posthumously excommunicated, although it’s doubtful that this bothered Aquinas!

It was only in 1324, fifty years after Aquinas had died, that Pope John XX11 overturned these previous Bishoply edicts and made Aquinas a saint. His main work was “Summa Theologiae”. He was a great believer in the power of God to influence man’s pursuit of knowledge, either directly or indirectly through his senses or mental capacity: “that for the knowledge of any truth whatsoever man needs divine help, that the intellect may be moved by God to its act.” He also thought we are not capable of entirely understanding God.

He made a close study of Aristotle, but In Aquinas’s time and before there was concern about Aristotle’s metaphysical conclusions. Aristotle was greatly respected, and theologians did not want to reject completely his ideas but how could these possibly support Christian beliefs? Aquinas addressed these issues and made great attempts to establish several logical arguments purporting to prove the existence of God. There were five in all: God as the Unmoved Mover, the First Cause, the Necessary Being, the Absolute Being and the Grand Designer. The main two could be considered as, in type, these arguments: teleological; cosmological.

His teleological argument said that “When nature is observed it is seen to follow certain laws. These laws are ordained by God.” Aquinas acknowledged Aristotle’s view about the eternal universe and of his ethics. But to bend this eternal concept to a Christian viewpoint he said that God created the universe in such a way so that it had always existed, although, he tries to argue in a tortured fashion that the universe is not infinite. It has always existed, by God’s hand, but won’t last forever, so says Aquinas. This sort of argument helps to fit in neatly with what the Bible says.

God was the unmoved mover, according to Aquinas, just like Aristotle’s claim of a Prime Mover. This argument proposes that everything has a cause and going back to the very beginning there must be a first cause; a cosmological argument. Aquinas was critical of Anselm’s ontological argument saying: “not everyone who hears this word ‘God’ understands it to signify something than which nothing greater can be thought, seeing that some have believed God to be a body.”

Western philosophy was advanced by Aquinas. He was a prolific writer on many philosophical topics, not just those to support a belief in God. He covered areas such as ethics, law, government and epistemology.

p.320

Today, a number of Catholics rate Aquinas as the church’s greatest philosopher and theologian.

48. Ockham, William

1285 to 1349 CE Scholasticism, nominalism

Ockham was quite a sharp cookie, operating right at the cutting edge of philosophy, so to speak. He was both a theologian and a philosopher and taught at Oxford in the 12th century. Many people will have heard of Ockham’s Razor. For those that haven’t this is nothing to do with either Gillette or Wilkinson. Occam’s razor is an expression which captures William’s idea about how to arrive at the most likely solution or conclusion to a question.

He said; shave it down until you get to the simplest possible explanation. William despised academics who came up with convoluted ideas and concepts. Peel away to arrive at the simplest answer was his approach. This was much more likely to be correct than a complicated answer, so said Mr Ockham. What he actually iterated was: “With all things being equal the simplest explanation tends to be the right one.”

Ockham’s Razor has subsequently been more influential in science than philosophy. Philosophers like things to be complicated. Following a long, narrow, twisted path of alleged logical thinking is meat and drink to most philosophers. He believed in a one above and thought God was the origin of all moral advice. Ockham was technically a nominalist and scholastic, dealing with the meaning of language and problems of logic. He also revealed his belief that people should be allowed freedom to think what they like: “Purely philosophical assertions which do not pertain to theology should not be solemnly condemned or forbidden by anyone, because in connection with such [assertions] anyone at all ought to be free to say freely what pleases him.” A democrat and a liberal one at that?

p.321

49. Erasmus, Desiderius

1466 to 1536 CE Scepticism, Humanism

Erasmus was born in Rotterdam, the Netherlands. He travelled around quite a bit, spending time in Paris, Oxford and Cambridge. During his stay in Cambridge he was Margaret Professor of Divinity at Queens College and Professor of Greek, teaching in Cambridge from 1510 to 1515 CE but did not entirely enjoy his time there, complaining about the cold, the weather, English ale and lack of wine. Why this Dutchman should complain so about English weather is hard to understand. Holland is not renowned for its warm sunny climate. It has a lot of watery canals for a reason.

Fortunately for posterity, he expressed many thoughts other than complaints. Either Erasmus could be considered as the founding father of humanism or as the philosopher who kick started the Protestant Reformation. Perhaps he deserves both accolades, although Erasmus’ humanism was of a sort of Godly variety rather than atheistically based. Erasmus was sceptical of people generally and theologians and philosophers in particular. He was particularly critical of the Catholic church and what he saw as its wide-spread corruption. Erasmus thought people like Augustine and Aquinas had tried to be too clever in attempting to draw together the ideas of ancient Greek philosophers with Christian theology.

His main work was called “In Praise of Folly”, written in satirical fashion and full of Erasmus’s dry humour. Folly is described as a Goddess, the daughter of the God of wealth and the nymph Freshness. Folly’s two nymph nursemaids are named Inebriation and Ignorance. We can get something of what he is driving at. But the humour does not disguise his serious intent. He encouraged people to listen critically, “don’t listen with ears …you use for preachers of sermons, but the ears you usually prick up for mountebanks, clowns and fools”. A main part of “In Praise of Folly” was an attack on what Erasmus saw as the corruption in the Catholic church and the superstition it fostered.

Erasmus said that there should be a return to simple, basic religious beliefs with people forming their own individual relationship with God. Erasmus should perhaps be the saint of teachers, or librarians. He was very keen that people should read books and learn. This does seem to be at some variance with his claim that there was no point in seeking to obtain wisdom because this was something unattainable. It maybe he thought ultimate wisdom can only lie with God.

p.322

Continuing with his exhortation to learn he said: “A constant element of enjoyment must be mingled with our studies, so that we think of learning as a game rather than a form of drudgery, for no activity can be continued for long if it does not to some extent afford pleasure to the participant.” and “Do not be guilty of possessing a library of learned books while lacking learning yourself.” To this day, Erasmus unknowingly encourages the pursuit of learning through the Erasmus funding programme. Many European students who wish to study abroad do so because of the Erasmus programme.

He could have influenced Darwin through his professed evolutionary beliefs in his book “Zoonomia”. Here he said that all life had a common ancestor. This ancestor branched off into all today’s species.

50. Machiavelli, Niccolo

1469 to 1527 CE Political, Realism

Machiavelli was born in Florence during the Italian Renaissance. Niccolo was a man of many parts. He was a Florentine infantry captain, a magistrate, diplomat, historian, humanist, politician and philosopher. In history he is renowned for his tortuous, underhand scheming. Such conduct has been given a new word in English “Machiavellian”.

Some claim he is the founder of modern political science. His political philosophy was described in his book “The Prince”. In this Machiavelli describes how a ruler should operate in order to remain in power. A combination of deviousness and unscrupulous cunning was required according to Niccolo. He said: “It is better for a ruler to be feared than to be loved.” and “If an injury has to be done to a man it should be so severe that his vengeance need not be feared.” His emphasis on the importance of being both cunning and powerful is shown by: “The lion cannot protect himself from traps, and the fox cannot defend himself from wolves. One must therefore be a fox to recognize traps, and a lion to frighten wolves.” For Niccolo in politics it was essential to act from a position of strength and to take quick, ruthless and decisive action when required; the ends justified the means. Machiavelli met and admired Cesare Borgia. He recommended some of Borgia’s methods for dealing with enemies. He also gave advice on the importance of being analytically bold and taking action, rather than sitting back and acting the wimp: "All courses of action are risky, so prudence is not in avoiding danger (it’s impossible), but calculating risk and acting decisively. Make mistakes of

p.323

ambition and not mistakes of sloth. Develop the strength to do bold things, not the strength to suffer." He certainly could be considered the present-day political spin doctor’s much studied and admired saint.

51. Montaigne, Michel de

1533 to 1592 CE Humanism, modern scepticism

Like a number of other philosophers, Montaigne had an unusual childhood. His father insisted that his young son be spoken to only in Latin. His interest in Latin may stem from his respect for the ancient Roman philosopher, Seneca, about whom Montaigne said: “Seneca’s virtue shows forth so live and vigorous in his writings, and the defence is so clear there against some of these imputations, as that of his wealth and excessive spending, that I would not believe any testimony to the contrary.” In his day Montaigne was known more as a statesman than a writer, but he turned out to be one of the most important scribes of the French Renaissance. He was a French essayist, humanist, moralist and recognised as the reviver and maybe even the father of modern scepticism. In his book, “Essays” Montaigne appeared to show that he was not a great believer in reason, concluding that any current understandings were only transient. This is a little unusual for a philosopher. He saw things as full of uncertainties.

Michel (we know him well enough by now to call him by his first name) had a high disregard for religious orthodoxy and for other conventional views of his time cynically but cleverly claiming: “Man is certainly stark mad; he cannot make a worm, and yet he will be making gods by dozens.” He spurned reverence for past great philosophers, saying this of Socrates: “There is nothing more notable in Socrates than that he found time, when he was an old man, to learn music and dancing, and thought it time well spent.”

Michel revealed his own high ethical standards when he said: “I do a greater injury in lying than I do him of whom I tell a lie.” He was quite an essay writer too. He accepted that views can change with increased knowledge: “How many things served us yesterday for articles of faith, which today are fables for us?” Perhaps he was warning against the danger of holding fixed, dogmatic opinions? Montaigne obviously not only had a sense of humour he considered some form of cheerfulness essential: “On the highest throne in the world, we still sit only on our own bottom.”; “A good marriage would be between a blind wife and a deaf husband.” and "The most certain sign of wisdom is

p.324

cheerfulness." It maybe that Montaigne’s type of humour, which had an underlying ethical dimension, was the reason he influenced so many later writers like: Descartes, Pascal, Rousseau, Waldo Emerson, Nietzsche and possibly novelist/playwrights, William Shakespeare and Isaac Asimov.

52. Bacon, Francis

1561 to 1626 CE Empiricism

Francis was a product of Cambridge, having attended Trinity College. Cambridge has been home to several other brilliant philosophers and scientists over the centuries. When not philosophising, he worked as Lord Chancellor and was made Viscount of St Albans.

The jury is divided on Francis. Was he a scheming manipulator, an English Machiavelli, with very odd views, or was he a brilliant operator at the heart of government with a ground-breaking method of obtaining truth and knowledge? We are interested in the latter. Here is this Renaissance philosopher who helped establish a new, more analytical method of obtaining truths. He said: “…the greatest change I introduce is in the form of induction which shall analyse experience and take it to pieces, and by a process of exclusion and rejection lead to an inevitable conclusion.” and “Read not to contradict and confute, not to believe and take for granted, not to find talk and discourse, but to weigh and consider.”

Bacon viewed science (meant as wisdom in his day, what we know as science now was called natural philosophy in Bacon’s time) as a way of understanding and dominating the natural world. His methodology was at odds with accepted opinion at the time. This was based on the Aristotelian method of authoritative argument by clever people. These, Aristotle said, should discuss a subject long enough for the truth to emerge. The great Aristotle had held sway for 2,000 years, but Bacon saw this approach as unsupported speculation. He said there were four false idols which got in the way of an objective study of nature. These were: the idols of the tribe, the idols of the cave, the idols of the marketplace and the idols of the theatre. By these he meant, that humans (the tribe) have senses that are fallible. Our senses and mind give us a false impression of the real world, so he thought (a bit like Plato’s cave, Kant, Montaigne and modern-day phenomenologists). His major work, was Novum Organon (New Tool) published in 1620. In religion Bacon leant towards puritanism. He rejected atheism saying: "It is true, that a little philosophy

p.325

inclineth man’s mind to atheism, but depth in philosophy bringeth men’s minds about to religion."

Bacon also revealed he had quite a sense of humour: “A bachelor’s life is a fine breakfast, a flat lunch, and a miserable dinner.” and “I will never be an old man. To me, old age is always 15 years older than I am.”

This combination of hard-headed realism, deep analytical thinking and humour make Francis a man to be admired. He definitely brings home the bacon I think. He even has had postage stamps in his honour. There was a Newfoundland one for six cents. He would be a bit miffed at being identified with this seemingly paltry sum. And in 1961 there was a Romanian stamp celebrating the 500th anniversary of his birth. In advancing philosophical thought, Francis certainly brought home the bacon. He put his stamp on key issues - apologies!

53. Galileo Galilei

1564 to 1642 CE Metaphysics

Galileo, an Italian, born in Pisa, is famously known for his astronomy. He made pioneering observations of the sky. Subsequent calculations led him to support the Copernican view that the Earth was not the centre of the universe and merely one of several planets that orbited the Sun. At the time, the Catholic church treated this view as a heresy. For them, God had centred his universe on planet Earth. It was dangerous for Galileo to publicise a contrary view. As he knew, Giordano Bruno had been burnt by the inquisition in 1600 for refusing to retract his heretical opinions in agreement with Copernicus. However, Galileo just could not deny the accuracy of his observations, called heliocentrism, and he went ahead and published them in: “Dialogue Concerning the Two Chief World Systems”. Later, he retracted his views under threats from the feared Inquisition, and spent the rest of his life under house arrest. But it was too late for Catholic authorities and their scripture based certainties, Galileo had quite definitely let the cat out of the bag, after Copernicus and Bruno had untied it.

Galileo was much more than an astronomer, gaining prominence in both mathematics and physics. His work became the foundation for modern physics. His inclusion in the list of philosopher greats is because of some of his thinking about the fundamental role of mathematics in the world.

p.326

This is something after the ancient Greek Pythagoras. Galileo’s commentary is important in philosophical, and metaphysical terms. He said: "Philosophy is written in that great book which ever lies before our eyes — I mean the universe — but we cannot understand it if we do not first learn the language and grasp the symbols, in which it is written.

This book is written in the mathematical language, and the symbols are triangles, circles and other geometrical figures, without whose help it is impossible to comprehend a single word of it; without which one wanders in vain through a dark labyrinth." and “What has philosophy got to do with measuring anything? It’s the mathematicians you have to trust, and they measure the skies like we measure a field.” and: “Mathematics is the alphabet with which God has written the universe.” Galileo was broadly supporting the much earlier view of the pre-Socratic Greek philosopher Pythagoras. Today, there is an assessment speculated by Max Tegmark that mathematics is at the base of the universe.

Galileo’s reference to God might make it seem as though he was a keen supporter of all Catholic Godly beliefs, but not so: “I think that in the discussion of natural problems we ought not to begin with the Scriptures, but with experiments and demonstrations.” Galileo died of heart problems in 1642, aged 77. In 1992, 350 years later, Catholic Pope John Paul 11 admitted that Galileo was right. The Catholic church finally accepted that the Earth did indeed go around the Sun. Phew, the Sun must be relieved to know it’s not just a large satellite of planet Earth.

54. Hobbes, Thomas

1588 to 1679 CE Political

Hobbes was an Englishman, born in Wiltshire. He was 38 at the time Bacon died so, not surprisingly, they knew each other. Apparently, Hobbes worked contentedly with Bacon. They both had an interest in government. But, unlike Bacon, Hobbes, it was claimed at the time, was an atheist. This however was not made clear in his writings. It may be his lack of acceptance of the established church norms made him labelled atheist. The term Hobbism was a byword for atheism so he had obviously upset church leaders with his unorthodox views. He irritated the church by claiming the king was in charge of interpretation of the scriptures. He had strong views on government and said the monarch had an absolute right to rule. He also said that the source of the monarch’s power was not God but the people. With these views, he annoyed parliamentarians.

p.327

His most famous work was “Leviathan”. In this he established his political philosophy which purported the rights of the individual and the natural equality of all men. It also claimed that political power, to be legitimate, must be representative and with the consent of the people.

He also said that people should be free to do whatever the law does not specifically forbid. Some of this seems to be at odds with his claim that the monarch has a right to rule. Many of Hobbes views would not be out of place in modern, Western democracies. He can be seen as the founder of the social contract related to what he saw as the laws of nature. Hobbes said the first of nature’s laws is that people seek the peace and fear death. But to achieve peace and reduce the chance of death requires people to give up some of their natural individual freedoms and obtain a mutual agreement in the form of a social contract. An absolute ruler would be given powers, set out in the contract, by the people. He was in effect arguing for laws that controlled people actions in relation to others and treaties between individual states instead of the alternative, endless war.

55. Descartes, Rene

1596 to 1650 CE Dualism, Scepticism

Rene was a Frenchman born in La Hye en Touraine, since renamed Descartes in his honour. Rene spent twenty years in the Dutch Republic, and served in the Dutch army. He has been given the accolade as the father of modern, Western philosophy. Here was another brilliant mathematician, as well as a philosopher. He constructed a form of mathematics known as Cartesian. Newton used this to invent calculus. He was also pragmatic; having decided that he agreed with both Copernicus and Galileo that the Earth orbited the sun and not the other way around. He took care to keep back his book that mentioned this, after he saw what the Inquisition did to Galileo and much worse to poor Giovanni Bruni.

His calculating, mathematical mind stood him in good stead in his philosophical endeavours, which he propounded in his major work, “Meditations”. He wanted to prove that he existed and was not subject to misleading control by some evil demon. His conclusion he summarised by saying “I think therefore I am”, or in Latin, the language of the intelligentsia in Rene’s time, “Cogito ergo sum.” Je pense dans je suis, in his native French. This is his famous proof of his own existence.

p.328

Descartes said that because he was aware of his own existence then this was a basic truth upon which he could build without self-doubt. He was not the originator of this idea, just the most famous proponent, because the way he expressed it was so clear and so memorable. Nearly 2,000 years before him, Aristotle had said that we know when we are thinking or performing an action so we must also know we exist. 800 years after Aristotle we have Augustine stating that he knows that he exists, even if he thinks he is mistaken he must exist because he is thinking this.

Rene Descartes is sometimes called the father of modern philosophy. This was because of his work in describing the correct way to reason. This was big development for epistemology. His method of reasoning followed four rules:

  1. Accept nothing as true which is not presented to the mind so clearly and distinctly that there is no reason to doubt it;
  2. break problems down into as many smaller problems as possible;
  3. begin with what is most simple and easily understood and build on this by degrees to larger and more complex matters;
  4. review the entire chain of thinking to ensure nothing is omitted.

Emphasising the importance of a sceptical approach as indicated in his first rule he said: “In order to seek truth, it is necessary once in the course of our life, to doubt, as far as possible, of all things.”

Rene also made an important distinction saying that the mind and body were separate. They were two distinct things, hence the term dualism. He led the way here for the modern exploration of the philosophy of mind.

Mixing with royalty was the death of him. In giving philosophy lessons to Queen Christina of Sweden he had to get up before 5.0am. After 10 days of this he caught pneumonia and died in Stockholm but, because of his French eminence, he is buried in the Abbey of Saint-Germain-des-Prés, Paris

56. Pascal, Blaise

1623 to 1662 CE Cover-all-bases-ism

Pascal was not only a French philosopher he was also a great mathematician; yet another in the pantheon of philosopher-mathematicians. His philosophy was reflected in the “Pensées” (thoughts). To help his father, a tax collector, he invented the first digital calculator. He also devised the

p.329

foundations of probability theory. In the process, exchanging views with Fermat. They had similar, but independently developed ideas on the topic. He also wrote a treatise on the relationship between liquids and gases. Later on, this was an important foundational work in the progress of hydraulics. Blaise was very religious, a Catholic who was a strong proponent of Jansenism. This was a strict Augustine based teaching that denied human free will, believing in pre-destination and that human nature was incapable of good. This movement was condemned as heretical by the Catholic Church.

It Pascal’s interest in probability theory may have led him to his famous wager. He said that it is not possible to have certain proof that God exists. However, betting that God exists could lead to a life in the hereafter of heavenly bliss. Insisting, during one’s lifetime that there is no God may be correct, but is at the huge possible cost of eternal damnation if you are wrong. He was an anti-rationalist, who challenged Descartes and he thought faith and reason were independent of each other. He was very sure the only root to happiness was to follow God: “There are only three types of people; those who have found God and serve him; those who have not found God and seek him, and those who live not seeking, or finding him. One of his less famous but very pertinent sayings about the basis of faith is: "“People almost invariably arrive at their beliefs not on the basis of proof but on the basis of what they find attractive.” This is so true Blaise, although the description of “attractive” needs teasing out. He fitted quite a lot into his relatively short 39 years, before dying of a brain tumour in his sister’s Parisian house.

57. Spinoza, Baruch or Benedictus

1632 to 1677 CE Monism, Rationalism

The Dutchman Spinoza is recognised as a leading figure of The Enlightenment, perhaps the most important originator of this ground-breaking period of change. Hegel later said: “You are either a ‘Spinozist’ or not a philosopher at all.”

In the 17th century Spinoza helped usher in a time when reason and a new, scientific approach to knowledge began to supplant established beliefs founded on superstition, and religious dogma. The idea of a people forming a nebulous grouping called “society” with some influence on established authority was born.

p.330

He was a brave figure, a Jew who went against the traditional Jewish teachings. In 1656 he was formally excommunicated, by the Jewish-Portuguese community in Amsterdam where he lived.

He had gone against traditional, Torah based, Jewish teachings. Benedictus had no respect at all for the religious chiefs saying: “The supreme mystery of despotism, its prop and stay, is to keep men in a state of deception, and with the specious title of religion to cloak the fear by which they must be held in check, so that they will fight for their servitude as if for salvation.”

The Catholic church banned his works. What did Spinoza write that warranted such severe censure, apart from the above quote? For him a monotheistic God, which he also referred to as nature, existed in everything and everyone and was eternal. Spinoza’s God was very different from the traditional Jewish or Christian transcendent creator of the universe. His God was a sort of all-encompassing cosmic Gaia figure. HE was in all of nature. There may even be a touch of Aristotle in this type of thinking. Benedictus’ approach was directly opposed to Descartes mind-body dualism. He said us humans could only find happiness by using reason to understand their place in this universal Godliness. His main work was “The Ethics”, a seminal book with five sections:

  • (1) God, nature and the meaning of substance;
  • (2) Nature of the mind;
  • (3) Knowledge;
  • (4) Passion and action;
  • (5) Virtue and happiness.

He is unusual among all philosophers in claiming that it was possible to know everything by self -reflexion because we are a part of the absolute whole of reality.

58. Locke, John

1632 to 1704 CE Empiricism, Political

John Locke was born in the village of Wrington, Somerset and later educated at Westminster School then Christ Church, Oxford. Locke was an important early figure in the Enlightenment period, just like his contemporary Spinoza. They were born on the same day but their birthday was about all they had in common. Spinoza was more into metaphysics and ethics while Locke’s philosophy focused on political concepts.

p.331

John was perhaps the greatest English philosopher; he had huge influence. Voltaire said Locke was “…the man of the greatest wisdom. What he has not seen clearly, I despair of ever seeing.” He was a true liberal, although his views on women are arguable. His empiricism pre-dated figures like Hume and Berkeley and Locke’s political ideas were a backcloth to both the French and American revolutions. Thomas Jefferson admired him and he influenced Thomas Paine, both these were major figures in the American revolution. Jefferson, in the United Declaration of Independence changed Locke’s emphasis on “life, liberty and property” He substituted “pursuit of happiness” in place of “property”.

Locke was an advocate of religious toleration, who argued for the rights of the individual and against authoritarianism. These individual rights included the right for people to defend themselves, their property and liberty. But people also had corresponding obligations too; not to hurt, others or their property and freedom. People should not simply accept unthinkingly what the established authorities - the state and the church - told them. Instead they should use their experience and reasoning powers to arrive at the truth according to Locke. They should, as a matter of convenience, appoint independent judges to defend their natural rights.

The link between the people and their appointed judges would be in the form of a social contract. He set out these thoughts in his great works: “Two Treatises of Government” and “An Essay Concerning Human Understanding”. These ideas are in contrast to Hobbes who thought there should be a single authoritarian power, a Leviathan. Locke’s emphasis on an empirical approach to knowledge explains his admiration for the scientific reasoning of his friend, the Irish scientist Robert Boyle and for Isaac Newton. He humbly said he was an “under labourer to Newton.” Locke did not believe in innate knowledge or that reasoning was the sole route to truth - the rationalist view of Descartes. One of his quotes typifies his thought that the search for knowledge and truth should be paramount for all people: “To love truth for truth’s sake is the principal part of human perfection in this world, and the seed-plot of all other virtues.” and “Reading furnishes the mind only with materials of knowledge; it is thinking that makes what we read ours.” His empirical approach to knowledge he sums up by saying: “All ideas come from sensation or reflection.” and “Whence has it all the materials of reason and knowledge? To this I answer, in one word, from EXPERIENCE. In that all our knowledge is founded; and from that it ultimately derives itself.”

He was a believer in God saying: “We are capable of knowing certainly that there is a God. Though God has given us no innate ideas of himself…”

59. Leibniz, Gottfried Wilhelm

1646 to 1716 CE Rationalism

Leibnitz was born in Leipzig, Germany. He was one of the greatest Renaissance thinkers. Like many of the very clever, sometimes geniuses, of ages long past Leibniz was an important innovator in several fields of endeavour. For example, Gottfried was a ground-breaking mathematician who introduced notation for calculus and he has a theorem named after him. To help him with his calculations he invented an early type of calculating machine. He thought this machine would be of benefit to astronomers saying: “It is unworthy of excellent men to lose hours like slaves in the labour of calculation which could safely be relegated to anyone else if machines were used”. Gottfried also expertly dabbled in physics, but he is perhaps best remembered for his philosophy. Here he is counted as a rationalist, giving us his views on Sufficient Reason.

Like Aquinas before him, he believed in a Cosmological argument for the existence of God. He differed slightly from Aquinas in the nature of his Cosmological argument. Leibniz asked: “Why is there any world at all and why is it the way it is?” He thought, using the principle of Sufficient Reason (a concept common with a number of earlier philosophers including Plato, Aristotle and Spinoza), that any contingent fact about the world must have an underlying explanation, in simpler terms this says that some things are true by the way things are, rather than because of logical proof, e.g. “all water is wet”, this is a contingent fact. The only possible answer to his question, so said Leibniz, was that God was behind it all. Leibniz quite firmly believed in God. He said: “Beyond the World, that is, beyond the collection of finite things, there is some One Being who rules the universe not only rules the world, but also fashions or creates it; he is the ultimate reason for things. For we cannot find in any of the individual things, or even in the entire collection and series of things, a sufficient reason for why they exist.”

In the final version of Leibniz’s universe there exists only God and soul-like entities he called “monads”. This has some broad similarity with Plato’s ideas of perfect forms and souls. With the Leibniz concept, everything, all material objects and even space and time, are just illusions. He recognised the difficulty posed by having an all knowing, all powerful and benevolent God alongside the existence of free-will and evil. He attempted to explain how the latter two concepts sit together with a God who has unlimited power and knowledge.

p.333

Gottfried’s elucidation is quite convoluted and definitely falls well short of a logical proof. His fame goes wider than maths and philosophy; he even has a Bahlsen chocolate biscuit named after him.

60. Astell, Mary

1666 to 1731 CE Feminism

This thoughtful lady could be counted as the first feminist, if Hypatia is seen more as a strong, capable woman. She deplored Aristotle, and what she thought were Locke’s comfortable view that women, the poor dears, had less reasoning power than men. Not true said Mary, this was the fault of expectation and education. Women were expected to be subservient to men, especially in marriage, and they were just not given the same level of education as men. She penned these words: “If all men are born free, why are all women born slaves.” She thought true knowledge can only come from reason. Mary was a sort of Royalist/Christian/Platonist who supported Descartes mind-body dualism.

61. Mandeville, Bernard

1670 to 1733 CE Ethics, egotism

This Dutchman moved to London when things got a little hot for him in Rotterdam. He was implicated there in a popular uprising. Bernard was a man who definitely could claim to think outside the box when it came to considerations of virtue and ethical behaviour. For him widespread adoption of Christian type virtue would be a bad thing for society as a whole. Bernard said vices, properly controlled, associated with the pursuit of self-gain, wealth and esteem, were the necessary engines of a powerful, successful nation. In other words, humans should act selfishly for the general spin-off benefit to society. Bernard was in a way claiming being bad was good! His views he set out in a poem entitled “The Grumbling Hive”: or, “Knaves turned Honest”. The poem appeared in a wider work entitled “The Fable of The Bees”, or “Private Vices, Publick Benefits”.

An example of Bernard’s argument - selfishness helps - can be translated to our modern world. Supposing no-one was selfish and greedy for more of everything? They were the complete, utter opposite. These super-virtuous people might say, we must live frugally and not squander resources.

p.334

They would turn off the central heating, make do with cold showers, eat plain food and only just enough of it to survive. Transport would be on foot or bicycle, rarely using any motorised version. Flying would be a no-no. Clothes would be worn until threadbare. Nothing new would be purchased unless absolutely necessary. If all our society followed this, what could be considered a virtuous regime, thousands of shops, restaurants, coffee shops, banks, leisure facilities and factories would close down. Air, bus, taxi and rail providers would go bankrupt. Tens of thousands of people would soon be out of work. The economy would collapse, scuppering the welfare state, especially social services and the NHS. People would die in large numbers.

Bernard started out his working life as a Doctor of medicine. He specialised in nerve and stomach disorders. His philosophical work certainly caused disorder and controversy, especially with the church. His views were unique. Thomas Hobbes’ philosophy, the social contract, is probably the closest to him, and that’s really at some distance. At further distance is the utilitarianism of Bentham and Mills. Bernard certainly set Scottish philosopher David Hume, and his fellow Scot the economist/philosopher Adam Smith, thinking hard. Were Bernard ever to be canonised, not much chance of this, he could be held up as the patron saint of modern capitalism and economic growth.

62. Berkeley, George

1685 to 1753 CE Immaterialism, Occasionalism

Bishop George Berkeley had a really weird view, certainly it was very odd for his time especially for someone who was an Irish Bishop. He reasoned strongly, that no matter, by that he meant that nothing of any material form, existed. That is, the Sun, Moon, Earth us humans were just not really there. Berkeley was an Irish Bishop, with an English father. He was one of the three most renowned empiricists of the 17th century, along with John Locke, whom he particularly admired, and David Hume. He admired Locke but did not agree with his materialism. The good Bishop held what could be thought of as strange metaphysical views. He set out his thesis in two similar works; “A Treatise Concerning the Principles of Human Knowledge” and later rewrote this in a more acceptable dialogue form. He called the later work “Three Dialogues between Hylas and Philonous”. These two words are Greek; hylas is Greek for matter and philonous defined as lover of mind. In these works, he purported that all that exists that is matter, only exists because it is in our mind, or is dependent on our mind for its existence.

p.335

This immaterialism, as he called it, claims that matter as a physical object does not exist - anti Locke, Democritus and others.

What he actually said was: “All the choir of heaven and furniture of earth - in a word, all those bodies which compose the frame of the world - have not any subsistence without a mind.” and “From my own being, and from the dependency I find in myself and my ideas, I do, by an act of reason, necessarily infer the existence of a God, and of all created things in the mind of God.” In essence, he considered that humans conceptualised their own reality. When at Trinity College, Dublin a fellow student lampooned Berkeley’s idea in a limerick about the existence or otherwise of a tree in the quad:

"There was a young man who said God, must find it exceedingly odd, when he finds that the tree, continues to be, when no one’s about in the Quad".

George cleverly replied in kind:

"Dear Sir, your astonishment’s odd, I’m always about in the Quad, And that’s why the tree, continues to be, Since observed by, yours faithfully, God."

Sadly, George’s seeming idea that something only exists because we imagine it does not seem to work when imagining a large suitcase filled with £50 notes.

63. Voltaire,

originally named François-Marie Arouet 1694 to 1778 CE Deism

Born in Paris Voltaire was a leading figure of the French Enlightenment. He became disconnected from his family, preferring to be called Voltaire, a name which was an anagram of his Latinised surname. He was a philosopher, historian and writer. A person renowned for his wit which he used to great effect in his attacks on the Catholic Church and other established religions including Islam. He criticised intolerance and religious dogma; expressing the need for freedom of religion, freedom of expression, and separation of church and state. But he respected the right of others to state views he did not share: “I do not agree with what you have to say, but I’ll defend to the death your right to say it.”

p.336

He wrote plays, including some focussed on Roman history and mythology; “Oedipus and Brutus”. Another, called “Mahomet”, took the siege of Mecca in 629 CE as its theme. His most important work was “Candide”. Many of his ideas were portrayed in this classic satire. Through Candide’s teacher, Dr Pangloss, he cleverly drew attention to religious apologies for the existence of evil, morally corrupt people and natural disasters. Why would a morally perfect God allow this, was Voltaire’s question.

Not surprisingly, he did not believe in a Catholic version of God, but supported the idea of natural religion, i.e. non-revelatory. In essence he was a deist: “What is faith? Is it to believe that which is evident? No. It is perfectly evident to my mind that there exists a necessary, eternal, supreme, and intelligent being. This is no matter of faith, but of reason.” In authoritarian France, his views earned him a spell of imprisonment in the Bastille. He negotiated his release accepting the condition of his exile to England. Among many pithy sayings are: “Love truth, but pardon error.”; “We should be considerate to the living; to the dead we owe only the truth.”; “Where there is friendship, there is our natural soil.” And humorously, “God must have a sense of humour, he invented sex.”

During Voltaire’s time, Scotland was developing a brand of humanism which Voltaire admired. He said: “We look to Scotland for all our ideas of civilisation”. Maybe he also had in mind the outpourings from his contemporary David Hume, the greatest Scottish philosopher. David was philosophising in Voltaire’s time. In the context of civilisation, what would Voltaire have made of haggis and chips, and deep-fried Mars bars?

64. Hume, David

1711 to 1776 CE Scepticism, Empiricism

David Hume was the greatest Scottish philosopher and a giant of his time. He went to Edinburgh university and later moved south to work as a clerk in Bristol. In 1743 it was off to La Fleche, France, where Descartes had been educated. Here he wrote his first and most important book, “A Treatise of Human Nature”. David was the sort of man who you might think would call a spade a bloody shovel. He never used those terms of course, but they indicate his clear, no-nonsense and determined style of argument. He was an empiricist. One of his greatest achievements was to inspire Immanuel Kant. Knowledge can only be gained through experience, so David thought: "In all

p.337

our reasoning’s concerning fact, there are all imaginable degrees of assurance. A wise man therefore proportions his belief to the evidence."

This idea opposed the stance taken by both Descartes and Leibnitz. These two were rationalist philosophers i.e. they said the reasoning ability of the human mind was more important to the pursuit of knowledge than experience alone. David did not completely discount reason, not surprisingly. Reasoning that was based on induction might lead to a truth but did not necessarily do so, said David. Such reasoning could only be conjectural, e.g. just because swans you see are white, you cannot say that all swans are white that is induced belief and cannot be a certainty. It is known as the is-ought problem and David’s rejection is called Hume’s law. This logic was at the core of David’s empiricist philosophy unlike Bacon and Locke. These two empiricists used the induction reasoning argument.

Causation, was another area he considered. He thought causation was simply a belief, a bit like induction. Important to understand the difference between induction and deduction. Induction is a bottom up method while deduction is top down. In induction, lots of things are gathered together from which, eventually, a general conclusion is reached. This differs from deductive reasoning which is a top down approach. It starts with a theory which is at first more clearly specified, then tested in some way before it is confirmed. The various stages of both look like this:

Inductive reasoning Deductive reasoning
Final Theory Theory Start point
Initial hypothesis Hypothesis
Pattern Observation
Observation Start point Confirmation

This chart makes it easier to see how induction can lead to an incorrect conclusion, as in the swans’ example.

David was also very much against the idea of innate knowledge, a concept originally promoted by Plato. In his forays into religious beliefs he also said:

"Epicurus’s old questions are still unanswered: “Is he (God) willing to prevent evil, but not able? Then he is impotent. Is he able, but not willing? then he is malevolent. Is he both able and willing? then whence evil?”

He argued scornfully against miracles. Needless to say, David was an atheist and one who had some doubt over his own self, where he found it difficult to have anything stronger than a perception of self, something like Descartes. His

p.338

greatest work, “A Treatise of Human Nature”, was largely ignored when first published. He then revised this in two smaller volumes, but again not much public reaction. It may be that he could only be fully appreciated at the time by such as Immanuel Kant. He later published a history book. That did have an impact and made people take more note of his philosophical ideas.

65. Rousseau, Jean-Jacques

1712 to1778 CE Political

Here was a man whose writings not only helped ignite the 1848 French revolution but also helped usher in the romanticism period. Romanticism, associated with nature, beauty and imagination was a contrast to the reasoned, scientific approach of the existing enlightenment period. He was born in Geneva but spent a great deal of time in France so Jean-Jacques is considered a French philosopher. Like Hobbes and Locke before him he was a proponent of the influential Social Contract theory. But Rousseau viewed human nature, the basis for the Social Contract, quite differently from Hobbes. He thought human nature essentially good, while Hobbes considered it brutal and in a state of war and savagery. Some quotes from Rousseau’s “The Social Contract”, the famous: “Man is born free yet everywhere he is in chains.”, and “Every man having been born free and master of himself, no one else may under any pretext whatever subject him without his consent.”, and “To assert that the son of a slave is born a slave is to assert that he is not born a man.”, lastly “In truth, laws are always useful to those with possessions and harmful to those who have nothing; from which it follows that the social state is advantageous to men only when all possess something and none has too much.”

Rousseau thought individuals should have both liberty and equality, although he recognised there could be a clash between the two. His views became the basis for the later French Republic’s famous motto: Liberté, Egalité, Fraternité. His strong anti-establishment writings made him unpopular with those in power. He was forced to flee arrest moving with the help of powerful sympathisers to Neuchâtel, a Canton of the Swiss Confederation.

He was a believer in God, thinking of him as the creator. He converted to Catholicism but later returned to Calvinism. Some modern feminists admire him. In his view women, although dependent on men, were cleverer. Jean-Jacques gave his thoughts on education in his book “Emile”, saying: “We should not teach children the sciences but give them a taste for them.”

p.339

Hume was an admirer and he influenced Kant on ethics. Jean-Jacques had an interesting view on war. “War is constituted by a relation between things, and not between persons…War then is a relation, not between man …” After his death Rousseau was considered a national hero and in 1794 was interred in the Panthéon in Paris.

66. Diderot, Denis

1713 to 1784 CE Encyclopaedist, Scepticism, Atheism

Denis was a French philosopher who was the major editor of an Encyclopaedia that brought together the philosophical and scientific knowledge of his time. Cleverly, he managed to upset royalists, atheists and the received Christianity of the time in his work: “Pensees Philosophiques” (Philosophical thoughts). In this work, he said: “Men will never be free until the last king is strangled with the entrails of the last priest.” A bit strong that Denis. Clearly you did not have much time for Kings or Bishops and their ilk. He also added, “The God of the Christians is a father who makes much of his apples, and very little of his children.” These two statements indicate why he annoyed the established church.

In 1746 “Pensees Philosophiques” was burned by order of the Parliament of Paris. He served 3 months in prison for his views. Denis was anti-establishment in a big way. He denied the divine right of kings and rejected the “argument by design” attempted proof of God. Instead he proposed that natural, biological evolution was the source of all. His ideas mirrored some of those of Rousseau, a contemporary. They were mildly argumentative friends. Of course, Denis lived in a new time, the Age of Reason also known as the Age of Enlightenment. From science there emerged a growing body of new knowledge. This was an age that loosened up brains and encouraged freer thinking.

In recent times, Denis has become an important historical figure to British Humanists, maybe because he said: “'Superstition is more injurious to God than atheism” and “Scepticism is the first step towards truth.” Denis gave us a large number of brilliant quotes. His friend Baron d’Holbach’s drew on Diderot’s observations when writing Système de la Nature, known as the bible of atheism. Empress Catherine of Russia must have liked and had some sympathy for the financially struggling Denis. She helped him out by paying for his Library, yet allowed him unhindered, continued use of it.

67. Baron d’Holbach

1723 to 1789 CE Atheism, materialism, metaphysics

Here was the third 18th century French philosopher who, like Rousseau and Diderot. d’Holbach, despised the professed religious and political wisdom of his time, The Baron lived and worked mostly in Paris. He was a renowned atheist and his philosophical views are founded in his atheistic beliefs, or rather his non-beliefs. The Baron’s determinism, materialism and atheism were expressed in his major works: “Système de la Nature” (System of Nature); “Le Christianisme Dévoilé” (Christianity Unveiled)“, and “Le Bon-sens” (Common Sense)”. These works created something of a storm, and like Diderot’s writings, they were publicly burnt by order of the Parliament of Paris. D’Holbach was careful to publish anonymously thus avoiding imprisonment – this might be the Age of Enlightenment but the religious establishment still had great power.

He held major social gatherings in Paris these were attended by great figures of the enlightenment and European establishment: the philosophers Voltaire, Rousseau and David Hume, the economist Adam Smith, historian Edward Gibbon, the writer Horace Walpole, the actor David Garrick and American the statesman and scientist Benjamin Franklin. Just imagine the intellectual firepower represented by these people. The conversation at the Baron’s soirees must have been quite something to listen to, both shocking and exciting. d’Holbach’s metaphysical views are summed up by his statement: “The universe, that vast assemblage of everything that exists, presents only matter and motion: the whole offers to our contemplation nothing but an immense, and uninterrupted succession of causes and effects.”

68. Smith, Adam

1723 to 1790 CE Political, economics, ethics

Adam Smith was a Scottish economist and philosopher. He was professor of logic then moral philosophy at Glasgow University. His contemporaries included David Hume and Voltaire. His economic influence extends right up to the modern day through his book: “Inquiry into the Nature and Causes of The Wealth of Nations”. In this work economics and philosophy cross over because in outlining the former he expresses a synthesis of economic and political

p.341

philosophy. He said: “Wherever there is great property there is great inequality. For one very rich man there must be at least five hundred poor, and the affluence of the few supposes the indigence of the many. The affluence of the rich excites the indignation of the poor, who are often both driven by want, and prompted by envy, to invade his possessions.”

Smith argued for free trade. He said that people should be set free to better themselves and that there should be a bare minimum of regulation over trade and commerce. This, he said would create greater prosperity for all. “The Wealth of Nations” was intended to be the first part of a complete theory of society, covering theology, ethics, politics and law. He expressed his views on these topics in a series of lectures, then published: “The Theory of Moral Sentiments”. In this he said that our moral sense must be based on mutual sympathy. Today this would be called mutual empathy.

In essence, in Smith’s two famous works, he was arguing for a form of ethical capitalism. Smith’s view differed from those before him like Shaftesbury who said people had a special moral sense. He had clearly read Mandeville and agreed broadly with Mandeville’s argument that human selfishness and a striving for more was good for society generally. However, Adam contended this had to be mitigated by application of a morality which helped all humans to benefit. He also was at odds with his friend David Hume’s concept that morals were a form of utility. Even today Adam’s views, especially his thoughts on economics and equality, are at the heart of much Western political debate and economic policy.

69. Kant, Immanuel

1724 to 1804 CE Transcendental idealism

Kant was a student and teacher of mathematics. Kant’s initial interest was in maths and science, particularly the works of Newton and Leibnitz. The former’s rational, scientific analysis was admired by Immanuel. It encouraged him to write on the subject of space and time. But his historical greatness is founded on his philosophical thoughts. Like several philosophers, Augustine for one, Kant was influenced by a devout and strict parent, his mother. His repressed sexuality and adherence to routine may have come from this.

Regularity was Kant’s watchword in the way he conducted his life. He was renowned for doing everything to a rigid, unchanging timetable.

p.342

He had his meals and did his daily walk to a fixed timetable in the little German town of Konigsberg where he lived and died (now it’s Russian Kalingrad).

There was nothing regular, or normal, about Kant when it came to philosophy though. His abilities in this area were abnormally brilliant. He was an immense figure in the philosophical world of the 18th century and right up to modern times. This was a German philosopher who acknowledged a great debt to David Hume. Kant said that reading Hume’s “Treatise of Human Nature” woke him from his philosophical slumbers. Rousseau was another philosopher who Kant studied.

Kant’s main written work was “The Critique of Pure Reason”. While admiring Hume, Kant was also worried about the destruction of metaphysics that Hume’s sceptical empiricism led to. It made him give a great deal of thought to an alternative philosophy. In the end, he was able to disagree with Hume’s empiricism and come up with different ideas. On causation, Kant said that what we believe, from cause to effect, was not based on prior knowledge alone. It was, said Kant, because of the very nature of our being. We saw it that way because of the filtering and categorisation process of the mind. Some of this process is of course based on actual experience, a posteriori knowledge, but things like intuition and a tendency to categorise were also at work in presenting the total picture. He said: “All our knowledge begins with the senses, proceeds then to the understanding, and ends with reason. There is nothing higher than reason.”

Kant gelled the rationalism and empiricism of previous philosophers into a new approach to reason and knowledge. Sometimes this is described as the golden middle between rationalism and empiricism but more technically it’s known as transcendental idealism.

Kant believed in God. His reasoning was based on a type of ontological argument: “The possibility of anything must be grounded in some real existent, and what exists prior to such possible existence would have to be a necessary existence that we call ‘God’.” He thought it helped ordinary people to have a sound moral base by believing too. He said it was logical that humans should be moral creatures, avoid cruelty to animals, and follow what he referred to as a categorical imperative. i.e. something morally correct that an ethical person must do regardless of the circumstances. Human morals were founded on reason thought Kant. He went on to say we should act only in ways in which this would be a universal law.

p.343

In other words, it would make sense for everyone to act in the same way. This he referred to as a universal law required by a categorical imperative, a sort of ethical idealism. Human’s limited sense of perception, based on intuition, gave them a false picture of the real world. Kant thought people could only know more of this unreal, noumenal world through scientific development. There was another world, the phenomenal. Only God, Kant considered, could know the full reality, that is both the mystical phenomenal world and the unreal, noumenal one apparent to our senses.

He clearly applied his impressive intellect to science; theoretical astronomy in particular. Kant hypothesised that there must be other planets in our solar system, beyond the six known in his time, Mercury, Venus, Earth Mars, Jupiter and Saturn. He made his prediction in 1755. A little later, in 1781, Kant’s conjecture was born out with the discovery of Uranus. Immanuel also predicted our galaxy as a rotating disc, held in place by gravity and he claimed there were separate galaxies, like islands on the sea of the Universe. He termed this concept the Island Universe. He also predicted the existence of planets outside our solar system. The first exoplanet was discovered 200 years after Kant, in 1992. Pretty good Immanuel, 10 out of 10 for your astronomical predictions. You were a great philosopher and no mean astronomer.

70. Paine, Thomas

1737 to 1809 CE Political

Thomas Paine had a unique impact on both the American and French revolutions through his free thinking and revolutionary writing. He was a critical figure in the American revolution and its subsequent constitution.

Thomas Paine was born in England at Thetford, Norfolk before moving to Philadelphia in 1774. His unsuccessful career as a clerk in England encouraged a move to America. Benjamin Franklin advised him to do so when the two met in London.

He abhorred the slavery he saw in America, writing a treatise: “African Slavery in America”. Hence Thomas Paine became a real pain for some of the American southern establishment. But his real influence was political and massive in its impact.

He had a bearing on the American revolution, through his work “Common Sense”. In this he said America must become independent of England and form its own government. Thomas argued forcefully in pamphlets and letters

p.344

against British authority in America, being particularly dismissive of the monarchy saying: "No man in his senses can say that their claim under William the Conqueror is a very honourable one.

A French bastard, landing with an armed banditti and establishing himself king of England against the consent of the natives, is in plain terms a very paltry rascally original. It certainly hath no divinity in it." His views were that a government was a form of necessary evil so it should be as simple as possible with frequent elections.

Later, he moved back to England and then France, continuing writing anti-establishment tracts like “Age of Reason” which praised the Enlightenment. He caused trouble for himself in France as an atheist who opposed the execution of King Louis XV1.This resulted in imprisonment for a while. Eventually he moved back to America after meeting Thomas Jefferson, author of the Declaration of Independence, partly inspired by Thomas Paine was the third President of h United States.

71. Bentham, Jeremy

1748 to 1832 CE Utilitarianism, Liberalism

Bentham was a British philosopher who invented an ism, British utilitarianism. He was strong on both moral and legal philosophy. Jeremy’s paid job was as a lawyer. He was quite dismissive in his views of the English legal system: “The English legal system was founded on an unscientific jumble of historical prejudice and religious superstition. It’s nonsense on stilts.” This overarching view led him to set out his detailed thoughts in: “Introduction to the Principles of Morals and Legislation” He has a claim to uniqueness as a philosopher, who lived more than 100 years ago because he can still be viewed. Jeremy Bentham may have died in 1832 but he remains to be seen, or rather his remains may be seen. Following a request in his will his skeletal body, dressed in his own clothes and with a waxwork head is to be found on display in a cabinet at the University College London.

The issue of utility, said Bentham, arises when promoting the idea that an action that promotes the most happiness is the one to go for. He said: “The greatest happiness of the greatest number is the foundation of morals and legislation.” His rhyme summarised this broad hedonistic tendency:

"Intense, long, certain, speedy, fruitful, pure- Such marks in pleasures and in pains endure. Such pleasures seek if private be they end:

p.345

If it be public, wide let them extend. Such pains avoid, whichever be they view. If pains must come, let them extend to few."

He proposed a number of legal and social reforms based on his guiding principle of the greatest utility. Specifically, he was a supporter of individual and economic freedom, something after the views of Adam Smith. Bentham was a great liberal, expressing a number of worthy ideas that have since been adopted by many modern, advanced societies such as: freedom for the individual; separation of church and state: “No power of government ought to be employed in the endeavour to establish any system or article of belief on the subject of religion.” He also spoke of the right to divorce; decriminalisation of homosexual acts; abolition of capital punishment and abolition of physical punishment - including that of children. This was an amazing list of proposed reforms for Bentham’s time, Georgian England. Today, The European Court of Human Rights legally protects the freedom of the individual in 47, Council of Europe, member states. At a global level, the United Nations Council on Human Rights takes up issues.

Well done Jeremy. You were a brave liberal voice against a rigid, unfair, discriminatory world.

72. Wollstonecraft, Mary

1759 to 1797 CE Woman, Feminism, Political

Mary was born and died in London. She is buried at St Pancras Old Church. She was a feminist who put some bones on the work of Mary Astell. Interestingly her daughter, Mary Shelley, became at least as famous, as the author of Frankenstein - a monster man. Her mother viewed some men as monsters writing: “A Vindication of the Rights of Woman: with Strictures on Political and Moral Subjects” in 1792. A long title but it certainly leaves you in no doubt as to the content. Mary was spurred into action after reading a report to the French National Assembly that suggested women should only receive a domestic education. In her “Rights of a Woman” she argued that the female sex was not inferior to men, they just lacked education. She stressed the importance of learning for women and said: “Strengthen the female mind by enlarging it, and there will be an end to blind obedience.” and in an encouragement to both sexes to stop treating women as objects, often sex objects: “My own sex, I hope, will excuse me, if I treat them like rational creatures, instead of flattering their fascinating graces, and viewing them as if they were in a state of perpetual childhood, unable to stand alone.” Mary was

p.346

also a political radical, championing republicanism over hereditary monarchy and rejecting the artificial distinctions of rank. She had a short life (dying after childbirth at 39) but it was eventful, and at the time scandalous.

Mary had several lovers, one of whom was married, another was a woman. She also had illegitimate children. In the more prudish time she lived in, this behaviour was seen by many as completely shocking. Mary was no doubt both ostracised and vilified. No wonder she attempted suicide twice. But Mary’s legacy lives on. She is a major historical figure for feminists and people generally.

73. Hegel, Georg

1770 to 1831 CE Idealism, Political, Metaphysics, Monism (possibly)

Hegel is the most difficult philosopher to understand. No less a person than Bertrand Russell said that. Others find him difficult too. I certainly do, but he has quite a lot of competition in the unintelligibility stakes from the likes of Derrida, Heidegger and Husserl.

In his youth Hegel studied both theology and philosophy. Of the two he was much more interested in philosophy. He liked women and a drink or two. Obviously, he was not so severe as his picture would lead you to think. He considered that his original and systematic approach to philosophy was the way to reveal the final truth of reality and of human history. So, what more specifically was he about? Technically Hegel is known as a German Idealist. He wrote on a wide range of philosophical topics: including the philosophy of religion; the history of philosophy; political philosophy; aesthetics; metaphysics and logic. His ideas were published in four books: The “Phänomenologie des Geistes” (“Phenomenology of Mind”) was perhaps the most fundamental. In this he attempts to develop speculative reason, as a form of logic, to explore the problems of the relationship between thought and reality. He used a dialectical method (taking directly opposing sides of an argument and working through the pros and cons). He started at the beginning with two concepts Being and the basic opposite of this, i.e. Nothing. He thought this dialectical process, gradually bringing together in a synthesis, time and again, a series of contradictory aspects, thesis and antithesis, would eventually lead to the idea of an Absolute (an ultimate something). This seems to be a final truth. Hegel said: "The bud disappears when the blossom breaks through, and we might say that the former is refuted by the latter; in the same way when the fruit comes, the blossom may be explained to be a false form of

p.347

the plant’s existence, for the fruit appears as its true nature in place of the blossom. The ceaseless activity of their own inherent nature makes these stages moments of an organic unity, where they not merely do not contradict one another, but where one is as necessary as the other; and constitutes thereby the life of the whole." He also drew an historical link to this concept: “But there is another logic. Knowledge has an evolutionary history that is made up of concepts, not isolated true or false factual propositions.”

Hegel’s ideas are very much in vogue in modern times, through Merleau-Ponty and others, phenomenology has come to the fore. What is consciousness? Can we truly know anything? These thoughts are a step forward from Hegel, and before him, Bishop Berkeley.

Hegel rejected Kant’s idea of noumenal and phenomenal worlds and that something in existent is unknowable. He said: “The real is rational, and the rational is real”. His religious views were difficult to pin down but he did say: “The result of philosophic examination is that God is the absolute truth, the universal in and for itself, embracing all things and in which all things subsist.” In his political philosophy Hegel recommended a strong, constitutional monarchy combined with people who are free and unregimented. Hegel had influence on Nietzsche; Kierkegaard and Karl Marx. The latter though was more interested in Hegel’s ideas of freedom for people than constitutional monarchy.

74. Comte, Auguste

1798 to 1857 CE Positivism, Science

Auguste might enter the philosophers record books as the guy with the longest name. His full moniker is: Isidore Marie Auguste François Xavier Comte. Perhaps for obvious reasons he decided his short name would not be Marie, so he became Auguste.

But Auguste has more claims to fame than just his long name. He is credited as the founder of sociology. Both his home country, France and Romania introduced a series of postage stamps in his honour to commemorate the centenary of his death.

Auguste had an interesting idea about how human perceptions of reality change over evolutionary time. What he said was that intellectual development followed three stages: theological; metaphysical and then positive. Auguste did not go very far back in terms of human evolution. He started with the

p.348

European medieval period. In this time, he said that people very commonly adopted the most primitive supernatural beliefs, this was what he referred to as the theological period. He then said that after this people gained a broader perspective. Supernatural belief still abounded in this later, metaphysical period, but now people were starting to speculate more widely on the true nature of reality. Following this second stage, later still, people reached stage three - the positive period which heralded the scientific age. He is known as the founder of positivism, later supplanted by neo-positivism or logical positivism. These two were very similar to the original concept of a philosophy that embraced science and empiricism. Auguste became the philosopher of science, treating developing philosophies successively of mathematics, physics, chemistry and biology. He had a high regard for the importance of mathematics saying: “Mathematical Analysis is… the true rational basis of the whole system of our positive knowledge”. Above all, his focus was on the social impact of science.

75. Schopenhauer, Arthur

1788 1860 CE Pessimism, Ethics

Arthur was an atheist, German philosopher who came from a very wealthy family. This allowed him to enjoy life as a young man. In his younger days, he picked up chorus girls from stage doors on a regular basis. It’s doubtful that this was for the purpose of discussing philosophy with them. His family relations and lack of friends probably had some influence on his psyche and gloomy philosophy. Arthur’s father committed suicide when Schopenhauer was 17. After this, Arthur’s mother enjoyed life as a youngish widower, taking a number of lovers. She was smart and a successful novelist who was, for a time, a leading figure in German social and literary circles, for a time. Goethe was a friend.

Arthur disapproved greatly of his mother’s behaviour. He failed to see that his own behaviour was not very different. He also appeared to be jealous of her position as a clever, independent woman. Arthur wanted to be regarded as the figure of importance and the sole genius in his family.

Perhaps not surprisingly his philosophy was pessimistic, basically claiming that everything is hopeless. Unlike Plato, Kant and others who thought there was some room for reason in the world Arthur saw the world as irrational and unable to escape from evil. People are full of unsatisfied desires, according to him. The only thing to be done, he said, was to renounce desire. Something after Gautama here and his four noble truths. Gautama, or Buddha, was his

p.349

predecessor by 1700 years. In fact, Arthur had a four of his own. He identified four different categories each of which had its own associated reasoning:

(1) Material things; associated reasoning is cause and effect; (2) Abstract concepts; reasoning here is in terms of logic; (3) Mathematical and Geometrical constructions; reasoning in respect of numbers and space; (4) Psychologically motivating forces; where moral reasoning or intention must be used.

This position of hopelessness, he thought, was slightly redeemed by the arts and philosophy. He said: “Creatures of will, we cannot stop striving. In art, we retreat from light.” His main theory was centred on the will, and set out in his work “The World as Will and Idea” in 1818. Here he said the will was the main, sometimes bad, influence on human character, consciousness and intellect. Conflict between opposing wills caused endless dispute and frustration. In his ethical philosophy, he introduced the moral will. he said we should take an empathic position with others, through our moral will.

76. Emerson, Ralph Waldo

1803 to 1882 CE Transcendentalism, Pantheism, Ethics

Here is an American who in the philosophical pantheon was known as a leading figure of the New England Transcendentalists. Ralph was mainly a writer of essays, sermons and poems. He emphasised the importance of personal integrity and morals and believed in the unity of all nature, a sort of pantheism. For Ralph, this meant that everything, mind and matter, was a part of a continuing whole, including the divine being. He thought people should be self-reliant and were capable of reaching any goal (very American). He said: “a man in debt is so far a slave”. This is not quite so in keeping with modern America where household debt usually exceeds $10 trillion. Ralph also stresses that we should live in harmony with nature (again not commonly so much the America of today). Be daring and explore outside of your comfort zone was his view: “Do not go where the path may lead, go instead where there is no path and leave a trail.”, and “All life is an experiment. The more experiments you make the better.” He also stressed the importance of keeping good relations with other people: “You cannot do a kindness too soon, for you never know how soon it will be too late.” and “The only way to have a friend is to be one.”

p.350

Ralph was a founder of transcendentalism. This was a move away from what he saw as the stultifying reliance on the Scriptures for intellectual guidance in the eastern USA.

His transcendentalist approach claimed that current religious and political institutions were corrupting of individuals. People were at their best, he thought, when self-reliant and independent. They had inherent goodness which could create a true community. He influenced Nietzsche to some extent (although the latter did not believe in a divine being).

77. Mill, John Stuart

1806 to 1873 CE Empiricism, Utilitarianism, Liberalism

Mill was probably the greatest English philosopher of his century. On his death, an admiring fellow philosopher Henry Sidgwick said:" 'I should say that from about 1860-65, or thereabouts, he ruled England in the region of thought as very few men ever did." Mill was extremely precocious as a child, reading Plato, Xenophon and others in Greek by the age of eight. He was encouraged to constantly study by his father and was denied a normal childhood. All this caused him to have a nervous breakdown at the age of 21. Later, he had a happier personal life in marriage to Harriet whom he idolised and thought was his intellectual equal. She was a strong advocate of women’s rights, as was Mill.

Mill was a utilitarian, his interest sparked by his father’s friend, the philosopher Jeremy Bentham. But he realised some of the potential problems with Bentham’s straightforward, utilitarianism. For instance, applying Bentham’s idea you could have a situation incompatible with generally accepted moral standards. Three torturers could get more pleasure from their action than their single victim would experience (unless he/she was an extreme masochist!) Strict application of Bentham would say this was a good, utilitarian outcome – the most pleasure for most people. To counter this sort of utilitarian problem Mill added another element that must be considered. In addition to Bentham’s quantity of pleasure Mill said the <u>quality</u> of pleasure must also be a relevant factor.

As a result, Bentham philosophy has been defined as <u>act</u> utilitarianism and Mill as <u>rule</u> utilitarianism. He also said the pleasures gained from a deep-thinking mind were superior to those of the flesh.

p.351

John Stuart, a question for you, how much does the mind have to be engaged in order to trump all fleshly pleasures? Why can’t we have both at once, e.g. eating a sumptuous meal while thinking about the nature of reality!

Mill did not just dwell on the theoretical aspects of utilitarianism He proposed many liberal and practical sociological actions in written works, his greatest being “On Liberty”. Here are some:

  • give women the right to vote;
  • reform labour unions and co-operatives;
  • reform Parliament by introducing proportional representation and the single transferrable vote.;
  • reform divorce laws to give women equal rights;

He backed up these ideas with forceful argument in Parliament where he was an MP. In economics, he was a free marketeer and did not believe in progressive taxation, with a bigger tax rate for high-income earners. Mill believed this punished the hard working, successful people and rewarded those who could try harder, but he did favour inheritance tax. He was not a believer in religion. He was an atheist. Some of the many notable things he said, along the lines of Epicurus: “Pleasure and freedom from pain, are the only things desirable as ends.” He also anticipated some aspects of the 1948 United Nations Universal Declaration of Human Rights saying: “The only freedom which deserves the name is that of pursuing our own good, in our own way, so long as we do not attempt to deprive others of theirs, or impede their efforts to obtain it.” Mill gave some empirical advice too: “There are many truths of which the full meaning cannot be realized until personal experience has brought it home.” What a great liberal he was.

78. Kierkegaard, Soren

p.352

80. Peirce, Charles Sanders

Charles was an American philosopher. Not only that he also managed to be a logician, mathematician, and scientist. His main claim to philosophical fame is as the originator of pragmatism. He might have been even more famously known as the inventor of computers. He had the idea that in switching electrical circuits these could be used in making logical operations. Sadly, he did not develop this correct theory. It was decades later that computers were invented.

His pragmatic views are the sort that often give philosophers a bad name with the general population. The pragmatism promoted by Peirce says that thoughts main function is to decide action, make predictions or solve problems. It was not to hold a mirror up to reality. To most people this may seem stating the “bleeding obvious”.

Peirce’ views were partially founded on his scientific mind. He held some support for both Kant and Hegel but clearly had a jaundiced view of some philosophers: "It will sometimes strike a scientific man that the philosophers

81. Nietzsche, Frederick

1844 to 1900 CE Existentialism, Nihilism

Fred (I will call him this, it’s easier to pronounce and write than his surname.) Fred was a German philosopher, poet and composer. Descartes apart, Fred made the most well-known statement in philosophy: “God is dead”. This comment is quite well known.

Fred’s concerns with God partly stems from the time he lived in. He read Darwin and understood that evolution was contingent (i.e. by chance) rather than, as had been widely promoted by many religions and beliefs, by telos (purpose). This affected Fred’s views on nihilism (life has no meaning or purpose). Before Darwin, an Abrahamic God was firmly in place for many. This belief in God was a huge counter to the apparent hopelessness of nihilism. With God, we have intrinsic values, a basis for knowledge and a justification for evil in the world. In this sense Christianity gave comfort in promoting a certainty and was a great buffer to the gloomy despair fostered by nihilism. Fred thought nihilism needed to be accepted but it should be fought against. Did Fred’s despairing nihilist views arise because he was severely affected by a combination of: poor medical health, (particularly mental health); acceptance of proof that God did not exist; and his forceful sister’s hard racial views? Fred’s possible answer to how nihilism could be defeated was by the emergence of a Superman, Ubermench. This new, superior being would supplant existing humanity and its crumbling values. Had Fred lived in modern times he might have taken comfort from the possibility that scientific rationalisation gives defeat to nihilism.

In his book “Beyond Good and Evil” he criticised past philosophers for their acceptance of moral precepts and, with further originality, said that sometimes ‘lies’ could be preferable to ‘truth’. These thoughts contributed to his concern with nihilism.

p.355

Fred had interesting views in several other areas. He thought the individual made free choices over values and beliefs and through this determined what sort of person emerges. He pontificated on both marriage and art: “When marrying, ask yourself this question: Do you believe that you will be able to converse well with this person into your old age? Everything else in marriage is transitory.”

And, “Without music, life would be a mistake.” Poor Fred suffered mood swings and bouts of depression. At the age of 44 he went into an asylum for a while. He also suffered physically and died comparatively young (at age 56), probably from brain cancer.

Not only were his mental and physical health the subject of doctoring, he also had his ideas doctored, by his sister, Elizabeth.

She was anti-Semitic and an ardent nationalist. After her famous brother died she changed some of his writings, which spoke against both anti-Semitism and nationalism, so that Fred appears to support her own views - he didn’t. 82. Frege, Gottlob

1848 to 1925 CE Analytic, Logic

This Professor of Mathematics was yet another in the line-up of noted German philosophers. Gottlob was around at the same time as fellow Germans, Marx, Nietzsche and Heidegger. He also followed not far behind Hegel and Schopenhauer. Gottlob had an interest in the logic of arithmetic and in language. In logical musings, he followed on from Aristotle who was perhaps the originator of the use of logic in philosophy. Maybe Gottlob was also inspired to think outside the box because he doubted the seeming certainty claimed by his contemporaries.

He thought there had to be a surer, analytical way at arriving at knowledge and true understanding than the opinionated utterances of fellow German practitioners and others. So, he developed an analytic, logic-based system. Gottlob led quite a secluded life with no particular major events to disturb his peace. This environment probably gave him the time to think deep, original thoughts.

p.356

He brought about a sea-change in Western philosophical thinking by giving up on traditional deductive logic and originating a new symbolic, formal version. In 1884 his “The Foundations of Arithmetic” appeared in print.

He explained his new logic where he thought he was able to make firm connections between mathematics and logic. He demonstrated that numbers are not objects, like cows or doors. He said: “Numbers are the properties of concepts or useful logical fictions - rather like the fiction of the average man.” This led the way for those 20th century philosophers who took up the analytic method. Gottlob did not stop with maths and logic. He also had a look at language where he thought meaning attributed through language, was not just about the what of an object but also its sense. Here he said that the sense of a language depended on its conventional, public use and this can change. A simple example is the use of the word “gay”. Before the 1970’s reference to a gay person would be interpreted as meaning that person was jolly. In more modern parlance the term refers to a homosexual person. Bertrand Russell took up and developed some of Gottlob’s ideas. He both impressed and influenced Wittgenstein. The latter was encouraged to go to Cambridge to study with Russell.

83. Husserl, Edmund

84. Bergson, Henri

1859 to 1941 CE Time, mind

Here we have a Jewish, Frenchman who was the most famous philosopher in the early part of the twentieth century, and very popular. His lectures in Paris and New York were always crowded out.

‘Time’ is appropriate to mention. His unique thoughts about time were Henri’s crowning achievement. He set out his ideas in: “Time and Freewill: An Essay on the Immediate Data of Consciousness (1889).”

Ideas about time in the 1880’s fell into two categories: 1. The older version was known as Newtonian absolutism which said time is independent, it’s always there, even if there is nothing else in the universe. 2. Kantian idealism of time, here Kant, although accepting the Newtonian version, thought time is intrinsic to our minds. We cannot perceive the world but in terms of time. These ideas captured Bergson’s interest. He concluded that our spatial concept of time, where we divide it into seconds, minutes, hours, days and years, was not the full story. These ‘times’ were all based on special concepts, such as the time that the Earth took to go around the sun and time it took for the Earth revolved on its axis.

They did not represent the human experience. He wrote: “We give a mechanical explanation of a fact and then substitute the explanation for the fact itself.” Lived time has ‘real duration, (durée réelle)’, according to Bergson, not like the artificial spacial measurement. Our experience of time is as if it were a flow where the past penetrates into the presence.

p.358

Some philosophers thought Bergson viewed time in an original way. William James was impressed, others not so. Bertrand Russell, was highly sceptical. He claimed Bergson was not logically rigorous enough. Bergson also upset Albert Einstein, whose showed the link between time and space in his theory of “General Relativity”. In 1922 they had a public debate where Einstein was seen to be the winner on points, but at another time, who knows what the outcome, Einstein might become second.

Bergson saw memory as a spiritual entity that assisted the brain in determining present action. For Bergson memory was entirely separate from the body. He was also critical of Descartes dualism. Bergson, said Rene defined mind and body as substance, whereas one, concerned with memory, was not. It was of a spiritual nature.

His last book, “The Creative Mind”, introduces us to Bergson’s foray into analytic method. He says: “What philosophy has lacked most of all is precision. 88. Ryle, Gilbert

Gilbert Ryle began and ended at the seaside. He was born in the south of England, at Brighton and, 76 years later, died in the north at Whitby.

When it came to philosophy, Gilbert was definitely not all at sea. His main interest was in language, like Wittgenstein. He thought philosophical issues could be dealt with by consideration of the subtle uses of ordinary language.

Consequentialism sounds much deeper than it really is. It simply means the consequences of whatever we choose to do are the final basis for any decision about the rightness or wrongness of our conduct. You might think this smacks of inconsequentialism rather than the opposite. Perhaps Elizabeth would have been better off if she had stuck to the task of uncovering and championing Wittgenstein.

100. Foucault, Michel

1926 to 1984 CE Post-Modernism

Michel Foucault (pronounced Foo-coo) was the son of a Poitiers surgeon and another off the production line of French 20th century philosophers. After studying with Maurice Merleau–Ponty he later taught at the universities of Uppsala, Hamburg and Warsaw. Michel was influenced by the German philosophers Heidegger and Nietzsche. His doctoral thesis was titled “Madness and Unreason: A History of Madness in the Classical Age”. This does not sound like a relaxing read on a sunny beach holiday.

Michel was not only a philosopher but also a historian of ideas and truth; a social theorist, philologist, literary critic and political activist. Towards the end of his life he said all the areas of his work and interest were intended to address one single issue, what he called the production of truth throughout history. He meant that at different stages in history different truths were accepted and power was wielded based on these generally accepted truths, this is something like Protagoras’ view of subjective perception. An example would be the Catholic inquisition which held that the Earth was the centre of the universe. Galileo, Copernicus and Giordano Bruno suffered for failing to accept this particular “truth”.

p.379

Michel explained: " I set out to grasp the mechanisms of the effective exercise of power; and I do this because those who are inserted in these relations of power, who are implicated therein, may, through their actions, their resistance, and their rebellion, escape them, transform them—in short, no longer submit to them." He claimed that power is everywhere. That seems too obvious to be worth stating but Foucault thinks that the usual concept of power is incorrect. He believes his approach, though a critical history of modernity (his words) shows that power is found in all interactions and relationships. Accepted truth implies knowledge and is associated with the use of power.

He also thought schools were just regulatory institutions: “Schools serve the same social functions as prisons and mental institutions - to define, classify, control, and regulate people.” Well, Michel surely this depends on the school? Don’t some, at least, educate their pupils and prepare them quite well for adult life? He died of HIV.

This, he seemed to claim (using “seemed” because Jacques is notoriously difficult to understand) was because our speech and language was not capable of expressing a true reality.

102. Plantinga, Alvin

Nov. 1932 to? Analytic

Alvin is best known for his analytic philosophy in defence of religion. He’s written much on this topic including: “God and Other Minds”, “God, Freedom and Evil” and “Warranted Christian Belief”. For his efforts, he was awarded the Templeton Prize in 2017 (made for exceptional contribution to life’s spiritual aspect). The awarding committee said about him: “an American scholar whose rigorous writings over a half century have made theism - the belief in a divine reality or god - a serious option within academic philosophy.” Richard Dawkins has been critical of this award commenting that it’s made: “usually to a scientist who is prepared to say something nice about religion”.

In answering questions about why God does not make it clear that he exists Alvin claims atheists have something akin to a disease which prevents them freely accepting, in the way Calvin correctly, apparently did, that God exists. He says “due to the entry of sin into the world sometimes our cognitive facilities don’t function the way they should.” Well, whom was it religious people claim invented intelligent humans? Why didn’t He ensure their brains always fired on all cylinders? What seems to have occurred, identified consistently in frequent surveys, is that atheists are more intelligent on average than religious people. It’s quite a mystery why God, assuming there is one, allowed this to happen, surely, he would want his believers to have the most brain power.

If this is so, why does He not make this crystal clear and so avoid the situation where there are many millions of non-believers. And then commenting on the perennial issue of why a kind and loving God allows evil to exist. There is a lot of debate over the question of evil. Alvin comes up with a clever but difficult answer to why God allows it to exist. He argues that evil is linked to its opposite, i.e. goodness. Alvin says God could not get rid of evil without also eliminating goodness. In essence, he says evil can logically exist if God has granted humans free will. They are free to be good or to commit evil. Interestingly he does not claim his argument proves the case for God’s existence, only that it is logically possible. Therefore, the existence of evil is ambivalent to the God issue. It does not prove God does not exist, nor does it prove he does exist.

It is possible to ask serious questions about both evil and free will. Is it really in the power of an omnipotent (all-powerful) and omniscient (all knowing) being to allow real free will? Schopenhauer did not think so.

“Man can do what he wills but he cannot will what he wills”. (Schopenhauer mainly rejects the idea of free-will, arguing that actions are determined by things that don’t change: motives, incentives and character.)

How does freewill sit with God as an omniscient entity? Everything we think, say and do is based on a number of factors: genetic make-up, inheritance, circumstances, culture, environment, desires, interaction with others, health and mind influencing substances. In short, our thoughts and actions are governed by the entire world, and universe around us, past and present.

The omnipotent entity that is God is the one who set up the entire environment which embraces and determines our whole existence. If we could gather all

p.383

HE can work out what we end up doing and whether we will be morally good or bad. In essence, everything about humans is therefore pre-determined, assuming the Abrahamic God exists. This dichotomy between freewill and a determined universe is known as incompatibilism. In addition to this, it’s claimed that God is omniscient (all-knowing) This means God knows what we are going to think, say and do before we know it ourselves. HE’s known this forever. So, how does omnipotence and omniscience fit with free will? Can God somehow close his eyes or switch off knowing? These points were made in Chapter 3, under the morality heading, but they are so fundamentally important they are worthy of another mention. We also recognise that religious theologians claim God has another key attribute, omnibenevolence (moral perfection).

Evil and moral perfection are uneasy bedfellows, no matter the clever arguments. The existence of evil remains disturbing in the God argument, as Alvin himself admits.

They found 72% were atheists, a 21% increase on those quoted by Pigliucci.

Personal Philosophy

A definition of philosophy has already been given earlier. We know from this that it covers a wide range of topics. But my suggestion here is focused solely on an ethical philosophy. This to enable people to be:

Fit For Life

Even people who claim complete ignorance of philosophy or those who are contemptuous of it have a philosophy of their own. They do not necessarily know this. Nor would some feel comfortable about having it pointed out, but it is so. The Oxford Dictionary describes philosophy as: “The study of the fundamental nature of knowledge, reality, and existence.” Now this sounds quite deep and lacking in the mundane of how most people go about their lives. Few spend much time pondering the metaphysical issue of “the fundamental nature of reality”. Yet the Dictionary, helpfully, goes on to give a more prosaic explanation of philosophy: “A theory or attitude that acts as a guiding principle for behaviour.” We are in business with this description.

Everyone has some sort of outlook on life that determines their thoughts, speech and behaviour in certain circumstances. This simply means that people have opinions or reactions which underpin their attitude to politics; finance; leisure; relationships with family, friends, other people, religion, and other things. In the same fashion, they can iterate their choice with vitally important questions (to some of us) such as what is the rugby side the manager should pick. Everyone can back up their opinions with argument and reasoning, no matter how instinctive or shallow this might sometimes appear. This is a form of philosophy. Often, the basic, underlying personal rules or steers that people use, sometimes completely unconsciously, is a type of ethical philosophy.

My own ethical philosophy is very easily described here. You may be relieved to know, there is no “ism” claimed for it! And you don’t need to study Aristotle’s highly analytical approach set out in Nicomachean Ethics. You can if you want, but my words though less, much less, deep have the advantage of simplicity. I try to keep to my own ethical philosophy but don’t always succeed. No matter, it is definitely FIT for purpose. Well, I would say that, wouldn’t I?

F is for fairness. This means treat your fellow man, or woman, with fairness and kindly consideration. If you treat others fairly they are more likely to reciprocate although not always.

p.388

Thinking of others and treating them well will help you feel at ease that you have done the right thing. You will have established an ethical, secure moral base for yourself. Being fair does not mean letting people trample all over you. Sometimes, being fair means making a comment or taking an action that you know may not be immediately appreciated. For example, parents are not being fair to their children if they allow them to do exactly what they like. Such children will grow up to be selfish. They will not know the boundaries of behaviour and may find it harder to have friends.

It has been shown in scientific studies that humans have an innate concept of fairness which they apply (well, some do) for the benefit of others even though it may be slightly detrimental to themselves in the immediate term. Of course, this innateness is not an absolute imperative. Some people easily override it and are totally or mostly selfish.

I is for interest. Take an interest in a wide range of concrete things, people or knowledge. Be like Socrates who said the unquestioning mind is not worth living. Broad knowledge can make for a happier life. You have a wider range of things to become fascinated by. Boredom and dissatisfaction are defeated, because there is always something you are interested in and are pursuing. Having wide-ranging knowledge also helps with better decision making. You are unlikely to become a narrow-minded bigot or intolerant fanatic if you ask questions and have some broad knowledge of our amazing world, or even more, know something of our astounding universe. Remember what Hitchhiker’s Guide to the Galaxy author, Douglas Adams, said: “Let us think the unthinkable, let us do the undoable, let us prepare to grapple with the ineffable itself, and see if we may not eff it after all.” A bit tricky you think, doing the undoable? Perhaps Douglas simply meant make more of an effort pull up your stuck trouser zip otherwise you will be undone. And bear in mind, or maybe not, that his “F it” has a different derivation than the FIT I suggest.

T is for try. Try and achieve small tasks and bigger goals. Don’t let life drift on by. Do things and plan to do more things. Making the effort and then succeeding can give a comfortable, warm feeling. You will feel good having achieved something after battling though any difficulties and sticking to your task. Even relative failure can be worthwhile and better than making no effort. It is certainly preferable to giving up too readily.

p.389

We can all learn from our mistakes. They can help point us in the right direction for another go at something. But don’t make a meal of it. You must not feel required to be always pursuing some worthy task or other. Relax and take it easy too. Get out occasionally into green countryside. It’s good for the psyche. Make time for leisure activities. We are social beings, contact with people is very important, so visit or chat with friends and family. Join a club whose objectives cover something of interest to you. Do other things like reading a book, going out for a meal, playing on the computer or smart phone, attending a football match, cinema or theatre. These types of activities are as important as diligence in working towards a goal. Just don’t become either totally driven, hedonistic or lazy. The great Sir Terry Pratchett’s Discworld philosopher, Ly Tin Wheedle, says: “You have to do something or be considered, in the great scheme of things, a tit.” And remember, whatever you do or try to do, laughter is essential. It’s proven to be good for your health. So, make sure you laugh very regularly. (see Chapter 21 Design a God).

Finally, and perhaps most importantly of all, there’s no need to believe or follow me or indeed anyone else. As Michel de Montaigne said: “He who follows another follows nothing. He finds nothing; indeed, he seeks nothing.” You can admire or agree with the views of others but never, ever, be a blind, unthinking disciple. Ask probing questions, seek the evidence and consider alternatives. Peter Abelard made this point: “The first key to wisdom is assiduous and frequent questioning.”

Never be led by the nose. Make up your own mind, always, but be tolerant of people who think differently to you.

So, there we have it, be FIT for life. With this approach, you set yourself in a good position to be happy and to enjoy life, as much as circumstances allow. You are also likely to make a positive contribution to human society generally.

Some isms explained

Absurdism This basically says, everything is hopeless, there’s no point in trying to find meaning in life, that’s just daft, it’s absurd. See Camus.

Animism This is a sort of all-embracing Gaia concept, where all objects – animals, plants, humans, rocks the weather – are thought to have a spiritual essence. This essence can influence future events. It’s a belief that may have been common with pre-historic humans.

Aristotelianism The clue here is in the word, Aristotle. If you are a follower of this ism, it means you quite like what this guy has to say. Try Averroes or, of course, Aristotle himself.

Behaviourism There are several varieties of this, including psychological behaviourism, but we are concerned with the common philosophical type. Here it’s to do with mind. Things in our mind, mental terms or concepts, can translate into behavioural terms and concepts, i.e. what people do as a result of a thought. Any mental state is directly linked to behaviour, so behaviourists claim. This theory is not so popular in modern philosophy. Gilbert Ryle may be a behaviourist of sort.

Buddhism You don’t have to sit under a Bodhi tree to understand what this is all about. Study the four noble truths then follow the right path, the eight-fold one, and you will get there. Gautama tells all.

Conceptualism Universals can exist, but only inside your head mind. A sort of middle road between nominalism and realism. Abelard and Ockham of this type.

Confucianism Not to be confused with confusianism. Confucianism encourages humility and a sense of knowing-your-place. Confucius’s philosophy was very useful to Chinese rulers, guess why?

Constructivism No, this is not a disease that serial, high-rise builders suffer from. In philosophy it generally refers to a theory of knowledge. Constructivism, from an epistemological point of view, says people use their own experiences to build up a picture of well, everything they think they know and understand. Would this better be referred to as obviousism, but that would leave out reason and that’s not rational, is it?

Cynicism You are not going to believe this, so no point in saying what this is about. Antisthenes was the first cynic, “Oh yeah?” I hear you cynically say, but his student Diogenes was the one who gave it serious momentum.

Deconstructionism Something to do with knocking down old buildings you could surmise? You might be right, because even its chief, maybe sole, proponent, Derrida, could not clearly explain what this meant. Apparently, it’s related to the interface between text and meaning – if that means anything mind you.

p.391

Dualism This is not something to mind. It doesn’t really matter, although there’s a body of work that says it does. Have a look at Descartes.

Empiricism Was Queen Victoria an empiricist? She certainly lorded it, or should that be lady-ed it, over the British Empire. Her knowledge of this was based on common sense and experience, this is empiricism, David Hume is with Victoria as an empiricist.

Epicureanism You can have quite a good time getting to grips with this one, but I fear you have to learn a bit of Greek first: ataraxia and aponia. This is a pain, I know. No! quite the opposite. Followers are Epicurus, naturally, and his Roman admirer Lucretius.

Essentialism Here we are born to be a certain thing which we may or may not achieve. It is the opposite of existentialism complete freedom. Plato and Aristotle were of this view.

Existentialism Jean Paul Sartre, in the 1940s, was this idea’s main proponent, but he did say he pursued his philosophy as a way of getting girls. Roughly, it says all we have, know and do is based on our own existence in the world. We find things out through free will and choice. This is a supremely true philosophy, to an extent. We definitely have to exist first before we can experience anything.

Fallibism Fallibism is quite useful in an argument. You can say, “I’m going to be fallibistic. I know I’m right, even though I can’t prove it.” Many have probably engaged in fallibism without knowing it.

Historicism What’s coming up now is because of what came up in the past.

Holism A bit psychological here: “The whole is greater than the parts.” Perhaps it should be wholism.

Humanism Humanists believe in non-belief. It is also a more positive stance towards the world, even to the universe, than their closely allied cousins, the atheists. Humanists think of science almost as a God. Everything is natural and can be explained, with the help of science. There is no need for the intervention of a deity, or any other supernatural entity, so say humanists. Grayling is one of these.

Incompatibilism Here we have maybe a cry for help from some religious inclined people. It recognises that it’s impossible to have a deterministic Godly approach – everything is pre-ordained – and freewill. The two don’t go together. They are incompatible. Help, what do we do if we want to have an omniscient, all capable God alongside humans who have freedom to decide whether to be goodies or badies. Along came Liberalism.

Idealism This assumes an order, mind and consciousness first, matter follows on and is secondary. But the universe had lots and lots of matter for a long time, before any mind or consciousness cranked into life, so this philosophy may not be ideal.

p.392

Immaterialism George Berkeley was very determined this one was correct. For George everything that we thought existed was simply our perception. It was all in the mind, aided by God. This links closely with Idealism.

Infallibism This is so true, you cannot doubt it.

Instrumentalism No, it’s is not a generic reference to the action of someone who plays the oboe or flute in an orchestra. John Dewey was enamored of it. Thought, he said quite pragmatically, it is just an instrument for solving practical problems. You might think that’s obvious.

Isimitis An essential contractible disease by philosophers. All seek to be associated with an original ‘ism’ like Diogenes with Cynicism and Plato with Platonism. If not the originator, important for philosophers to be associated with an ism that they add a few twists of their own to. These additions are sometimes so extensive the philosopher receives the accolade of establishing a neo-ism, such as Plotinus with Neo-Platonism.

Liberalism: No, this Liberalism is not referring to the key policy aim of the British political party, the Liberals. Well, in fact it is their underlying aim, but in philosophy of religion it can have two meanings. First, it may refer to tolerance of other religions, or it can also refer to the idea of humans having free will. They can choose to be good or evil, it is said. Alleging that despite an all-knowing God etc, our thoughts, speech, and actions are not pre-ordained. See also essentialism and existentialism.

Materialism Opposite of immaterialism and idealism, materialism is where matter comes before mind. Well, our brains are made of matter and are a source of our consciousness, so maybe 5 out of 5 here. Materialist say everything is composed of matter. Thales and Democritus were early materialists. For Marx, materialism meant something different. It was bad, the quest for more and more and more was something those nasty capitalists were engaged in. This one is a good points earner for philosophers, because it’s also a form of monism. Two isms at once result in a good score in the philosopher’s league.

Monism Constantly complaining would be moanism, close to monism, but not close enough. Monism is a recognition of a single source for things. The Eleatic school was keen on monism. All is one and one is all. Sounds like the script of a Three Musketeers movie. The Abrahamics are monists, whereas ancient Egyptians are certainly not. The former have just the one God, while the latter enjoyed thousands.

Nominalism An anti-term that in its most common usage says there’s no such thing as a universal something – that is, words that imply separate things have some group commonality. It’s anti-universals, those such as Plato’s Perfect Forms.

p.393

Non-cognitivism Don’t you understand this? Well, you are on the right track. In philosophy it’s usually an expression related to ethics. Non-cognitivism indicates that sometimes you have not got the complete picture. For example, saying a robbery is wrong is not, strictly, an ethical statement about morality. We have to go on to say why it is that robbery and any robbery is wrong, otherwise we are stuck in non-cognitivism. This is too abstruse for many of us, but philosophers enjoy this sort of thing.

Occasionalism Everything comes from God, Nicolas Malebranche said “there is only one true cause because there is only one true God.”, although he thought this was true all of the time, not just occasionally. Bishop Berekeley was one of these.

Objectivism Here we have a new-ish kid on the block. It is anti the build it up idea of constructionism. It says its already there, objective reality, is what it is, it does not change. You don’t need to construct it. There’s an ethical element too. It has four tenets: 1. Reality is what it is, e.g. the cosmos is there; 2. Reason is all that’s needed to understand all, don’t embrace faith; 3. Self-interest, in pursuit of happiness, but value others; 4. Capitalism, and its promotion of individual freedom. All this in the pursuit of self-happiness. Google Ayn Rand (1905-1982), or look at page 365.

Platonism, Neo-Platonism Platonists love Plato, only Platonically though. Any other form of love is impossible, given his nearly two and half millennia of deadness. Neo-Platonists were a Johnny-come-lately lot. They were not satisfied with pure Platonism so added a few bits and pieces, from Christianity and Eastern mysticism. Plato’s thoughts, mainly from his dialogue Republic, were slightly re-jigged to bolster the religious views of Jews and Christians. Plotinus was the chief instigator of the neo-Platonic world, although he did not know this. There’s also a sort of halfway house, between the original Plato and the neo, a middle Platonism.

Pluralism This expression has not just one meaning, monism or two meanings, dualism. It has at least three! The most common usage of pluralism, in philosophy, is in reference to politics, usually western politics. In the main it says, let lots of people’s voices be heard; an expression of democracy. It can also point to a theory that there are more than one or two ultimate realities. Finally, it is a societal term saying that within a common framework of government and law, different ethnic and religious groups can do their own thing. This last is also known as multi-culturalism.

Positivism Into the realm of knowledge with this. Knowledge is also called episteme, but that’s all Greek to most people. Positivism says if you want to know something, use scientific method to get to the truth. There’s also logical or empirical positivism, originated by the Vienna Circle. It stresses science is the path to truth, forget metaphysical approaches say the LPs.

p.394

Post-modernism If something comes after the modern era, as in post-modernism, that means we are talking about the future, yes? No, it’s not that in philosophy. Post-modernism refers to a vague method where ideas from different sources are lumped together and then the bones are picked out. Specifically, it’s a reference to different, 20<sup>th</sup> century, philosophical perspectives characterised by jaundiced, analytical views. Derrida is often touted as a post-modernist, that means you can forget about it.

Pragmatism American philosophers invented pragmatism. William James said: “What’s true is what works.” His fellow American, Charles Pierce, mostly went along with this but added that the effects of the apparent truth of statements must be considered. In essence, he said try and find out what is really being communicated, not what you initially think this may be.

Rationalism Reason is king, it’s the source of knowledge, so say the rationalists, for good reason perhaps. Socrates is an early proponent.

Realism In normal parlance something concrete, solid, might be considered real. In philosophical realism this idea of a reality can be extended to non-concrete things. So, Plato’s Perfect Forms, of beauty, justice etc, these are realistic. Things are seen as existing in reality, not just as a concept. There are several other concepts of realism, follow up if you want to know more.

Representationalism What we see is not the real thing, it’s just representative.

Scepticism We cannot be certain of anything, but can we sure of this? If we are sure, then there is at least one think we are not sceptical about. Sceptics are closely allied with cynics.

Scholasticism This is where learned mediaevalists attempted to reconcile Christian theology with ancient philosophy, particularly with Aristotle’s sayings.

Scientism It claims that all-natural sciences, as well as the physical sciences, can benefit from the application of scientific method.

Shamanism This is much older than the Abrahamic religions, which may have picked up on some shamanistic beliefs. Shamanism gives us the concept of good and evil, represented by Gods and demons. There are two other key features: firstly, a belief in the existence of ancestral spirits and secondly the conviction that special people, shamans, can access the Gods and spirits for help, through the appropriate rituals. This usually involves the shaman going into a trance-like state, possibly with the help of a drug. Shamanism, like animism goes back to pre-historic times.

Sophism Ancient Greek teachers of philosophy, especially of arête - virtue - and rhetoric, were called sophists. Protagoras was sophistically inclined. Plato was critical of sophists, and the term came to mean the use of deceptive language.

p.395

Solipsism You go on the ultimate ego trip if you believe this one. The only thing that exists is you, your mind, even your body might be a bit dodgy if you don’t think hard enough. See George Berkeley and Rene Descartes.

Stoicism Keep your emotions in check. They can cloud judgement, just take it on the chin. that’s the way of the stoics, plus a bit more. Zeno, he of the uncatchable tortoise, was an early proponent.

Taoism Going for a Chinese here, but chopsticks are not needed. Taoism (sometimes Daoism) is a spiritual, ancient Chinese, philosophy. It says people should seek to live in harmony with the Tao, or Dao, through meditation. The Tao is the base of the whole universe, see Laozi.

Transcendental Idealism Immanuel Kant had this idea that space and time are just something humans perceive. They are not necessarily the real thing. We make up knowledge of things from our sensory impressions.

Utilitarianism What results in the most good is the most good. Jeremy Bentham came up with this claim. John Stuart Mill rightly amended it.
