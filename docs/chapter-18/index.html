Modern view of the Bible

Anyone reading the Bible and trying to interpret it within the context of our current level of understanding will have trouble accepting its purported Godly truths. We have much more general knowledge than our forefathers. There are things we know of the world; it’s history; geography, cosmology and science generally that provide explanations not previously known. Prime examples are:

  • what we now know about evolution, through work started by Darwin and via the fossil record;
  • the age and development of the Earth, through Geological studies and cosmology has given us a much deeper understanding of the enormous size, age and complexity of the universe.

This wealth of modern knowledge helps lift the veil of mystery and superstition that underpins much of the Bible. It casts grave doubts on of the validity of much Biblical text. We know that the cosmos is about 13.8 billion years old. It was not formed and populated with the first humans in seven days, as claimed by the Genesis chapter of the Bible. The Earth is not at its centre. It is one tiny planet, 4.6 billion years old, among many billions of others. Our Earth is not 5,650 years old, as Bishop Ussher, in the 17th century, calculated from the Bible. We know that humans evolved rather than were formed directly by God. These are just a few of the many issues with the Bible that our modern knowledge and understanding throw up.

Theologians and Church leaders are brilliant at this. When modern knowledge shows some biblical stories to be blatantly untrue, then it is often claimed the stories are merely allegorical. They are not to be taken as gospel, it is said.

So, despite the evidence, or lack of it, that’s how quite a few reasonable and pleasant people may hold on to their beliefs. They are sometimes supported, and their belief encouraged, by governments and political leaders. These have found it useful to encourage belief.

Christianity, a weapon of politics and Empire building

The Bible underpins all Christian belief. It’s a fundamental holy text for today’s 2.4 billion Christians. This book has helped Christianity achieve an enormous impact on world history for the last 2,000 years.

Christianity and its Bible has been a handy political weapon. In 313 CE the Roman Emperor Constantine was the first to try and use the growing Christian faith in this way, on a large scale. He thought insisting on a common faith, Christianity, could be a unifying force across his vast, disparate realm. It was Constantine who called the First Council of Nicea, in 325CE. This resulted in the first clear statement of Christian belief - the Nicene Creed. He also made Sundays a day of rest.

Since Constantine governing structures have often used Christianity as a supportive and controlling prop. It’s been an instrument of Empire building by European nations from the early middle-ages. This helped establish Christianity in places like Africa, South and North America, Australia, New Zealand and the far east; most of the world in fact.

From Constantine and later the Crusades - war between Christians and Muslims - the Christian God has regularly been invoked, sometimes by both sides in a conflict. Even in modern times antagonists have claimed that God is on their side. The British Prime Minister, Winston Churchill, said this at the start of World War 2, in in his first speech as PM, in 1940: “…to wage war, by sea, land and air, with all our might and with all the strength that God can give us.” Before the German, air armada blitz, again in 1940, he said: “Upon this battle depends the survival of Christian civilisation.”

Churchill’s main antagonist, the Nazi Adolf Hitler, also appealed to God’s support or claimed to have it in his book Mein Kampf he wrote: “I believe today that my conduct is in accordance with the will of the Almighty Creator.”

The full Christian story is a long one. Diarmaid MacCulloch covers it quite thoroughly in his 1,100-page tome: “A History of Christianity.”

What’s on offer overrides all misgivings

Finally, almost, for many believers the advantages of belief, as described in earlier chapters, helps swamp any niggling misgivings that believers might have. There are just a few more issues to raise in a subsequent chapter. There’s the question of the soul, eternity, heaven and hell and an imagined conversation between God and Archangel Gabriel concerning God’s plan.
