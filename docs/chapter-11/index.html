Chapter 11

Moving from City-states to Civilisation

Pages 87–97

City-states, like Jericho and Catal Höyük, were individual, a law unto themselves. Next on the ever upward ladder of human development was the banding together of several cities into a distinct, homogeneous group with common governance and laws. Trade was more easily developed in a safer setting provided by the power of the bigger state. The sense of group belonging was re-enforced by increasingly developed religious beliefs. Countries and what we would call civilisations were born.

And what was the first civilisation that we know of? There are a few early cultures in the frame. The Harappan folk of the Indus valley in north west India and parts of Pakistan are one. Here cities were built, including the mud-brick houses of Mohenjo-Daro, this was from around 2,600 BCE. Some claim it was much earlier.

Mohenjo-Daro and other Harappan cities of the Indus Valley Civilisation, about 2,600 BCE

These towns or cities joined up and developed into a regional culture which lasted in the early period for around 700 years. Then there are various claims that an ancient Chinese civilisation was the first of all. The Xia then Shang Chinese dynasties are thought to have existed about 2,000 and 1600 BCE respectively, but not much is known about what these consisted of. More on these later.

Despite that early modern humans took a long time to reach South America, Peru can claim to be the home of an ancient civilisation at Caral. This was a large settlement of more than 3,000 people and dates back over 4,000 years. It was around at the same time the great pyramids of Egypt were constructed. Caral had a temple and amphitheatre as well as houses. It was one of about thirty population centres, comprising the Norte Chico civilisation.

Here comes Sumer, Earth’s first Civilisation (probably!)

However, in the debate over what is Earth’s first civilisation most experts say that the accolade can be given to Sumer. Located in the middle east, all the trappings of a civilisation were established here 6,000 years ago.

The title is not about the great Jerry Keller song of 1959. That was about summer, with two m’s, this Sumer only has one and was around a long time before Jerry’s.

Sumer, Mesopotamia, now largely Iraq

Sumer was the name for a group of cities located in the fertile crescent, Mesopotamia, this was the land between the Tigris and Euphrates rivers in the Middle East. These are all references to the same geographic area of what we know today as mostly Iraq, with parts of Kuwait and Iran.

Sumer was the earliest grouping of cities over a wide geographical area. It existed as early as 4,500 BCE. The land was named Sumer but it might be more appropriate to call this, the first flowering of civilisation, Spring rather than Sumer. The Sumerians did not know they were the first. They may have been aware though that theirs was a different, bigger, wider culture, because Sumer has a distinct and appropriate meaning: “the land of the civilised lords”. Why was Sumer the first civilisation on planet Earth? We cannot know for sure but there are positive indicators.

p.89

Firstly, the area of Sumer is on the route to Asia and Europe for MH, after their breakout from their original home in Africa. By following the coast line, from the Red Sea crossing from Ethiopia into Yemen, MH eventually reach the Persian Gulf and Mesopotamia. Here, settlers would find a rich land for hunting and gathering, with a good climate and plenty of fresh water. At a later stage the two rivers, Euphrates and Tigris, would help with transport and trade. It took thousands of years for MH to reach Europe, Asia and other areas. MH living in Mesopotamia could use this time to develop first agriculture then settled communities and later on individual city-states. Let’s imagine how Sumer began to expand from its earliest city-state base of Eridu.

Ashtar stopped. He knew that with ten steps still to climb he was out of sight from anyone on the altar platform above. No one could see, him, in the faint, glowing light that preceded the dawn. He could not appear to be weak. To maintain his prestige, as High Priest, he must always give the impression of indomitable strength and reliability. That was why he was now pausing, to gather his breath, to stop wheezing and gasping. The climb up the 120 steep steps of the Ziggurat was exhausting for anyone. But Ashtar had been making this climb twice a day for the last 5 years, ever since this first mighty temple to the god Enki had been constructed. And Ashtar was now 54 years old, well past the average life span of most Sumerians.

He began to breathe evenly and slowly. After pausing to adjust his tunic he calmly climbed the last few steps. As he stepped out on to the wide stone platform a young figure hurried towards him. Ashtar maintained his rigid posture and stern demeanour. He knew that his young acolyte would be waiting for him, despite it was still not quite dawn. Imoun smiled briefly but with his head slightly bowed so Ashtar could not see his expression. He had known for many months the procedure that Ashtar followed when climbing the Ziggurat. But Imoun was devoted to Ashtar and did not want his master to lose face, so he pretended to be unaware of the rest that Ashtar took when near to the temple platform.

The caged chicken which Imoun held did not know it was fated to be sacrificed. Ashtar said: “Imoun, we will pause before making the dawn sacrifice. I want to look to the south in the light of the rising sun.” Ashtar and Imoun walked to the southern edge of the platform and stared southward. In the sun’s red morning glow, they could just make out in the very far distance wisps of dark smoke. Imoun asked: “What are you looking for master?” “Your eyes are sharper than mine Imoun, what do you see.” said Ashtar, pointing dramatically.

“I see smoke faintly rising. The fire must be a long way away.” “That is the future you look at Imoun. The smoke signals the mighty work our great King Meskiagkasher has commissioned. Uruk will be a city the like of which no one has seen before. The smoke is from the cooking fires of 2,000 workers.”

Ashtar and Imoun are imaginary people. They must have had extremely good eyesight because they could see smoke in Uruk about 45 kilometres from their temple in Eridu, but the atmosphere was much clearer back then. Eridu is considered the first town/city of a region later to be called Sumer. It had expanded from a village, at the centre of a small agriculture community about 5,400 BCE. A number of autonomous city-states grew up alongside Eridu and Uruk in this region - the fertile crescent between the rivers Tigris and Euphrates. This area became the cradle of civilisation on planet Earth.

The very early culture in Eridu and immediate region was not Sumerian but known as the Ubaid culture, named after a village, Al-Ubaid, where remains were first uncovered. Ubaid society had a hierarchical structure but with elements of democratic decision making. They had a chieftain-priest as top man who was advised by a council, although this structure may have changed over the 2,700 years of the Ubaid period (about 6,500 to 3,800 BCE), especially because Semitic immigrants brought and integrated their own cultures. The Ubaids also had painted pottery; made tools like hoes, sickles, knives and adzes. Importantly, they made bricks, an essential component for building. They were expert in irrigation, draining marshes and used this skill to keep their drier southern settlements going. The climate became increasingly arid and eventually forced the abandonment of villages. This was the prelude for the end of the Ubaid period. Their culture represented an embryonic civilisation, and they gifted important elements to Sumer, especially their governance and religious worship.

People called Sumerians came to the Ubaid region, possibly from Anatolia (modern Turkey) and probably mixed with local populations. By 5,000 years ago there were several Sumerian cities like Eridu, Uruk, Ur, Kish, Nippur, Lagash, Bab-Tibira and Larsa. Each of these was characterised by certain common features. They comprised a walled city, with surrounding villages and agricultural land. Each city had its own deity whose temple was the main, central building. Early on, the citizens held political power but gradually kingship became the form of governance. These were features of the Sumerian, Uruk period, which succeeded Ubaid. Uruk, for its time, grew to become a big city and the most important in Sumer.

p.91

The Sumerians referred to themselves as the “black-headed people” and to Sumer simply as “the land”. Much of what is known of Sumer comes from both the archaeological record and the Sumerian King-Lists, although the latter is clearly a heroic record. Several versions of the lists have been discovered by archaeologists. The oldest, from about 2,000 to 2,500 BCE, come from Larsa and Nippur. They were written in the Sumerian, cuneiform language on clay tablets. The lists attempt to show continuity of governance, by listing all the kings of the region in order. They claim that Etana of Kish was the first King to bring a common order to the land. Before him the city-states were probably broadly independent.

The Sumerian King List tells of eight kings before the great flood. In the flood myth Ziusudra, who was the last king of Sumer before the mighty flood, heard of the Gods decision to drown humanity, apparently humans were becoming a serious nuisance. Ziu built a very large ship to save himself from the flood.

Noah’s Ark: the Bible’s version of the flood story told earlier of Ziusudra and Utnapishtim. Woodcut, Koberger Bible, 1483 (University of Edinburgh, CC BY 2.0)

This myth is repeated later in the 1800 BCE poem, the Epic of Gilgamesh, where Utnapishtim builds a huge vessel to save himself, his family and animals. Floods were thought to be the Gods punishment for human wrong-doings. The same flood story appears in other ancient cultures and in the Bible, but for biblical readers it is Yahweh who is the instigator, not the Sumerian or Mesopotamian Gods.

One feature of the pre-deluge kings of Sumer was their unbelievably long-life spans (shades of biblical Methuselah). The 4,000-year-old Weld-Blundell cuneiform stone tablet, which is in the Ashmolean Museum explains: “After the kingship descended from heaven, the kingship was in Eridug. In Eridug, Alulim became king; he ruled for 28,800 years.” Eridug was Eridu.

p.92

Sumer became a powerful region of cities holding allegiance to one King, although the cities continued to vie with each other. This was the first such arrangement, as far as is known, on planet Earth. Sumer had all the trappings of a discrete civilisation with the first written word, government, laws, trade, taxes, customs and a social hierarchy. They certainly had a sense of humour, some Sumerian sayings: “For his pleasure, marriage. On his thinking it over, divorce.” And “You may have a lord, you may have a king, but the man to fear is the tax collector.” But the key cohesive element of Sumer’s civilisation was religion.

Governance and religion

Sumerians believed their civilisation was the result of the Gods triumph over chaos. Each of the cities of Sumer had a priest-King. These Kings ruled originally with advice from a General Assembly made up elected men - evidence that democracy, of some sort, pre-dated the ancient Greeks! This system eroded over time and Kings, known as lugals, became more or less the sole power. Kings claimed their authority came from the city God and that they were able to act as the mediator between citizens and their God. This is the earliest example we have of the use made of religion to support the ruling body.

Religion was integral to all aspects of life. Citizens often had specific duties to perform related to the temple and its maintenance. They thought that civilisation came about through the Gods victory of order over chaos. The Sumerian creation myth claimed the God An, and Heaven and Earth, came from the primeval sea, called Abzu or Nammu. At a later stage in Mesopotamian history, Abzu was renamed Tiamat, a dragon like monster and Goddess of the watery deep. Tiamat was part of another creation myth. She battled the Gods champion, Marduk, who eventually slayed her. Marduk, according to Babylonian texts, founded Eridu, the earliest city of Sumer.

p.93

Sumerians worshipped four creator Gods:

  • Enki (God of water),
  • Ki (God of earth),
  • Enlil (God of air and storms),
  • An (God of heaven).

An was counted as the most important. His wife was Ki. Sumerians believed that these Gods ruled the universe, which they thought was heaven and earth. There were hundreds of other Sumerian Gods, all brought into being by the four original creators.

The individual city Gods, all anthropomorphic, were varied. Citizens of the famous city of Ur patronised the moon God Nanna, Eridu’s God was Enki and Kish worshipped Zababa, the warrior God. Regular worship, which included animal sacrifice, took place in the most dominant, mud-brick city building, the temple. This later developed in the shape of a ziggurat - a pyramidal construction with a platform at the top.

Beyond the early days of Sumer

Sumerians believed in an afterlife, although it was a dismal, dark one where people sat around eating dust and clay. This was an early version of hell. It may possibly have influenced later Jewish, Christian and Islamic ideas. They hyped up hell to be a fiery, much more frightening place, than the dull, boring one of Sumer. The Sumerian Heaven was reserved for the Gods.

Sumer existed for 1,000 years before invaders from the North, Akkadians, defeated them. Their leader was Sargon I. Several stories are told of Sargon. In one he was claimed to have once been a lowly cup-bearer to the King of Kish. In another the baby Sargon was found floating on a river in a basket made of reeds - shades of the later Biblical story of Moses, more plagiarism perhaps?

Akkadians lasted about 200 years, up to 2154 BCE, before morphing eventually into Assyria in the North and Babylonia in the South. These later people, the Assyrians and Babylonians continued with heavy-duty religious beliefs, with the polytheistic, huge pantheon of Gods that had been pioneered by the land of Sumer. Their names and/or functions differed though. In ancient Assyria, Assur was King of the Gods.

p.95

There was a god of wisdom, Enki, Nisaba was first a Goddess of agriculture then became in charge of writing, perhaps she got too old to be out in the field all day. Biblical Nimrod, (Genesis 10:8-9) apparent builder of the city of Nineveh and possibly a King, was also accorded divine status.

One famous Assyrian warrior King was Ashurbanipal. He expanded Assyrian territory and control to such an extent this was the first empire on Earth. Ashurbanipal was an unusual warrior King, he was interested in books, or their equivalent. These were written on papyrus and sometimes chiselled on stone. At his palace in Nineveh he assembled a huge library and was proud to be a literate king, unlike his predecessors. His pride did not end there, he said: “I am Ashurbanipal: King of the World, King of Assyria.” His reign lasted over 40 years, from 668 to 627 BCE.

Ashurbanipal hunting on horseback 645-635 BCE (British Museum)

The Babylonians, vassals to the west of Assyria, captured Nineveh and conquered Assyria in 605 BCE, after many civil wars. This was perhaps the height of Babylonian power, but the city itself and its rulers had held sway in the region some 1,000 years before. At this time Hammurabi was king of Babylon, in 1810 to 1750 BCE. Ancient Babylon had lots of Gods and religious rules. Clearly though, the rules were insufficient so thought Hammurabi.

p.96

In 1754 BCE the Code of Hammurabi appeared with 282 laws chiselled on to a 2.25 metre stone. Almost half the laws concern contractual matters, like the wages to be paid to an ox driver. They do not say how much should be paid to a lawyer.

The length list of laws is a sign of the increasing complexity of society and the attempt to establish some commonality of behaviour, with penalties for infringements. This is a core feature of any civilisation. Hammurabi should be revered by all lawyers because his Code of Hammurabi was the first major listing of laws.

Abraham, the first prophet of Judaism, Christianity and Islam, was thought to have been born in Ur just before the reign of Hammurabi. Ur, was a Sumerian city originally. There is no absolutely clear evidence of Abraham’s existence.

The fertile crescent, bounded by the two rivers, Euphrates and Tigris, was also known as Mesopotamia. This was an attractive piece of real estate and so was squabbled over by various peoples. Sumerians, Akkadians and Babylonians have already been mentioned. There were also Elamites, Ammorites and Hittites. All of them had a wide range of Gods and Goddesses.

The Hittites had an unusual claim to fame. They introduced iron. Before this bronze had been the main metal for use in weaponry. Their realm, at its height, covered the northern part of what was Sumer and a large chunk of modern-day Turkey. Assyria was to the south and ancient Egypt to the west.

Hittites Religion

Hittites tended to adopt the deities of the Hattic people that they partly displaced. They were also influenced by Sumerian and Akkadian religion. As a result, in common with other early middle-eastern culture, they had a large number of Gods and Goddesses. The total number of Gods named on various stone tablets exceed 600.

For the Hittites, Gods were highly knowledgeable but not omnipotent, or omniscient. Theirs were Gods to ask favours of, through prayer. It seemed though that the Gods reserved the Hittite afterlife only for Kings and high

Just like the Sumerian Marduk and Tiamat myth, the Hittites told of a weather God battling with the monster Illuyankase. This time the monster was an enormous serpent, rather than a dragon. And again, like some Sumerian rulers, Hittite Kings had divine status, although this did not save them from assassination.

Hittites are mentioned several times in the Bible under the name Heth in Genesis. In the Book of Judges 1:26, the Israelites allowed one man to escape to: “the land of Hittites”. These references, together with Abraham, who lived in Ur make clear Biblical authors were fully aware of the various Mesopotamian powers. This knowledge may have extended to the flood, creation, and afterlife myths which they then could have adopted and embellished.

The Hittites were about for some four hundred years, from 1600 to 1178 BCE. Eventually, they were fatally weakened by battles with Assyria and Egypt, and the disruptive, murderous mayhem within the Hittite royal family.
