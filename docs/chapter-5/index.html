Chapter 5

When Coming up Now and Noth's Story

Pages 40–48

So far, the why and what of God has been explained. This, is partly conjectural, although based on a strong thread of reasoning. We move on to consider the issue of when God first became important to men. In this chapter concrete, pre-historic data will be presented showing man’s interest in a form of spirituality. We will see what solid evidence - literally solid - has been uncovered to demonstrate spiritual aspects from the dawn, or early morning of mankind - the Stone Age.

When was it that mankind became interested in some kind of God? If He’s been around always it follows that there ought to be some record of Him throughout every age of mankind. Modern Humans, that is Homo Sapiens, have existed for about 300,000 years, with a nod to those impatient Creationists. I’m going to try and ignore them from now on. They can really cramp one’s style for ages.

For a good chunk of this time (the 300k years) there were no recording and communications mechanisms like computers or mobile phones. One could be excused in calling this the pre-text age, but this period is more correctly known as the age of pre-history, or more commonly, the Stone Age. The implements and tips of hunting weapons were made of stone; hence the name Stone Age. There is yet another name for it. The Palaeolithic era is its scientific name, in a European context.

p.41

Stone Age man couldn’t write, let alone send Text messages. This wasn’t because he was high on drugs. This was the stone, not the stoned, age. He couldn’t write because there were no biros or paper, and he had not invented a written language. However, eventually he could shape objects and paint, he could imagine and wonder and he could develop a view of the world and his place in it. Mankind was short on numbers back then in pre-historic times. There were an estimated 20 million people on Earth at 30,000 BCE, one for about every 240 who exist today. This meant Stone Age hunter gatherers were very interested in fertility. The promise of new life was vital. It ensured continuance of the sparse family, clan or tribe.

Once people became too old or incapable of hunting they could not fall back on the state for help through meals-on-wheels and other social services. The only recourse they had was to look to kith and kin for aid. The old, who could no longer hunt or gather food, (“old” is a relative term with the average lifespan around 27), would either be looked after by the young, or they would wither and die. An adequate food supply was essential to survival and continuance, but food resources were often precarious, especially in winter. Stone Age mums could not just pop down to the supermarket when they ran out of muesli. This might explain why, once Stone Agers got into painting and sculpture they generally chose prey animals and fertility figures as their subjects; more on these later.

Because of the lack of written evidence from these pre-historic times, much remains a mystery to us of mans’ pagan beliefs. But we have indicators that strongly suggest Palaeolithic man was likely to have had some form of belief in the supernatural or spirit world. This has been reasonably conjectured from the many “earth mother”, fertility Goddess, carved figurines that have been excavated and from other evidence. Archaeologists have discovered that the bones of certain of the dead were laid out in a seemingly ritualistic way and the bodies buried with earthly objects.

The objects could be intended to be of help to the dead person’s spirit in the spirit world. Then there are the skulls, not only human but also those of cave bears. The way these were placed indicates they might have been associated with some form of religious ceremony. This is the hard evidence we have, together with a number of cave drawings.

We will look at all this in more detail after Noth’s story. Who or what is Noth you might ask? Well, wait a bit, all will be revealed in less than a young male attendant at a royal court (i.e. in a page)

We also know something of the environment within which Palaeolithic man lived. It was a harsh, hand to mouth existence. Death was never far away. Infant mortality was high and life expectancy short.

Unlike other animals, humans had high intelligence and an ability to conjecture. This would have led them to wonder about the world around them. At every age of mankind wonder and speculation has been core human attributes. These always lead to the desire and search for certainty or an acceptable truth. Stone Age man would not have been any different. But much more of the world was a mystery to him than it is to us now. Where did animals, trees and plants, come from? Why were some hunts successful and some not? Why was the weather on occasion amenable and at other times furiously unreasonable? How and why did people get sick and sometimes die? What happened to people when they died? The mighty sun, the moon and the stars, what roles did these play, in the material and spirit world?

p.43

Such thoughts must have crossed the mind of some Stone Age people. These early humans did not have the advantages of the knowledge we enjoy today. There was no weight of scientific study in zoology; botany, geology, climatology, astronomy and other sciences. They could not log on to the Internet to find the answer. Palaeolithic man had to arrive at some simpler understanding of all the mysteries and unknowns of the world he inhabited. It is a natural human tendency to seek out some form of answer. In the lack of any other explanation spirits or Gods could have been invented to help fill in Stone-agers gaps in knowledge and understanding of the natural world, the “safety deposit box” syndrome, referred to in the previous chapter. So, pre-historic religious beliefs would have acted as a spiritual polyfilla. They conveniently filled in holes in the comprehension that ancient humans had of their world. This embryonic religiosity established the first hillock of a religious mountain that was to grow and grow over subsequent millennia. Let’s borrow Dr Who’s time machine, the Tardis, and travel back 35,000 years and take a brief look at what’s going on in one Stone Age family. We arrive during a time sometimes referred to as the Transition. Modern man is still the hunter gatherer he’s been for millennia. His tools are of flint and stone, as they have been for the preceding 265,000 years. Now though, they have become more sophisticated and varied. Great changes are afoot, truly a major shift from what has gone before.

In this period of transformation modern man is not only developing his hunting weapons and creative abilities, more importantly, he’s improving his language skills. In addition, he also pushes out, or kills and replaces, his primitive cousin and predecessor, the Neanderthals. These intelligent hominids lived in Europe and elsewhere before Homo Sapiens arrival. The Tardis, our time machine, takes us back 35 millennia to a place in what is now southern France, close to the Spanish border, and within a few miles of the Mediterranean Sea:

The flickering fire chased shadows across the rock face of the cave. Noth absent mindedly added another log to the glowing embers. It was late at night. He’d been up for hours after his family had gone to their bed furs. Cassea, his wife, had fallen asleep last. Looking across the fire, to the back of the cave, Noth can see the five of them huddled in their furs. Cassea was nearest to him, her black hair shrouding a round face; still beautiful even though she was now over 23 years old. Noth smiled tenderly. His eyes shifted from Cassea to his three children. They were all a man could wish for. Huddled close on Cassea’s left was Lurco; the youngest at seven years old, Lurco was inquisitive and alert. His interest extended to everything. He asked questions non-stop. On the other side of her mother slept Semith, his daughter. Her sharp features and fair hair were in keeping with her nature. She was like a little jewel. She sparkled and darted everywhere, full of laughter. Every clan hearth brightened up when Semith paid a visit. Tam, a little away from his two siblings, was the eldest. He was tall and strong for a young boy. In temperament, he was calm and wise beyond his 10 years. He helped Cassea with the younger two, as did Cassea’s mother, Letia. She was at the back of the cave, sleeping in the heaviest furs. Her grey hair and lined face were a testament to her 36 years. Noth turned back to look again at his son Tam. Today, had been a milestone for Tam. For the first time, he had gone on the hunt, with Noth and three other clan adults. Tam was instructed before they started out. He was told to move quietly and keep still when signalled.

p.44

He had always to remain at the back of the group, with his father. Tam found it hard to keep up with the adults, but Noth was proud that his son never complained of the pace and had not fallen behind.

The hunters made for a river that danced and tumbled through a valley in the mountain foothills. It was just a morning’s fast walk from the clan’s camp and a favourite place for the clan to hunt. Animals often came down to the stream bank to drink. A good hunter was sure to make a kill. The hunters skirted the river, remaining alert and quiet. They picked their way between dense shrubs and trees before halting at the edge of a small clearing. Here the river bank sloped shallowly down to the swift stream. And there was their prey, standing in the shallow water. Tam was not allowed to take a direct role in the kill of the slowly limping great stag. A spear was deeply embedded in its left flank. This had clearly weakened the deer, so the kill was easy and quick for the experienced hunters. They gave humble thanks to the spirit of the deer in a brief ceremony. This concluded with Tam, having to drink blood of the kill; signifying a new hunter’s rite of passage.

Noth pulled out the strange spear and examined it closely. Its point had been crudely struck from a flint and was roughly bound to a heavy wooden shaft. It lacked the sophistication and good balance of a human spear. He knew at once that it was a weapon of the low-brows. There were still some around. Humans and low-brows tried to avoid each other. There had been many more low-brows when Noth’s clan came to the area some 8 years ago, but most of them had gone since then. The four adult hunters staggered slowly home each dragging two poles hitched over their shoulders with a hide stretched between. Tam was pleased that he was allowed to carry the low-brows’ spear back to the clan dwelling caves. Children excitedly crowded around the hunters when they returned, Semith skipping between Noth and Tam. Lurco wanted to know why Tam was carrying a strange spear. Cassea was relieved that Tam and Noth were back safely from the hunt. Letia knew Tam would be thirsty and had a drink ready for him. After gulping cool water from the drinking horn, Tam was ready to tell his tale to a big audience. These were mostly children but some adults too wanted to hear from the clan’s newest hunter and find out more about the strange spear. Noth recalled the serious and unassuming way that Tam told his story of the hunt. Tam praised his fellow hunters and did not try and claim any of the credit, nor did he dwell on the arduousness of the return journey. Noth knew that Tam had shown by his conduct that he would be a clan leader one day.

He had told of the day’s hunt accurately and without embellishment, to the slight disappointment of some of the children. They wanted to hear a wilder, even more exciting tale. Noth was enjoying his reminiscence of the past day, but knew he must stop and plan for something that would cause a great upheaval in his clan and probably in other clans too. The children were going to get their excitement soon, although not in any way that they could anticipate.

Noth got up from the fire side and walked to the cave entrance. The cave was low down on a rocky hill side. But it was still some way above the valley floor. He could make out the shape of trees in the gloom. The sounds of the night-time drifted up to him from the woods below. He recognized all of them. There was the hoot of an owl, the barking of a fox nearby, and very faintly, in the distance, he could identify the sound of a wolf-pack. He raised his head to study the night sky. Dominating the sky was a pock marked white ball; the full moon. It was haloed with a host of twinkling lights. He felt a thrill of excitement. He had worked it all out clearly in his mind. At last he understood, after months of thinking. He now knew how the moon, the stars and the mighty sun fitted into the world of spirits, especially those elemental entities; the Earth mother and the spirit of the hunt, symbolized by the clan rock paintings and the mighty cave bear. The spirits occupied a mystical realm, beyond the material world around him; one that only the clan shamans had regular access to. The shamans though were conservative and repetitive. They repeated the traditional mantras at every clan event that honoured the spirits. Noth had become increasingly dissatisfied at the shamans limited view of the spirit world. For some time he had thought that the mighty sun; moon and countless stars must have a role too, although the shamans never mentioned them.

p.46

Noth did not know it, but he was a genius. He was able to conceptualise and use his high intelligence in ways that no one else in his clan, or in most other clans, could. He was capable of exploring areas of thought outside the demands of day-to-day living in a harsh environment. Analysis, interpretation and origination came relatively easily to him, even though he was wholly unaware of this terminology. As he studied the face of the moon Noth felt a light touch on his arm. Cassea was at his side. She smiled and leant her head on his shoulder. “Why are you up so late Noth? You need your rest after the hunt today.” “I’ve been thinking, mostly about Tam and the hunt.” said Noth. “I know you, my husband.” responded Cassea, “that’s not all you’ve been pondering. For months now you’ve had that faraway look on your face whenever you get more than a few minutes free.” Noth was the youngest elder of the clan. Cassea appreciated that he was highly respected for his knowledge of animals, their habits and the best way to track and catch them. When cave bears and other larger prey were scarce last winter the clan hunters attempted to spear river fish, but the fish often slid back into the water off the spear point. It was Noth who had the idea of making a shallow notch in the spear point, to hook on to the fish. The clan was amazed at the effectiveness of this innovation. Soon boys were spearing fish as well as adult hunters. Cassea speculated that Noth was about to come out with another important new idea. She did not know what it was though and was determined to get Noth to tell her, so she went on: “You’ve been planning something haven’t you?” Noth looked sideways at her; did Cassea know what he had been thinking? It wouldn’t surprise him. She was so astute. He sometimes wondered whether she could read his mind. He decided it was time she knew: “I wanted to tell you Cassea, but not before I was sure. Look up, what do you see?” Cassea did as Noth asked and craned her neck: “I see a black, black night sky studded with little lights. Of course, I can also see the round face of our moon. It’s shining on us so brightly tonight.” Noth nodded: “I’m sure I know what those lights are Cassea.” and he went on: “The shamans say that when we die, our spirits move into the spirit world above. But they have never been able to explain exactly where this world is that the spirits inhabit. I think the shamans “world above” is as we see it now. Each one of those flickering lights, stars, is an ancestor. They are our departed kith and kin looking down on us.”

p.47

Cassea studied the lights more closely: “There are so many.” “Yes, there has to be.” said Noth. “Think how many generations have died and passed into the spirit world.” This was a new concept to Cassea, but she realized the spirits must go somewhere above. “Yes, I can understand what you say husband. Perhaps that is why the lights twinkle, our ancestors are trying to keep in touch with us, their Earthly brethren.” Noth was slightly taken aback at Cassea’s perception. “There is more.” he said, “The ancestor-stars are many but small. They sit above us at night with the moon. The moon is much bigger than any star, even when it hides part of itself. On cloudless nights, it gives us gentle light. It must be an important spirit.” “Is that its only role, to sometimes give us light in the darkness?” said Cassea, Noth put his arm around Cassea’s waist. Then replied: “No, there’s the sun too. The moon has a role linked to the sun I think. How essential the sun is for us. It lights our day much more brightly than the moon, so we can hunt. The cave bear spirit is only captured when the sun lights the way of our hunters. The sun warms us and at winter’s end it melts the frost and snow. Great Mother Earth waits for the sun to awaken her from her winter’s slumber. Earth life is renewed every spring through the gentle caress of the sun’s golden rays. New plants begin to grow and leaves uncurl on the bare branches of trees. The sun must be the mightiest spirit to achieve all this, yet we do not recognize this or honour it, as we should.” Cassea: “Yes, perhaps this is why no one can look on the face of such a powerful spirit when it shines in a bright blue sky. But, if the sun is the greatest of spirits, what is the moon?” Noth was pleased that Cassea had so readily accepted his ideas. He eagerly responded: “The moon gives a softer light than the sun. It’s much smaller and usually gives way to the sun, although they are sometimes together in the sky above us. I think the moon is like a woman. I believe it is the wife of the sun. We must honour her too.”

Six weeks later, at the mid-summer gathering of all the local clans, Noth stood and spoke powerfully about his beliefs. He had intelligently paved the way beforehand. His own clan shaman and chief knew that Noth would make an important revelation about the spirit world. The shaman was uneasy over the prospect of anyone meddling in his territory, but he respected Noth. It also helped that Noth had told him that his shaman status would be enhanced after he had spoken.

Nearly a hundred and fifty people listened entranced to Noth as he wove his tale with eloquence and conviction. Many glanced up at the sky as he spoke. They hoped to catch a clearer sign of the sky spirits that Noth told of. By co-incidence as the camp fires blazed in the darkening early night, a shooting star wove a fiery trail across the heavens. Then another followed and finally a third.

People shouted in awe at the sight. Noth seized on this chance to prove his case. He pointed dramatically skywards and said: “Look, the sun, moon and ancestral spirits have been listening to me. They tell us that I am right. They want our respect and honour.”

Noth did not know it but he had started a revolution in Palaeolithic man’s concept of the spirit world. A decade later, after Noth had died, Tam had first taken over the chieftainship of the clan. An enraged cave bear killed Tam in the first year of his leadership. Lurco was the natural successor to Tam. His sharp wit, perception and boundless energy helped him, in time, to become a great clan chief. Semith became known and respected by everyone. She always wanted to help, especially when death marked a transition to the spirit world. She easily took up the role of a revered female shaman. Together with Lurco, she promoted and spread her father, Noth’s, revolutionary ideas among many clans.

The concept of a sun and moon God took thousands of years to spread among the Homo Sapiens communities. This was because people were few on the planet and distances great when walking is the main means of transport. Communication between far flung communities was slow and infrequent. But Noth’s ideas endured for thousands of years. They were taken up and developed by the Mesolithic people who eventually built the first Mesopotamian town - Eridu and the first city of Sumer - Uruk.

Of course, this is just a story made up by me, the author of this book. Noth, Cassea, Tam and the rest did not exist. Noth was but a figment of imagination. He was, in effect nothing. Hence his name, “Noth”. But “Nothing” can sometimes give rise to something. Our Universe started from nothing - if this is what preceded the point of singularity 13.8 billion years ago. Look at what’s become of that particular “nothing”. From it a great deal of “something” has arisen with the multitude of galaxies, stars and planets that now populate the cosmos. Noth’s story may also lead us to something, not remotely on the scale of the expanding singularity of course. There must have been very clever people like Noth in the transitional period from Middle to Upper Palaeolithic. Incidentally, this period was also known as the Late Stone Age, especially in reference to Africa. Another name for it is the Reindeer Age, for more obvious reasons, i.e. there were a lot of reindeer about.

Some palaeontologists refer to this time as the Upper Palaeolithic Revolution. Whatever the terminology used - Transition; Late Stone Age, Reindeer Age; or the Upper Palaeolithic Revolution - it lasted between 45,000 and 20,000 years ago. Because it’s a time of change, Transition probably best describes this new period.
