Chapter 2

Why, why, why?

Pages 12–20

If it is accepted that there has never been a conclusive proof of God, why is it that He has existed for so many humans? Is this something to do with our humanity?

It is difficult to imagine logic centered robots having any truck with Gods. But humans are not robots. We are not coldly logical computers, with appendages added on. Our brains allow us to compute certainly, but we also have emotions and feelings like love, hate, empathy, derision, worry, hope, despair, fear, contentment, pain and happiness. A complex mix of logical reasoning, sentiment and biological responses (mainly physical pain, hunger and sex!) guides human thinking and actions. Added to the maelstrom mixture that comprises the human psyche is our realization of a limited shelf-life. We know we will kick the bucket; croak it, pass away or pop our clogs - whatever is our favourite euphemism for the act of dying.

All this can mean that sometimes some of us are prepared to believe something to be true without clear evidence, or any indisputable line of reasoning. This is largely because humans want to believe and accept what others say, if it suits them. The result is that Gods have had not just a field day but an entire savanna epoch over the course of modern human existence.

Deities, and the attributes that humans have conveniently attached to them, have grown to fill a large, vacant niche. Exploring this niche reveals the compelling reasons why a God has so appealed to much of humanity.

Anthropologists John Monoghan and Peter Just say that, “it seems apparent that one thing religion or belief helps us do is deal with problems of human life that are significant, persistent, and intolerable. One important way in which religious beliefs accomplish this is by providing a set of ideas about how and why the world is put together that allows people to accommodate anxieties and deal with misfortune.”

p.13

Life Beyond The Grave

Homo Sapiens is unique in the modern animal kingdom. The “modern” distinction excludes our intelligent but extinct Hominin ancestors; including Homo Heidelbergensis, Homo Erectus, Homo Habilis, and our cousins, the Neanderthal, who died out about 18,000 years ago. We, modern man, have intelligence, emotions and a restless imagination. These attributes enable us to have a strong and usually unwelcome appreciation of our own individual mortality. Godly development may well have arisen, in part, to mitigate against our realisation of eventual death and nothingness. A form of spiritual or religious belief has helped humans over eons dismiss the thought of an inevitable void. Faith offers the prospect of continuance after death. This advantage makes religions, any religion, enticing to embrace. For archaic humans and indeed for later ones; the concept of some type of superior being - a God(s), and his promise of an afterlife was both reassuring and comforting. As Voltaire said: “If God did not exist it would be necessary to invent him.” However, He is invented whether or not He exists.

Supernatural tendencies, science and evolution

This emotional yearning for continuance has been re-enforced by other fundamental human tendencies. There is scientific evidence, not conclusive, to show humans, especially children, have a predisposition towards belief in spirits and the supernatural world. In this way belief in a type of God could be inherently hard-wired into all humans (Dean Hamer - “The God Gene”), from the earliest times of pre-history. No prizes for guessing who the religiously inclined consider to have done the hard-wiring. But there is a powerful alternative to God’s involvement in any innate human spirituality. Darwin puts a big oar in here. Belief in spirits or God is thought to have an evolutionary advantage. This is evolution not through competitive selection but through co-operation. The clubbiness and security offered through common spiritual beliefs would have helped cement fragile and sparse early human societies.

At the conclusion of a major 2011 research project Dr Justin Barrett, from the University of Oxford’s Centre for Anthropology and Mind, claimed that people who share an attachment to religion: “might be more likely to cooperate as societies”. In a hostile world, this would be an advantage to the continued existence of the species. Curiously, there is something of a dichotomy here. A partial reason for religious growth, could be the evolutionary advantage. Yet Darwin, the great evolutionary guru, was an atheist! Did he realize that evolution itself might be a reason for religious development?

p.14

So, we have the prospect of: immortality, a tendency to believe the supernatural and evolution in the frame as three possible reasons for God’s rise, over millennia, on a large balloon of faith, but these are not the only reasons for the development of Godly belief. There is more, quite a lot more.

We want it now

Human disposition and want is varied. Continuance through spiritual existence after a very final death is an attractive prospect. However, humans don’t want to wait for death until they get something beneficial from their revered supernatural being. HE’s got to be able to help out with life’s problems too. These further wants of an incorporeal immortal are another possible answer to the “why God” question. To achieve all the things humans want from life then a God has to be endowed with a set of superior powers and abilities. These enable Him to deliver the goods. In many societies, human “want” strengthened to “need”, as religious dogmas, doctrines, rituals and rites developed into a holy industry that, for thousands of years was ingrained into nearly all societal structures.

Safety Deposit Box

Let’s return to the sort of pre-death bonuses that humans have looked for from their Spirits or God. A common one is a secure place to lodge that which is disturbing or unknown. Down the ages God became a safety deposit box where the mysterious, unknowable and the fearful can be securely stored. Great natural events that were not explainable were attributed to a God or spirit, like the waxing and waning of the moon, eclipses and thunder and lightning. In Viking pagan mythology, thunder and lightning was the domain of the mighty, hammer-wielding Thor. Ancient Sumerians, Egyptians and many other cultures had sun and moon Gods or Goddesses.

Something unfathomable may be described as “an act of God”, or the will of god. Even today the former is a recognized legal phrase, mainly in the insurance industry, also when sometimes describing adverse natural phenomena.

p.15

Attributing what we now know are natural events to a God has provided a degree of comfort throughout ages past. No need to worry further when something happens that we do not understand or are afraid of. With God around we don’t have to work it out, it’s all down to Him. HE knows what it is all about. HE will sort it out for us. Some major events might induce worry though, they were considered portents of nasty things to come, or as a form of punishment for backsliding individuals.

But humans needed more positive attributes of a God than just this parking place for things they didn’t understand or feared. Over time, the role of superior beings, spirits and Gods, was expanded to address these new desires. Shamans, priests and other religious authorities gradually invented new, attractive and sometimes frightening abilities and dispositions attributed to their spirits and Gods.

Humans can feel remorse or guilt for something they’ve done. These feelings are often because the society they live in or their religion might consider some individual act or omission to be a bad thing. In these circumstances forgiveness and a clean slate is something highly desirable, particularly if without this we might suffer in both the present and the afterlife. This “mercy” facility was an attribute often credited to God. Peace of mind and sometimes even secret forgiveness - “no one need know” - is attractively on offer through belief in God. Hence, the Roman Catholic confessional box.

Hope for the Best, now and ever after

The prospect of hope is yet another godly attraction. Hope of a better life now; with the help of a spirit or deity. Stone Age hunters probably hoped their cave bear hunt would be successful and may have appealed to natural spirits to make it so. Later, Bronze Age farmers might have danced or prayed to a rain god. (There is evidence of this sort of behavior from studies of more recent indigenous peoples). In modern times, God could be appealed to for help with a winning lottery ticket.

p.16

If not a better life in this world people had hope that a God would lead them to a better life hereafter in an eternally pleasant heaven, or the equivalent of heaven. This prospect is a soothing and comforting thought when death is imminent; for the person dying and for their friends and relatives. People are more accepting of extreme hardships in this life with the knowledge of an eternally pleasant afterlife. Some have even been persuaded to kill themselves, or to kill others with the belief God wants this and will reward them in an afterlife.

A God has been the supreme source of appeal for help and support when the going gets tough. God is universally accessible - anyone can pray or appeal to Him through the right rituals. God is more available than any convenience store. HE is always open for business, 24/7, bank holidays included. HE is also quiet and unobtrusive. This is definitely so, have you ever heard or seen him?

To date God offers:

  • a constant listening service;
  • security and comfort;
  • forgiveness and the prospect of a clean slate;
  • hope and convenience;
  • a source of final arbitration;
  • an afterlife of eternal bliss.

For believers in the rewards of a godly life death would be just a staging post. It would not have the finality and generally unpleasant emptiness of a godless universe.

Ultimate Comfort Blanket

With all these benefits God(s) became the ultimate comfort blanket. This was in addition to His much-wanted other key role; as a safety deposit box. These two attributes are wholly in keeping with Maslow (1908 - 1970), the eminent American psychologist, Hierarchy of Needs, usually shown as a pyramid. They fit neatly into his second tier; i.e. security. The evolutionary advantage of group spiritual conformity matches with Maslow’s row three; i.e. belonging.

p.17

We mustn’t forget the personal evolutionary advantage. This could be ascribed to tier one, as a fundamental survival need. It also comes under Safety and Security in the Maslow analysis.

So, it can be argued that both Darwin and Maslow obliquely point to religious beliefs as being an evolutionary advantage. Even atheists ought to be grateful for that. If our ancient ancestors did not commonly share such beliefs, maybe none of us would be here. MHs could have died out.

We now have a longish and desirable tick list of Godly advantages. With all these, what more could be asked of a supreme being? A God who can provide so much is such an alluring prospect. Many people have desperately wanted to believe in Him because of this, regardless of the quality of evidence for His existence. “Wow, with those potential benefits, I’ll sign up to that belief” they decide, often in an unconscious way. To maintain widespread belief in a God, people must be reminded of these advantages, and of the disadvantages of non-belief. This moves us on to those who are His worldly representatives.

Heed me and feed me, I’m God’s representative

We must consider the power and status accorded to religious leaders; the shamans; witch doctors; medicine men; priests; vicars; bishops, rabbis, imams and their clerical ilk. It was to their advantage to strongly support and perpetuate the particular religious or spiritual brand that they avowed. They did, and still do this, through the traditional carrot and stick technique.

The “carrot” employed by these miscellaneous clerics was, and still is, the emphasis they put on all the advantages of belief in their creed. Often, conveniently, these advantages could only accrue after death.

p.18

Their own reward for succeeding in this task was frequently, with some altruistic exceptions, very earthly: money; food; housing; respect; influence and power. Not all were self-serving, power and status junkies, and money grabbers. Many had and have served their flock humbly, fairly and well, although perhaps based on a dubious premise.

Now for the “stick”. In the middle ages the Catholic Church made considerable sums from an astutely devised indulgence system. Sums of money were made by clergy promising eternal salvation and forgiveness of sins. Pay up and you can go to heaven, if you are poor and can’t pay, then it’s hell for you, seemed to be the message. This was a sort of combined reward and punishment scheme in one simple package. The clerics gained worldly reward from worrying people of dire after-life consequences of their actions.

Clergy and religious chiefs of all varieties warn of horrible things happening to any backsliders to keep them inline. In the not so distant past anyone of the wrong religious denomination could be tortured and killed. This continues, to an extent, even today, but it was more prevalent in times in the past. The crusades spanning the 11th to 13th centuries are a well-known example.

Even if of the right religion, sinners, after death, could expect to spend eternity in hell if they did not abide by certain rules of behavior. In some faiths, they might be re-incarnated as a cockroach. But often nasty retributions also occurred in the corporeal existence of the unfortunate sinner.

p.19

A famous example from the past is the punishment meted out by the Roman Catholic Spanish Inquisition, in the Middle Ages. In 1600CE poor Giordano Bruno was burned at the stake for his heresy in daring to state the sun was a star, and for his pantheist views. Prevailing religious dogma, unknowingly supported by Aristotle, nearly 2,000 years previously, said the Earth was the center of the universe. Later, in 1633CE Galileo was threatened with torture for supporting the Copernican theory that the Earth orbited the sun and not the other way around. There are many other examples of actual and threatened physical violence, even in the present day. In some parts of the Islamic world of today you might be stoned to death for adultery, or until recently, you could be whipped if you were a woman found driving a car. The Rabbinic law in Judaism, sets out eighteen crimes for whom the punishment is death by stoning.

Religions have set out a multiplicity of rewards for believers and punishments for lapses, of the wrong belief, or non-believers. This carrot and stick approach has been hugely successful in keeping adherents, well, adherents.

A summary of “Why”, with some extra’s

The benefits offered through the safety deposit box and the comfort blanket syndromes, are why many people want God(s) to exist. This desire is so strong it overrides any serious examination of evidence, especially so when combined with our cranial hard-wiring and evolutionary steer towards the supernatural. Added to this scenario is the encouragement by priests etc, in particular forms of belief. The lack of evidence does not mean that God is non-existent of course. He may actually be able to provide all those things in the box and under the blanket, because he does exist. But there is no solid proof of Godly existence, or is there? Some attempted proofs have been put forward.

Professed Proofs of God’s Existence

There have been and still are many believers, billions of them, in a God or spiritual figure. It is unlikely that these believers will admit to wanting to believe solely because of the safety deposit box and comfort blanket attractions. These two are bed-rock features underpinning belief, but over hundreds of years there has been a realisation by some believers that there cannot be a God just because people find it convenient to have one. Something more substantial was needed to support the belief of thinking, questioning people. Ergo theologians and their more humbly referenced predecessors have attempted to point to stronger signs of His existence.

p.20

They wanted to demonstrate there is a God by offering one or more proofs. These purported proofs can be split into two categories: (1) religious texts and (2) logical proofs.

Religious texts: Their very existence is pointed to by many of the faithful as proof of God and justification for their religion. Absolutism, ie. these writings are unchallengeable, is often claimed for various holy texts such as the: Bible (Christianity); Qur’an (Islam); Tanakh, of which the Torah is a part, (Judaism); sacred Vedas (Hinduism); Sutras (Buddhism) and Guru Granth Sahib (Sikhism). There are others.

The texts, particularly relating to the Abrahamic God, have often been disputed by unbelievers. In response to these challenges some past and present theologians have come up with a number of attempted Logical Proofs. These, they believe, give credence to the claim that God exists.

Logical proofs: Aside from sacred texts there are six main arguments made for God’s existence, mnemonic for these = COTHAM:

  1. Cosmological;
  2. Ontological;
  3. Teleological;
  4. Hard-wired;
  5. Anthropic Principle;
  6. Moralistic theory

You won’t find these six purported proofs neatly packaged together in any Google or Wiki search, but they certainly can be found individually in the vast body of literature on this subject.

All these attempted proofs are just that, attempts. They are not proofs. They demonstrate evidence of creative thought, but not evidence of the creator.
