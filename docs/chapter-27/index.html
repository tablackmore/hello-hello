Chapter 27

Can We Answer the Biggest Question?

Pages 459–462

Left Column

How did we get here in the first place? How did we go from the Big Bang to sitting in a chair, reading a book, and pondering the nature of reality?

We have traveled quite a journey. We’ve talked about what philosophers consider to be real. We’ve seen how physicists analyze different structures, with the atom at the center of it all. We asked where does everything come from? And we traced the elements in your body back to the Big Bang and up through stars that have long since died. We looked at the crazy world of the very small, the quantum realm, and all the weird implications it might have for our universe. And we have even pondered what it all means, which is a question perhaps best left for philosophers and poets.

We’ve focused primarily on the physical. It is what we can see and touch and measure. The scientific method has been the most successful tool in human history for understanding our universe. And the biggest question we can ask is also a question about the physical: Why is there something rather than nothing?

It is a deceptively simple question. Why is there a “here” at all? Shouldn’t there be just nothing—not even an empty space, but simply nothing? Why instead do we have matter, energy, space, and time? Why do we have pizza, dogs, iPhones, the Eiffel Tower, and the sound of Louis Armstrong singing “What a Wonderful World”?

It is the ultimate question and, before we answer it, let’s set the ground rules. Whatever reasoning we come up with, it has to be based on what we observe in the universe. We cannot invent things that are impossible. We need to at least be able to conceive of them being possible without contradicting what we know.

So, here is my attempt at an answer.

Before, there was nothing. Then, about 13.8 billion years ago, that changed. Suddenly, matter and energy existed. The dominant theory about this event is that it happened very quickly and with enormous amounts of heat. Everything we see around us started as a tiny speck, far smaller than an atom, which exploded and expanded at an incredible rate.

But why did this happen? What came before the Big Bang that could create such an event? This is perhaps one of the hardest questions for us to wrap our heads around because it requires us to think about a time before time. It is a state we simply cannot conceive of because we live in a world where things happen in sequences, one after another. Before, now, and then—this is the structure of how we experience everything.

However, the Big Bang created time itself. There may not have been a before. It isn’t that there was something before the Big Bang. It’s that there was no time in which anything could have happened before the Big Bang. Time came into existence with the Big Bang.

Right Column

This is hard to imagine, but that doesn’t make it impossible. Remember, reality doesn’t care about what makes sense to us. It just is.

There are other ideas about how the Big Bang came to be. Maybe our universe bubbled off from another one, like a balloon branching off from a larger balloon. Or maybe the Big Bang is just one event in a long chain of expansions and contractions—universes that expand, collapse back down into a tiny point, and then explode again. We don’t know which, if any, of these theories is right. But what matters is that they are all possible. They don’t contradict the known laws of physics.

Then, after the Big Bang, fundamental forces and matter began to take shape. The universe expanded and cooled. Atoms formed. Stars were born. Those stars forged the elements that make up our world. Some stars exploded in supernovas, scattering those elements across the cosmos. They drifted through space until gravity pulled them together into planets. On at least one of those planets, the conditions were right for life to emerge, and eventually, through billions of years of evolution, here we are.

All of this happened because of the physical laws that govern the universe. Gravity, electromagnetism, and the strong and weak nuclear forces shaped everything. These forces act consistently. They are not random. Because of this consistency, we can study them, understand them, and predict how they will behave.

The fact that the universe operates on rules means that we can answer many of the biggest questions. Science has given us the tools to look back in time, to see the remnants of the Big Bang, and to understand how stars are born and how they die. We have measured the structure of atoms. We have weighed the universe itself. We have traced the paths of light from the most distant galaxies.

p.460

But there is still a question we cannot fully answer: Why do those laws exist at all? Why is gravity the way it is? Why does electromagnetism work as it does? Why are the constants of nature—things like the speed of light or the charge of an electron—exactly what they are?

Some people argue that if any of these constants were even slightly different, life as we know it wouldn’t exist. The universe would be a very different place. Maybe matter wouldn’t form. Maybe stars wouldn’t burn. Maybe atoms couldn’t bond together to create molecules. This has led some to wonder if the universe was designed specifically for life to exist—a concept known as the anthropic principle.

Page 2

Left Column

But the anthropic principle is not proof of design. It simply states that we observe the universe to be the way it is because if it were different, we wouldn’t be here to observe it. That’s a bit circular, but it’s also true.

Another idea is that there are countless universes, each with different constants and different laws of physics. In most of them, nothing interesting happens. But in a few, the conditions are just right for stars, planets, and life to exist. We happen to be in one of those universes. This is called the multiverse hypothesis.

The multiverse is a controversial idea. Some scientists love it. Others hate it. The problem is that it’s very hard to test. If other universes exist, they are likely beyond our ability to observe. But that doesn’t mean the idea is worthless. It offers a possible explanation for why our universe seems fine-tuned for life. It wasn’t designed. We just happen to be in one of the rare universes where things worked out.

Whether you believe in the multiverse or not, the point is that there are possible answers to the biggest question. We don’t have to throw up our hands and say, “It’s all a mystery.” We can explore, hypothesize, and test our ideas against what we observe.

And even if we never fully answer the question of why there is something rather than nothing, that doesn’t mean the question is pointless. The search for answers has driven humanity forward. It has given us science, philosophy, art, and literature. It has pushed us to explore the farthest reaches of space and the tiniest particles of matter. The question itself is valuable, even if the answer remains elusive.

So, where does that leave us?

We live in a universe that exists. We don’t know exactly why, but we have some pretty good ideas. We know how matter and energy behave. We know how stars form and how galaxies spin. We know that the universe is vast, ancient, and governed by consistent laws. We also know that we are a part of it, made from the same atoms as everything else, connected to the cosmos in ways we are only beginning to understand.

That, in itself, is pretty amazing.

Right Column

And perhaps the most important thing we’ve learned is this: We are not separate from the universe. We are not outside observers looking in. We are the universe experiencing itself.

Every atom in your body was forged in a star. The calcium in your bones, the iron in your blood, the oxygen you breathe—all of it came from stellar furnaces billions of years ago. You are literally made of stardust.

That is not just poetic language. It is scientific fact.

When you look up at the night sky, you are not looking at something foreign. You are looking at your origins. The light from those stars has traveled for years, sometimes millions of years, to reach your eyes. You are seeing the past. And you are made from the same material as those distant suns.

We are connected to everything. The air, the ocean, the mountains, the stars—it’s all made from the same building blocks. The same forces that hold atoms together also hold us together. The same energy that powers the sun also powers your cells.

Understanding this changes how we see ourselves. We are not isolated beings clinging to a rock in the middle of nowhere. We are part of a cosmic story that stretches back billions of years. We are participants in an ongoing process of creation and transformation.

p.461

And that brings us back to the biggest question: Why is there something rather than nothing?

Maybe the answer is that the universe has always existed in some form. Maybe it is eternal, cycling through endless expansions and contractions. Maybe it bubbled off from another reality. Maybe it emerged from quantum fluctuations, where particles pop in and out of existence all the time. Maybe there is a multiverse, and our universe is just one of many.

Or maybe the question itself is flawed. Maybe “nothing” was never an option. Maybe existence is the default state, and nothingness is the illusion.

We don’t know. Not yet.

But we keep searching. Because that’s what humans do. We ask questions. We explore. We wonder. And in doing so, we become more than just collections of atoms. We become beings capable of understanding our place in the cosmos.

Because it took a traditionally described incomprehensible process, Humans made it comprehensible! By asking: “What is God?” Now, if you’re like me, you’re asking: What’s an incomprehensible process? It’s basically any process that we can’t understand by using the scientific method. If it has no reason, is governed by no rules, follows no laws, is subject to no cause and effect patterns, and cannot make sense of it. This would be the opposite of nature, something outside the laws of reality—something supranatural!

But remember, “we’ve reached a point where the incomprehensibility itself has taken a step around us” in the claimed creation. It becomes something we’ve reached to understand the world. Why? What does this mean? What’s going on here?

Because unlike nature, which moves, evolves, and changes from point to point, an incomprehensible “something” cannot be investigated, tested, measured, or predicted. It cannot be tested. That doesn’t mean the answer “God did it” is wrong. It just means we can’t investigate further if we accept that as the answer. Unlike other answers that can be tested, examined, and potentially proven wrong, a supernatural explanation stops all inquiry. We’ve reached a point where we can’t ask any more questions because the answer is “It’s just that way. God exists. That’s it.”

In other words, invoking God as the answer to the biggest question completes the inquiry. It isn’t an explanation—it’s a conversation stopper. It’s like asking why the sky is blue and being told “because God made it that way.” Okay, but that doesn’t answer the question. There are real, testable, scientific reasons why the sky appears blue. Invoking God doesn’t help us understand the process.

It’s a bit like saying a magician made a rabbit disappear. Maybe he did, but if you want to understand how the trick works, you need to look at the mechanism—the hidden compartment, the sleight of hand, the misdirection. The moment you say, “It’s magic,” you stop looking for the real answer.

Right Column

So, in question, does not seem to work because it’s based on the assumptions of God, but what do theologians say in an answer to it?

“God created the universe and sustains it.” He is reason enough for existence. He exists independently of all creation, and He has the power to create something from nothing. This idea solves the mystery. There’s a higher power responsible for all we see, and that power exists outside the constraints of time and space.

p.462

Now, here’s a fundamental problem with that explanation. If God created everything, who created God? Believers often say that God is eternal and needs no creator. He has always existed and will always exist. But if we can accept that God has no beginning, why can’t we accept that the universe has no beginning? Why do we need an extra step?

This is where the concept of an unmoved mover comes in. It’s an old philosophical idea, most famously argued by Aristotle and later adopted by Christian theologians like Thomas Aquinas. The idea is that everything that moves is moved by something else. But you can’t have an infinite chain of movers. At some point, there must be something that started it all, something that didn’t need to be moved itself. That something is God.

It’s an elegant argument, but it has a problem. If everything needs a cause, then God needs a cause. But if God doesn’t need a cause, then why does the universe need one?

Page 4

Left Column

Some believers argue that God is different from the universe. He exists outside of time and space, so He doesn’t need a cause. But this is a special pleading argument. We’re making an exception for God without good reason. If we say that some things don’t need causes, then maybe the universe itself doesn’t need a cause.

Another issue is that invoking God doesn’t actually answer the question. It just pushes the mystery back one step. Instead of asking “Why is there something rather than nothing?”, we’re now asking “Why is there a God rather than no God?” The fundamental question remains.

That said, belief in God is not inherently illogical. Many intelligent, rational people believe in a creator. But from a scientific perspective, it’s not a satisfying answer because it doesn’t give us anything we can test or investigate. Science thrives on questions that lead to more questions. A supernatural explanation ends the inquiry.

So, if God isn’t a scientific answer, does that mean science has all the answers? Not exactly.

Science is incredibly powerful. It has given us vaccines, space travel, the internet, and a deep understanding of how the universe works. But science has limits. It can only study things that are observable and testable. It cannot answer questions about meaning, purpose, or morality. It can tell us how the universe works, but it cannot tell us why it should exist at all.

Some people are comfortable with that. They see the universe as a brute fact—something that just is, without needing a deeper explanation. Others find this unsatisfying. They want an ultimate reason, a purpose behind it all.

Personally, I think both perspectives have value. Science gives us the best tools for understanding the physical world. But it doesn’t have to answer every question. Some mysteries may remain beyond our reach, and that’s okay.

Right Column

What matters is that we keep asking. We keep exploring. We keep pushing the boundaries of what we know. Because even if we never fully answer the biggest question, the journey itself is worthwhile.

So, can we answer the biggest question?

Maybe. Maybe not. But we’re getting closer every day.

We’ve learned that the universe began 13.8 billion years ago. We’ve discovered that it’s expanding. We’ve found that it operates on consistent laws. We’ve traced the history of matter from the Big Bang to the present day. We’ve uncovered the quantum nature of reality and the strange, counterintuitive ways that particles behave.

All of this is progress. All of this brings us closer to understanding why there is something rather than nothing.

And even if we never get a final, definitive answer, we can still appreciate the universe for what it is. We can marvel at its complexity, its beauty, and its sheer existence. We can recognize our place in it as small but significant, temporary but meaningful.

Because ultimately, the biggest question isn’t just about the universe. It’s about us. It’s about why we care. It’s about what it means to be conscious beings in a vast and ancient cosmos.

And that’s a question worth pondering, even if we never find the answer.

We are here. The universe is here. That alone is extraordinary.

And maybe, just maybe, that’s enough.
