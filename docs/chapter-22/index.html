What else if not Religion?

Pages 231–241

Religious belief in some countries may be declining. This is certainly so for Christianity in Britain, as indicated by the British Social Attitudes Survey (BSA). Their report in 2013, based on 3,000 people interviewed, showed the following:

  • 50.6% of the population regarded themselves as belonging to no religion in 2013, up from 47.7% in 2012. Only 41.7% defined themselves as Christian and just 16.3% as Anglicans, both lowest ever figures.
  • This marked decline in religiosity comes despite only 19.1% of respondents in 2013 saying that they had been brought up in a non-religious household
  • Just 24% of respondents saw Christianity as an important element of national identity in 2013, down from 32% in 1995 and 31% in 2003.
  • 62.4% of the population never attends religious services and meetings, whilst merely 13.1% go once a week or more.

Worldwide, in 2019, the irreligious number between 500 to 750 million, according to sociologist, Phil Zuckerman. Other sociologists, Ariela Keysar and Juhem Navarro-Rivera say there are about 500 million definite atheists and agnostics, with China topping the league at 200 million non-believers. Non-belief appears to be on an upward trend. But maybe there will be a religious resurgence, because of the worry and fear generated by world-wide pandemics, like the COVID 19 virus, and through the growing impact of Climate Change. In times of major crisis, people desire to grasp at something they see as re-assuring and hopeful. Recovery of religious belief though, is not certain.

This begs the question, what else to follow/practice, if not religion?

Humanism

Humanists are focused on humans, not Gods of any sort. Some ancient Greek philosophers could be considered of a humanist inclination, particularly Socrates, with his denial of Gods and focus of high ethical standards. Basically, humanists want to help people and communities so that there is movement towards a better, more just society. Many Humanists might attach other labels to themselves. They could also be atheists, agnostics or secularists. What they do not have is a religious affiliation. Humanism is worldwide. It’s prevalent in at least 40 countries. In the United Kingdom it is major: An Ipsos MORI poll, published in January 2007 for Humanists UK indicated that 36% of people - equivalent to around 17 million adults - 1 in 5 people - are in fact humanist in their basic outlook; despite they may not formally identify themselves as humanists. The UK has 90,000 formally signed up, with dues paid.

There are some well-known people running this body as at 2019: Chair of the Board, Tamar Ghosh (also Chief Exec. Of the Royal Society of Tropical Medicine and Hygiene), President, Professor Alice Roberts (evolutionary biologist, author and TV broadcaster); Vice-President, Professor Jim Al-Khalili (theoretical physicist, author, radio and TV broadcaster); Vice-President, Professor A C Grayling (philosopher and writer, regular on radio and TV), and Vice-President Shappi Khorsandi (stand-up comic, performing in theatre, appearing on radio and TV, author).

A great early Humanist was Margaret Knight. While in her third year at Girton College Cambridge Margaret said that she found the “moral courage”, finally to at last give up the religious beliefs she had been uncomfortable with for a long time. She explained this defining moment in her book: “Morals Without Religion (1955)”, writing “…a fresh, cleansing wind swept through the stuffy room that contained the relics of my religious beliefs. I let them go with a profound sense of relief, and ever since I have lived happily without them.”

On the radio, BBC Home Service, 1955, Margaret argued intelligently that children’s moral education should be uncoupled from religious education saying: “I am not out to destroy the Christian convictions of people in whom they are deeply implanted and to whom they mean a great deal. And I am sure that nothing I say here will have the slightest effect on believers of this type. But what I do want to argue is that, in a climate of thought that is increasingly unfavourable to these beliefs, it is a mistake to try to impose them on children, and to make them the basis of moral training. The moral education of children is much too important a matter to be built on such foundations.” This caused a bit of a controversy with conservative Brits at the time.

Buddhism

The several varieties of Buddhism, have the aim of helping people reach nirvana (see Chapter 23, 8 Philosophy). Zen Buddhism is unusual. It requires people to focus inside themselves, looking for inner truth, and eventually becoming Buddha. Zen claims we all are Buddha.

Modern Spiritualism

Varieties of spiritualism have been common throughout the world and throughout human history, most usually in the form of shamanism and animism. These two are both associated with nature and spirits and are more fully described in Chapter 5. Modern Spiritualism is largely of an American location - the National Spiritual Association of Churches (NSAC), USA. Although it’s said to be ‘modern’ one of its doctrines is Confucius ‘golden rule’. Confucius was a Chinese philosopher who expounded his wisdom 2,500 years ago. The Golden Rule says ‘treat others as you would yourself.’

The NSAC has 8 other principles including reference to prophecy and healing through use of mediums. These are people who claim to have physic abilities. Other, non-proven, but claimed aspects of modern spiritualism include clairvoyance, levitation, ectoplasm, the gift of tongues and communication with the spirits of the dead. Mediums, usually women, are the conduits for these attempted spiritual events.

Emanuel Swedenborg was a Swede who was both a theologian, with religious belief, and a medium. He is seen as the father of Modern Spiritualism.

One medium, the French woman Eva Carrière (1886 to 1943), was famous in her time. She managed to pull the wool over the eyes of Arthur Conan Doyle but not Harry Houdini, what would Sherlock Holmes make of his originator being hoodwinked? Eva’s séances were highly erotic. She often appeared nude, perhaps to put off balance her male séancers, and distract them so they would not notice they were being duped.

Astrology

p.236

Loosely linked to Spiritualism is astrology. Spiritualism is, not surprisingly, associated with 'Spirits". These are usually of a human nature. A dead human’s spirit is thought to be contactable. Astrology is also connected to spirits, but these are of the sky. They are related to one or other of the moon, sun, stars and constellations. Astrology is still around today but its origin goes back a very long way in human history. The earliest evidence is found in Paleolithic times, 25,000 years ago. Scratchings on cave walls and bones, dated to this time, show the influence of the Moon on tides and tidal rivers. Knowledge of constellations slowly developed in the Neolithic, agricultural, period. After the 25 millennia old scratchings, Mesopotamia, home of the first civilisations, kicked in. The oldest known astrological references here occurred in the reign of Sargon of Akkad, 2334 to 2279 BCE. Slightly later, the Sumerian ruler, Gudea, (2144 to 2124 BCE) apparently was told by the Gods in a dream to look out for particular constellations. These would be to show the best time to construct a temple. In Babylon, a later major power in Mesopotamia, the first system of astrology was created in 1800 BCE. It was used to predict the weather and for political purposes. Astrology continued to be of importance in ancient Egypt, Greece, Rome. Islamic scholars picked up the astrological baton, although some Islamic theologians, such as Avicenna argued against the use of astrology, claiming it was purely conjectural not empirical in nature.

Why astrology?

The mysterious movement of stars and their grouping into identifiable clusters, called constellations was thought to have some significance. The ancients, and many that followed them, thought this must be some sort of omen. The stars could predict the future, say what path should be taken, with the help of expert interpretation by psychics, mystics and anyone else who can tell a good story.

Wicca

This is a modern pagan religion, tracing some of its nature focused, partly shamanistic practices, to ancient times. The followers have no temples, or specific buildings in which they worship. They gather in groups, called covens of 10 to 15 members. They engage in witchcraft, various rituals and practice magic, using their “Book of Shadows”. Two of their main deities are a female Goddess and the male, horned God. A key Wiccan sect is known as Wiccan Rede. Its ethical code says: “If it harms none, do what you will.” Wiccans are mainly to be found in Great Britain and the USA, but also in continental Europe. One estimate put their number at 5 million, worldwide.

What’s Good about the good

As seen earlier, many non-religious people could be described as Humanists, that is, those who have a care for other people and do good works. This is analogous to some religious people, of the non-fundamentalist sort. Many religious people, of every religion, are not rigid, fundamentalists. In fact, some are at the opposite end of the religious spectrum holding on to a gentler belief concept. Of course, there are many levels of religious zealotry between those two poles. But let’s focus on just the lower end of these two extremes. These are closer to the views and actions of the best of non-religious people.

Very anti-fundamentalist religious persons are characterised by their respect and toleration of others outside their faith. They help others, no matter what alternate beliefs they may hold, and do much good for their fellow man. Typically, they obey the rules and laws of their society, despite this may be of a secular nature. They have tolerance and engage amicably with those of a different religious persuasion. Even atheists and agnostics are accepted with equanimity. In this way these “goodies” contribute to, and help build a just and thriving society. This is in contrast with the negative and sometimes destructive impact of their more ardent, narrow minded and fundamentalist brethren. These latter tend to believe there is only one way, their way. They will seek to impose their ways and may even use violence to achieve their goal. The goodies are able to live their loving religious good are people with a Tolerant, Democratic Faith. They could be initialised as the TDF crowd.

TOLERANCE CONDEMNS PERSECUTION OF OTHERS.

TDFs can be found in followers of every faith and of no faith at all. Their good deeds can be both composite and individual. Many philosophers have outlined the essentials of such behavior, generally saying we have empathy towards others and treat them fairly.

Of course, there is the alternative, an encouragement to selfishness by philosophers such as Ayn Rand and Bernard Mandeville, but the majority of philosophers advise, in essence, look out for others, ‘don’t be selfish’.

These ‘goody’ behaviors can be found in the heavily stripped-down, core instruction, of almost every religion. The TDF crowd demonstrates them, and they are at the heart of non-religious Humanism.

Several psychological studies show that people are generally inclined to be kind and helpful to others. Neuroscience research also backs this up (Christov-Moore & Iacoboni, 2015). The tendencies are not absolute imperatives. Studies also show that urgent self-interest can overwhelm the kindness tendency. These tendencies, of all people of all and no faiths, are the building blocks of the type of ethical behaviour most approve of. They can be encouraged to establish firmer moral attitudes that do not require any religion as a guide, and be prepared to ask difficult questions about any claim of certainty over required forms of behaviour.

All need to have some education to lead them along this path. In their formative years children ought to be taught to have more open minds in schools free of association with a particular religion.

Let’s have more TDF’s, either of the religious or humanist variety. Their aims and work are supportive of their fellow man and are ethically centered.
