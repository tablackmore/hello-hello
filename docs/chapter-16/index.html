Chapter 16

Abraham, Judaism and beyond

Pages 128–138

Ancient civilisations have told us how religion was central to human aspiration, and consolation. It was a glue that helped hold disparate people together. Ergo, it was manipulated to be a useful prop for governance.

We are now leaving behind the ancient civilisations. These had, mostly inadvertently, laid the groundwork for a sea change in religious belief that would eventually spread across a large part of the globe. The religions of major civilisations were characterised by a complex and huge range of Gods. Many of these Gods appeared to be engaged in nasty, reprehensible practices, such as murder and rape. They were not good role models and there were so many Gods it must have been difficult and wearisome to pay heed to them all. The stories about them may have become increasingly doubted, especially through the attack of the growing and newish subject of metaphysical philosophy. All this helped pave the way to the emergence of the monotheistic Abrahamic religions, Judaism, Islam partly, but with Christianity receiving the largest boost via Byzantium. The Abrahamic seed eventually grew into a very large tree. In 2012 it was estimated that 55% of the world population was either Christian or Muslim, with an additional 0.2% Jewish.

The principle religious variation these three brought was monotheism. The single God they espouse is a huge rejection of the polytheism common to ancient religions, Akenhaten excepted. In Christian denominations the one God is also the three, a sort of quantum, deity-particle, tripality conundrum.

The three are called Abrahamic religions because, despite some variations in belief, all of them can be traced back to prophet Abraham. God, named Yahweh, is said to have first revealed himself to Abraham.

p.129

All three religions believe this God is everlasting, omnipotent and omniscient. In addition, the Abrahamics think God is outside of space and time, that is He is transcendent. This does not stop Him from acting as a personal God, one who listens to the prayers of believers.

Abraham and the Biblical Account

What we know of Abraham stems mainly from the Bible, as recorded by one of the Biblical authors in Genesis. He is also mentioned in the Qur’an.

Abraham was sometimes known as Abram or Avram and for Muslims he was Ibrahim. This first prophet was born in Ur, Mesopotamia. It’s not clear when he lived, suggestions range from about 500 to 2,000 BCE.

It’s claimed he lived for an astonishing 175 years. The religions around in Abraham’s time had many Gods. God, the singular one, made it known to Abraham that He was the only one and that Abraham should spread this news. God encouraged him to leave Ur for the land of Canaan (somewhere between Syria and Egypt) to found a new nation, the Jewish state. Abraham had faith in God and made the move at the ripe old age of 75, along with his entire family. Abraham eventually trusted God sufficiently to take steps, as ordered, to kill his son Isaac. Fortunately for Abraham and even more fortunately for the son, God relented at the last moment and accepted a ram as an alternate sacrifice. He had only been testing Abraham’s commitment to Him.

Both Jews and Islamic Arabs consider Abraham, or Ibrahim, to be the father of their people, through his two sons Isaac and Ishmael. For Muslims, Muhammad was descended from him. Christians see Abraham as an important prophet, leading the way to Jesus. Despite their fundamental core sameness, Judaism, Christianity and Islam have engaged in much bitter and deadly strife, first between Christianity and Islam, from the advent of the latter in the 7th century. Later there was considerable Christian persecution of Jews.

Some of this is to do with different beliefs in the role of Jesus. In Islam, Muslims see Jesus as a prophet. They do not accept that he was a God, as do Christians. Jews also view Jesus as a prophet rather than the Christian God. For them the messiah or savior has not yet arrived. But it has not been 2,000 or so years of constant warfare. There have been significant periods of relative calmness and tolerance between the trio of Abrahamic religions.

The three religions appear to suggest two types of God. A personal God, whom people pray to and maybe receive something in return. This is also the God whose ethical instructions must be followed, in order to get into Heaven, or equivalent. The other type of God is concerned with much bigger, non-ethical issues - the order and structure of the universe. The former is not much associated with science but the latter is. Both are the province of philosophy.

The Three Abrahamic Religions

p.130

Now to look at the three Abrahamics individually.

Visual Summary

The Star of David - Judaism

Oldest and by far the smallest in number of followers. It’s about 3,500 years old and started up with a claimed covenant between God and prophet Abraham. Moses played a big role, receiving the 10 commandments.

The Cross - Christianity

Second up and biggest of the Abrahamics. Started in the 1st century CE and grew in prominence thanks to successive Roman Emperor’s. Under Theodosius I it became the state religion of Rome.

The Star and Crescent - Islam

Last to kick off founded in the 7th century by Muhammad. Now the second largest of the Abrahamic religions. But, like the other two had in Abraham a common first prophet.

Judaism

Historian Simon Sharma says there are two special things about the Jews: They have endured for over 3,000 years and secondly, they have a dramatic tale to tell. It is unusual in human terms for one clearly identifiable race and religious group of people to have been around so long, although Chinese and Greeks, at least, could make a similar claim. These two could also vie with Jews in terms of vivid history. The Jewish drama though has added elements. It is uniquely linked to a specific religion - Judaism. This, combined with centuries long persecution, sometimes morphing into deadly discrimination, makes the Jews stand out from other peoples. The Jewish story is long, and full of adjectives: exciting; mystifying; unsettling; horrifying; warring; diasporic; unjust and noble. There is lots to say, too much for this book to cover in depth.

p.131

Judaism, at maybe 3,500 years old, is the oldest of the three Abrahamic religions, but this timescale depends on the exact lifetime of Abraham. It is by far the smallest in size of the Abrahamics. There were an estimated 14.6 million Jews worldwide in 2020. This is only 0.5% of the other two - Christianity and Islam. But Judaism in modern times punches far above its weight, largely due to its main base, Israel, having a very big, very powerful friend, the USA.

The Chosen People

Jews think, following on from what Abraham was told by the one God, that they are the chosen people. They believe that God wanted Jews to lead the way in setting an example to the world of good, holy behaviour, so that others would copy. If they did this, on his side of the bargain God would look after the Jewish people.

Jewish belief, like many religious areas is divided into slightly different groupings but with many core similarities. The one God is present in an everlasting covenant clear in the Jewish Torah (Bible), Genesis 12-17.

Moses, who arrived on the scene several hundred years after Abraham, was the conduit through which the final, detailed covenant was set out. He led the Jews, who became the Israelites, out of slavery in Egypt. God gave a lot of help. He aided their escape by tormenting Egyptians with a series of unpleasant events, then smoothed their path by helpfully parting the Red Sea.

On Mount Sinai Moses received the Torah from God. This was authoritative Godly guidance in the form of a list of precise instructions. The Ten Commandments were the most memorable element. These moral directives listed 8 things people should not do and 2 that they should. The ten were not all the instructions though, just the main, overarching ones. In total the detailed instructions exceeded 600. These were subsequently kept safe and secure in a gold-covered wooden chest. This chest, the Ark of the Covenant, became the permanent repository of the 10 Commandments.

If you find it, you will become world-renowned, but only if the Ten Commandment tablets are still inside. Be careful not to break commandment 8, Thou Shalt Not Steal, (sometimes numbered 7) when obtaining the Ark.

The Moses Theory

p.132

In another story concerning Moses an interesting theory is proposed by the originator of psychoanalysis, Sigmund Freud. He thought, using Ernst Sellin’s 1922 work, that Moses was murdered by the Israelites. Then full of remorse, they became extra zealous in promoting the commandments, and so firmly establishing the base of Judaism. For Sigmund, this fitted neatly with his belief that major early experiences acted as an influence throughout later life. God’s commandments and Moses role would help cement Jewish identity forever after, through thick and thin. However, there is no archaeological or other hard evidence for Moses murder, or indeed much else in the Moses story, or even of Moses himself.

Maimonides, the super-clever Jewish philosopher cum theologian of 800 years ago came up with thirteen articles of faith. The first few of these describe God, the almighty creator. He then goes on to claim that Moses was the father of all prophets. For Maimonides, Moses received the Torah from God the creator.

Francesca Stavrakopoulou, Professor of Hebrew Bible & Ancient Religion at Exeter University, says Moses did not exist. No one seems to have come forward to offer clear evidence that would refute Francesca’s assertion. If true, her view puts a big coach and four heavy horses so thoroughly through the middle of Judaism, as to leave nothing but tiny fragments of a religion behind. The lack of a Moses also severely impacts on the two other Abrahamic religions.

However, there are still millions of people who still believe in Judaism. They hold certain things to be core truths.

Basic Beliefs of Judaism

Strictly Jewish religion is not based on beliefs but on their laws, but here’s what most Jews believe:

p.133
  • There is one God - he exists forever and he created the universe, Deuteronomy 6:4 “Hear Israel, the Lord is our God, the Lord is One”
  • God is all-knowing, all-powerful, just and merciful and is the source of morality and goodness
  • He is interested in every person, knows what they are up to and intervenes on occasion and is the decider of mortality
  • All people are made in the image of God, because of this they must be treated with kindness and respect
  • God gave people free will
  • A Messiah, a saviour, will eventually come to unite the world in peace, but he has not yet arrived (Jews, unlike Christians, do not see Jesus as God or as the saviour. For them he is another prophet)

Jewish Ethics

These are lengthy and detailed with many ancient Rabbinic sages, slightly more modern ones and even more modern ones giving their Talmudic interpretation of the holy moral guidance. In essence the important bits can be said to be these:

  • be kind
  • treat your neighbour as you wish to be treated
  • cultivate friendship
  • seek peace
  • go to war only in self-defense

A Jewish sage of about 250 CE, Rabbi Simlai, simplified all beliefs and Godly instructions, or so he thought, advising “Six hundred and thirteen commandments were given to Moses; then David came and reduced them to eleven in Psalm 15.; Isaiah (33:15), reduced them to six; Micah (6:8), to three: ‘To act justly and to love mercy and to walk humbly with your God’; Isaiah again (56:1), now down to two: ‘Maintain justice, and do what is right’; and Habakkuk (2:4), to one: ‘The righteous person lives by his faithfulness’.”

All this advice is based on the Talmud and Moses instructions from Yahweh. But if Moses is just a story…?

Afterlife

Curiously, Judaism seemingly does not have a clear and consistent belief about an afterlife. Jewish scriptures talk of a place called Sheol where all people go after they died. It is a dark and gloomy place, not somewhere you would choose to be. You might be a bit miffed to find yourself there if you had spent a goodly life, obeying all the scripturally rules. Perhaps that’s why later teachings sought to improve the situation by introducing the existence of a heaven and a hell. Daniel 12:2 gave some assistance here:

“Many of those who sleep in the dusty earth will awaken: these for everlasting life and these for shame, for everlasting abhorrence.”

Again though, it’s not completely certain what this refers to, but surmise that it obliquely refers to heaven and hell seems reasonable. In Judaism the former is called Gan Eden with Gehenna being hell, both a place and something to be suffering. One strand of Jewish belief is that God will sort people, both living and dead, into one or other location at the time of the Last Judgment. In addition, people’s body and soul will be re-united at this time. Is there a theological explanation as to how a re-formed physical body can then get into spiritual heaven or ghostly hell? While awaiting the Last Judgement Sheol may still be used as a temporary parking place by departees.

The Soul and Sleep

p.134

In Judaism, there is a soul. Some Rabbis teach that when people are asleep their soul visits heaven, for refreshment and re-invigoration. Does this explain why some Jews don’t eat breakfast and are raring to go early in the morning?

Another claimed Kabballic (mystical tradition) feature of sleep is that it is 1/60th of death. Such amazing precision. How is death measured in sixtieths? Is a cat-nap then an ninth of a sixtieth?

The Names: Jews, Hebrews, and Israelis

There are three names for the peoples sometimes also known as the chosen ones: Jews, Hebrews and Israelis. Abraham is behind all of these. In the Jewish holy book, the Torah, Abraham is referred to as a Hebrew, so his descendants were said to speak Hebrew. Abraham’s grandson, Jacob had a second name Israel, hence its association with the Israelite homeland and later the land of Israel where Israelis reside. One of Jacob’s sons was Judah, who became head of the tribe known as Jews. He also gave his name to the religious creed, Judaism.

Holy Books

Judaism is guided by the three holy books of the Hebrew Bible.

  • First there is the Torah, this covers the detailed law which Jews must obey. The Torah, sometimes referred to by the Greek term Pentateuch, covers the first five biblical books. These are the Books of Moses. The five are well known names to biblical readers: Genesis, Exodus, Leviticus, Numbers and Deuteronomy.
  • Second is the Nevi’im which mentions the prophets
  • Thirdly Ketuvim which refers to writings, these include Psalms, Book of Job, Proverbs and Book of Daniel
  • The three combined are referred to by the acronym Tanakh. There are 24 books of the Tanakh.
p.135

So, so far, we have the Jewish holy books called Torah, Pentateuch, Nevi’im, Ketuvim and Tanakh. These form various agreed readings of the Torah. This is the all-inclusive written, definitive law - almost - but Judaism is not finished with the guidance to its followers. There is yet another step. The Talmud becomes the basis for Halakhah (more law). This is composed of the Mishnah (original written version of the oral law) and the Gemara (a record of elucidations by learned rabbis). Of course, there is even more than all this, but enough is enough. Christians and Muslims have it a lot easier with just the Bible or Qur’an, although they too add on a bit of expert guidance.

Multiple Names for the Same Book

The multitudinous references to the same book, if we start with the Torah, could be useful for a pub quiz question: “What are the other four ways in which the first book of the Torah can be referred to?”

Answer: 1. First of the 24 books of the Tanakh; 2. First Book of the Pentateuch; 3. First of the 5 Books of Moses; 4. Genesis.

In fact, moving outside Judaism, there is a fifth way, i.e. the Torah is also synonymous with the first book of the Bible. Thus, making a total of 6 descriptions of the same thing.

Naming of the Jewish Monotheistic God

The score of 6 for names of the Jewish holy book is far outstripped by the number of names in Judaism for God. We start with the voweless YHWH, referred to as Yahweh and Jehovah in English. The others are:

  • Elohim - God
  • Eloah - God
  • Elah - an Aramaic word for God
  • El - the Mighty One
  • El Shaddai - the Almighty
  • El Roi - God of Seeing
  • Adonai - Lord (Adonai was sometimes in the past replaced with Adoshem)
  • Elyon - the Highest
  • Avinu - Our Father
  • Tzevaot - God of Hosts
  • Ehyeh asher eyah - I am what I am
  • Hashem Kel Olam - The Eternal One

That’s a total of 14 Jewish names of God, if the name God is excluded.

In the very early days of the Jewish history, when the one God was vying with many others for attention, the name Baal was treated as synonymous with Adonai. But Baal was originally a Canaanite storm God named Ba’al Haddu. This was something of an embarrassment so Baal was gradually removed from the naming of the Jewish God. If all of these are counted they total 17 names for God.

Rituals

As with other religions Judaism has its fair share of rituals. The most common is the weekly holy day, the Sabbath, which is on a Saturday.

Rosh Hashanah is the Jewish new year. Jews believe that this is when God decides what will happen in the year ahead and acts in judgement on the previous annual failings and good deeds of Jewish people (the 6.9 billion other people in the world don’t come into it).

Yom Kippur, known as The Day of Atonement. This is when God makes his final, final decision about who will live, die or prosper in the coming year. Very important for Jews to attend the synagogue for this one.

p.136

Hanukkah, this is the Jewish Festival of Lights. It reminds Jews of their struggles for religious freedom and is symbolized by the lighting of the candelabra Menorah which can have 7 or 9 candles.

There are other rituals and ceremonies, perhaps one that is well known outside of Judaism is the Bar Mitzvah. This is a coming of age ceremony for boys. Girls do not have the same ceremony. Theirs is a Bat Mitzvah. Boys have one more ceremony to look forward to, the Brit Milah, male circumcision.

What’s Kosher?

Judaism specifies a dietary requirement under the heading kosher food and kosher food preparation. According to the Torah Jews must not eat camels, rock badgers, hares or pigs. That is because those off the menu include all animals with cloven hoofs or those who chomp their cud. When it comes to sea food, lobsters, shrimp and crabs are definitely off the plate but fish generally are OK to eat. Those animals that are termed kosher must have all blood removed and be slaughtered in a particular way. This “all” must be extremely difficult to achieve with absolute surety.

There is no specific Torah food guidance on present day dietary issues like sugar, salt and fat. Cholesterol does not get a mention.

Where Are They?

Jews are now mostly located in Israel. In modern times the state of Israel was founded in 1948. This followed, firstly from the defeat of the Ottoman Empire in WW1. Britain then took control of Palestine, the land eventually to become Israel, and declared in 1917 an intention to set up a: “national home for the Jewish people”. During the time of the Ottoman Empire there was only a small Jewish population in Palestine. The rest were Muslim Arabs. Over the preceding 2,000 years the land was at first primarily Jewish then, after Jesus and the Roman Empire, Christians were a force. Then came the Muslim conquests of the 7th century, Islam and Arabs predominated. The Crusades of the 12th and 13th centuries had at various times re-introduced Christianity in Jerusalem and other near-by territory. After these Islam was again firmly to the fore, but with small Jewish settlements. This is the way it had stayed, with the Jewish population numbered at only a few tens of thousands, illegally after 1945, leading up to the mid 1950’s. By 1958 the Jewish population had increased from 800,000 to 2 million. All this caused much displacement and loss of homes of Muslim Palestinian Arabs, resulting in friction between Israel and most of its Arab, Muslim, neighbours.

Israel’s Position

Israel survives although surrounded by generally hostile Arab neighbours. This is because support of its mighty friend, the USA. Their friendship has enabled Israel to acquire powerful modern ordnance, which includes atomic weapons. A sign of Israel’s miraculous strength is its possible remodeling of geography. In 2018 Israel won the Eurovision Song Contest and hosted three stages of the Tour of Italy (Giro) cycling race. Most people would think Israel is not in Europe. It’s located in the Middle East, several hundred kilometers from the main land of Europe and from Italy.

Historical Persecution

Jews have suffered repeatedly from pogroms; off and on over many hundreds of years. They dispersed from the original Israel and often successfully settled in a number of countries. Their financial and business acumen has often been recognized, but this has sometimes resulted in persecution by the resident population, because of envy and resentment. Sometimes, in countries with a predominant Christian religion, they were viewed as fair game for forms of oppression, being seen as the people who allowed Jesus to be killed. Forms of anti-Semitism have been in evidence for more than 2,000 years: in 139 BCE, Jews were expelled from Rome; but in 59 BCE Jews were obviously still in Rome because Cicero condemned them as being too influential. The mistreatment of diasporic Jews reached a peak during World War II. This is when the genocide known as the holocaust took place. In the six years of the 1939-1945 war it’s estimated that the Nazis’ killed 6 million Jews in Europe.

All this violent discrimination at many times and in many places has probably helped strengthen the Judaism religion, because it’s uniquely identifiable and so has unifying properties for Jews.

This is a two-way street effect. Their faith helps give them a shared identity; Judaism cements the Jewish character. This means that even though Jews have not had a country of their own for many hundreds of years they have maintained their own idea of a common, national identity. Judaism is common but not universal to all Jews. There are a number of Jews who have no religious affiliation and who do not speak Hebrew, but they still see themselves as Jews.

Judaism was the first of the Abrahamic religions. We now move on to the second one, Christianity.
