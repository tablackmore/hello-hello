Chapter 4

Why to What

Pages 37–39

We are still concentrating on the monotheistic, Abrahamic God, mainly because He has the most supporters, not only nowadays but also much of the historic past. HE’s the one worshipped currently, and for the past two thousand years, by more religious people on Earth than any other God(s). Billions have knelt or bowed before him.

What are the Godly Abilities and Attributes?

The “why” of God line of reasoning points to the litany of needs Modern Humans, from ancient times, had for a God, these were so strong it caused them to search for and eventually invent/discover the one God. From this we can infer God’s attributes and capabilities to warrant all the roles vested in Him through the “why” quizology. Theologians and religious authorities are renowned for their desire to help us with this understanding of God. Through them we know that these Godly abilities and attributes are broad and deep - right up to kick starting the universe and living forever. HE has also to be a kind, loving and merciful entity. Most importantly, HE must provide His faithful with an eternally blissful after-life, called Heaven by Christians. These are desired and persuasive attributes - the carrot referred to in earlier chapters.

In addition, for many people, He must be tagged as a being to be feared; a judgmental God capable of having people, who do not toe the Godly line, condemned to eternal damnation - the stick. In fact, He has a multitude of hoops to jump through. To achieve all that’s wanted of Him God has to be a being with the most extreme capabilities. HE must have a full set of “alls”:

  • all knowing;
  • all powerful;
  • all where;
  • always.

Uniformly overall in fact - everything covered top to bottom. These core abilities are what God has to have to enable Him to guide, explain, understand and deliver. The needs of the faithful determine the “what” of God.

It’s worth looking at what a great scientist said about the human need for God. Richard Feynman (Nobel prize winning American physicist and communicator of science) said: "God was invented to explain mystery. God is always invented to explain those things that you do not understand.

p.38

Now, when you finally discover how something works, you get some laws which you’re taking away from God; you don’t need Him anymore. But you need him for the other mysteries. So therefore, you leave Him to create the universe because we haven’t figured that out yet; you need him for understanding those things which you don’t believe the laws will explain, such as consciousness, or why you only live to a certain length of time - life and death - stuff like that. God is always associated with those things that you do not understand. Therefore, I don’t think that the laws can be considered to be like God because they have been figured out."

Two hundred years before Richard Feynman a French/German philosopher, Baron d’Holbach, said something similar, but in a more brutally, blunt way:

“If we look back at the beginning we shall find that ignorance and fear created the Gods; that fancy, enthusiasm, or deceit adorned or disfigured them; that weakness worships them; that credulity preserves them; and that custom, respect and tyranny support them, in order to make the blindness of man serve their own interest. If the ignorance of nature gave birth to Gods, the knowledge of nature is calculated to destroy them. All religions are ancient monuments to superstitions, ignorance, ferocity; and modern religions are only ancient follies rejuvenated.” A bit strong that Paul-Henri.

The Baron published his ideas anonymously, for fear of imprisonment or worse. In his time, the Catholic church had a lot of clout.

On the other hand, we have Albert Einstein, one of the greatest scientists of all time saying this: “I am not an atheist and I don’t think I can call myself a pantheist [the belief that God and the material world are one and the same and that God is present in everything]. We are in the position of a little child entering a huge library filled with books in many languages. The child knows someone must have written those books. It does not know how. He does not understand the languages in which they are written. The child dimly suspects a mysterious order in the arrangement of the books but doesn’t know what it is. That, it seems to me, is the attitude of even the most intelligent human being towards God. We see the universe marvelously arranged and obeying certain laws but only dimly understand these laws. Our limited minds cannot grasp the mysterious force that moves the constellations.”

p.39

Einstein held a type of religious attitude based on admiration and wonder of our world and the wider universe.

This was well short of belief in a personal God. He expressed it like this: “A knowledge of the existence of something we cannot penetrate, of the manifestations of the profoundest reason and the most radiant beauty - it is this knowledge and this emotion that constitute the truly religious attitude; in this sense, and in this alone, I am a deeply religious man.” and, “I do not believe in a personal God and I have never denied this but have expressed it clearly. If something is in me which can be called religious then it is the unbounded admiration for the structure of the world so far as our science can reveal it.” (Albert Einstein, 1954)

Einstein’s earlier point, that even the greatest human minds cannot conceive of what a God, or “mysterious force” is about, is an intriguing one. The force or God is way beyond our understanding, so argues Einstein. This could be said of Einstein too for most of us. Not many can do the maths behind the famous E=MC². God and His purpose will always remain something of a mystery, according to Einstein. This is a FIFU contention, but FIFU has flaws, as we have seen. Einstein also expanded his Anthropic argument, pointing, perhaps, to a deist view, by saying: “I see a pattern, but my imagination cannot picture the maker of that pattern. I see a clock, but I cannot envision the clockmaker.”

The universal cosmic order that was at the heart of Einstein’s belief was mostly, after his death, thrown into disorder by the proven randomness and incongruity found within the atom. This was discovered through developments in quantum mechanics, especially by the work of Pauli, Heisenberg and others. Would Einstein say the same things about God and atheism today? Maybe not, but we cannot know.

So, in determining the “what” of God. Some religious people point to a list of supreme abilities and attributes. For others, as suggested by Richard Feynman and Baron D’Holbach, God is given the ability to fill in the gaps not yet answered by science, but the gaps are progressively narrowing. No matter how small the gaps, Einstein thinks we are not capable of knowing the answer to the “what” question.
