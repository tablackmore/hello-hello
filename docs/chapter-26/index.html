Chapter 26

Space and Travel in Space

Pages 455–458

I cannot tell you much about the actual workings of matter and energy except perhaps that it’s all a lot simpler to produce than you might think. But you may be glad to know that matter is probably eternal (and perhaps infinitely re-creatable). Our ability to predict the future behavior of the Universe down to the finest detail is extraordinarily precise (but is also limited by the Uncertainty Principle). And, of course, we can’t tell you whether the Universe is expanding or not. The last time someone asked me, I didn’t know and I probably still don’t know.

What we can tell you is that your existence is absolutely dependent on the fact that you live in a tiny-weeny world of your own creation. You move about in this tiny-weeny world in a very small little bag called a ‘body’. It is approximately 65% water and, at most, a meter wide. Nevertheless, you are a thing of stunning complexity: just think about how your body works. If you want to scratch your nose, or run to the corner shop, or lie in bed, your ability to carry out these astoundingly complex activities is all controlled from a single biological processing unit (your brain), which is incredibly sophisticated and which we still don’t fully understand.

Now, standing on the surface of the Earth, which is rotating at approximately 1000 mph at the Equator and zooming around the Sun at approximately 67,000 mph, you are entirely and blissfully unaware of any of this movement. The reason why is because of inertia (which you’ll recall from Chapter 1 describes things that keep on doing what they are doing). Essentially, when you are moving with the planet, it’s like you and the planet are ‘stuck together’ and so everything just seems normal. But as we’ve discussed many times, it’s all relative (and that’s not just the family that come over at Christmas, ho ho). So, moving past each other at such huge speeds, your ability to perceive the movement is entirely determined by your frame of reference which, in this case, is ‘moving along with the Earth’.

If you were standing on the Moon looking at the Earth, you would see it moving. But if you are on the Earth itself, you just see day and night, and perhaps the occasional slight wobbling of a lamppost (if it’s a windy day and you have lamposts where you live). But the overwhelming sense you have on the Earth is one of stillness, which is why the idea that the Earth was moving seemed completely absurd for most of human history.

Tripping past the Universe at Speed

Now, here’s a question. If you’re in a rocket ship and you’re traveling through space - past planets, past stars, past solar systems and galaxies - how fast would you be going? Well, the answer is that you couldn’t be going fast at all because, remember, speed is relative. Fast compared to what?

If you are alone in the middle of empty space in your little rocket ship, you can’t tell whether you’re moving or not. Even if you are zooming along at some colossal speed relative to something far away, unless you can see that thing and measure how quickly it’s getting closer to you (or further away), you have absolutely no way of knowing whether you’re moving at all. In fact, from your perspective, it is equally valid to say that you are stationary and the rest of the Universe is whizzing past you.

Now I know what you’re thinking. This is completely preposterous. Just fire the rocket engines and see if you are moving. Of course, you could do this, but that doesn’t tell you how fast you were moving before you fired the engines. It just tells you that you accelerated and thus changed your velocity. Before that, you might have been moving at 1000 mph or 1 million mph relative to your home planet, but unless you could see your home planet and knew where it was and could measure the change, you would have no idea.

This is a very fundamental aspect of the Universe which Einstein formulated as his Theory of Special Relativity. It states (amongst other things) that there is no such thing as absolute motion. All motion is relative to something else. In other words, there’s no ‘universal reference frame’ sitting in the middle of space saying “everything is either moving or not moving relative to me.” Everything in the Universe is moving relative to everything else.

I hear you say “hang on! I’m just sitting here, I haven’t moved all night, apart from turning over now and again, and perhaps going to the loo, perhaps. Quite a moving story don’t you think?”

Yes, I think that is a moving story. But remember, you are on the Earth, and the Earth is zooming around the Sun at 67,000 mph. You are also rotating with the Earth at around 1000 mph (if you’re near the Equator). The Solar System itself is moving around the center of the Milky Way galaxy at around 514,000 mph. And the Milky Way galaxy itself is moving through space at around 1.3 million mph relative to the Cosmic Microwave Background radiation. So, whilst you were lying in bed, you were in fact hurtling through the cosmos at phenomenal speed. You just didn’t notice because everything around you was moving with you.

Now, here’s where things get really interesting. We’ve established that there’s no such thing as absolute motion - everything is relative. But there is one thing in the Universe that isn’t relative, and that’s the speed of light. The speed of light is the same for all observers, regardless of how fast they’re moving relative to each other or relative to the light source. This is another key part of Einstein’s Theory of Special Relativity and is, frankly, completely bonkers when you first hear about it.

What this means is that if you’re on a train moving at 100 mph and you throw a ball forward at 50 mph, then relative to someone standing on the platform, the ball is moving at 150 mph (100 + 50). That makes sense. But if you’re on a train moving at half the speed of light and you shine a torch forward, the light doesn’t travel at 1.5 times the speed of light relative to someone on the platform. It still travels at exactly the speed of light. The same speed. Always. No matter what.

This leads to some truly bizarre consequences. Time dilation, for example, where time actually runs slower for objects moving at high speeds. Length contraction, where objects actually get shorter in the direction of motion when moving at high speeds. And the famous equation E=mc², which shows that mass and energy are equivalent and interchangeable.

Planet Earth

So, let’s get back to something a bit more tangible and talk about our home: Planet Earth. The Earth is, quite frankly, an extraordinary place and one that we should cherish and look after (which, let’s be honest, we’re not doing a particularly fantastic job of at the moment, but that’s another story).

The Earth is approximately 4.5 billion years old (give or take a few million years - we’re not that precise about these things). It formed from the same cloud of gas and dust that formed the Sun and the rest of our Solar System. Over billions of years, this ball of molten rock gradually cooled and formed a solid crust. Water collected on the surface (probably delivered by comets and asteroids), an atmosphere developed, and eventually life appeared.

p.457

The Earth is not a perfect sphere - it’s slightly squashed at the poles and bulges a bit at the equator due to its rotation. The diameter at the equator is about 12,756 km (7,926 miles), whilst the diameter from pole to pole is about 12,714 km (7,900 miles). So it’s about 42 km (26 miles) wider than it is tall. Not a huge difference, but worth noting if you’re planning to wrap it up as a present.

The Earth consists of several layers. At the center is the core, which is made up of two parts: a solid inner core (made primarily of iron and nickel) with a radius of about 1,220 km (760 miles), and a liquid outer core (also iron and nickel) about 2,300 km (1,400 miles) thick. The temperature at the center of the core is estimated to be around 5,700°C (10,300°F), which is roughly the same temperature as the surface of the Sun.

Surrounding the core is the mantle, which is about 2,900 km (1,800 miles) thick. The mantle is mostly solid rock, but it can flow very slowly over geological time scales (like a very, very thick treacle). On top of the mantle is the crust, which is where we live. The crust is very thin compared to the rest of the Earth - typically 5-70 km (3-44 miles) thick. The oceanic crust (under the oceans) is generally thinner (5-10 km) than the continental crust (30-70 km).

Some Very Moving Images

Planet Earth and all its inhabitants orbit the Sun at about 67,000 mph. This is actually quite fast. If you were to stand still and watch the Earth go by at that speed, you would see it flash past in approximately 1.3 seconds. That is, assuming you had excellent eyesight and could track something moving that fast, which you almost certainly couldn’t.

Something very big smashed into the Earth a long, long time ago, knocking it off its axis. As a result, the Earth is actually tilted at an angle of about 23.4° relative to its orbit around the Sun. This tilt is what gives us our seasons.

The Earth, and us, orbit the Sun in 365 and a bit days at 67,000 mph.

The Milky Way spiral galaxy speeds through space at 1.3 million mph. This is in fact much faster, though, since any and all large objects in the universe are moving at incredible speeds relative to the expansion of the universe - and that expansion is accelerating. As we stand around in our ordinary lives - watching the telly, eating, sleeping - we are hurtling through space at literally breakneck speed. It all seems stationary to us, though, because everything we can see is also hurtling along with us. This is not to say that we’ll never get to see stars and galaxies whizzing past though, as our universe does keep “re-arranging” itself quite regularly. Well, regularly if we’re thinking on a geological timescale. We can see lots of galaxies moving, but most of them are so far away that we don’t really notice them shifting about much. That would be like sitting on a train in a station waiting for the train next to you to move, but that train is in Tokyo and you’re in London.

Expansion of Space - Impact on What Can Be Seen from Earth

The Sun and its attendant planets, Earth included, spins in the Orion spiral of the Milky Way galaxy with 200 to 400 billion other stars. Light years from the centre of the Milky Way, this distance is constantly increasing, because of the expansion of the universe. Eventually, after some tens of billions of years, the galaxy will become apart from the Milky Way itself. Andromeda and about 50 nearby, stellar stars will be all the Milky Way. Andromeda and Large Magellanic Clouds will still be visible. Of course, this assumes there is some intelligent being to do the seeing.
