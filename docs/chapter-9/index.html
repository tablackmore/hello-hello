Chapter 9

Now back to our Ancestors

Pages 70–80

To date we’ve focused on Modern Humans (MH). These are people like you and me. If you transported the first MHs from 300,000 years ago to modern times, they would not stand out in a crowd. Of course, first you would have to give them a bath, haircut and then put them in jeans and a shirt. If they spoke, that would be a giveaway and they might swiftly come to a sticky end if plonked down in a busy city street. Cars are more dangerous than mammoths and cave bears. But the human family lineage goes back much further in time than the 300k period occupied by Modern Humans (MH), i.e. Homo Sapiens.

Our MH ancestors include: Homo Heidelbergensis; Homo Ergaster; Homo Erectus; Homo Habilis and the Australopithecines. The latter are oldest. They can be traced back more than 4 million years. Our bipedal ancestors are thought to have split from the ape line about 6 million years ago. But we know little or nothing about the very first non-ape creatures, who could have broken away from apes about 12 to 6 million years ago. We will talk about the ones we do know something about later. What these ancestors all had in common with MH, their evolutionary descendants, is a bipedal, upright posture. They also had comparatively large brains, although smaller than ours. All of them used tools, mostly stone of varying sophistication, although usage of tools by one of our earliest ancestors, Australopithecines, is debated. The very first of these bipeds, Danuvius, could be over 12 million years old. This baboon sized ape’s bones were discovered in a clay pit at Hammerschmiede, Bavaria, Germany in 2019. Danuvius was still very much at home in trees, like other apes, using his long arms to swing on branches and climb trees. He had not moved on to open savannah to spend life mostly by walking upright. Interestingly, Bavaria is some distance from Africa where us Modern Humans are thought first to have evolved from bipedal ancestors. Is Germany really in the frame as a long-distance ancestor of us Modern Humans? The jury is still out on this one. Perhaps Danuvius died out without leaving a successor. Later, brainier and walkier MH predecessors were definitely African.

Neanderthal (Homo Neanderthalis)

But if we are considering MH predecessors the premier focus has to be on Neanderthals. These fierce browed individuals are more often portrayed in the media than other MH predecessors. They’ve made regular appearances in newspapers and scientific journals (Science, Scientific American, New Scientist and National Geographic). They’ve also captured our imagination through books (Jean Auel’s engrossing

p.71

Earth’s Children series) and in films (Quest for Fire, 1981). They even appear on TV (Neanderthal, Channel 4; Neanderthal Code, ABC documentary 2010, among others). Through all this Neanderthals have become a small part of popular culture, although sometimes cruelly misrepresented as brutal, ugly, shambling beasts.

The name Neanderthal stems from the first discovery of a partial skeleton in 1856. This was unearthed in a cave located in the Neander Valley, near Dusseldorf, Germany. 50 odd years later (1908) a French paleontologist called Marcellin Boule wrote about the Neanderthal remains found at La Chapelle-aux-Saints in France. Boule’s description of a hairy, bent-kneed creature, who was very slow witted, was characterised by his illustration.

In 1888 Herman Schaaffhausen, Professor of Anatomy at the University of Bonn, drew an illustration of a brutish Neanderthal man which may have influenced Boule. This, we now know, was an even worse travesty than the later re-construction made by Boule. To be kind to these two, they did not have the wealth of fossils since discovered. Nor did they have the investigative techniques available today.

Together, the Boule and Schaaffhausen drawings set an indelible image of our cousin for about 100 years. But today, we know that our cousin was much more like us. An increased number of skeletal findings, nearly 500, and the application of scientific techniques completely unknown in Boule’s time, especially DNA analysis, has helped raise the stature (literally and metaphorically) of the Neanderthal.

In 2020, Rebecca Wragg Sykes well received book, “Kindred”, explained the life and culture of Neanderthals was more complex than originally thought.

Our updated understanding has also turned on its head some earlier statements about Neanderthals. We now have much more knowledge of Neanderthals, their anatomy, intelligence and how they lived. We have moved well beyond the studies made by B and S in the later part of the 19th century. There have been detailed examinations of hundreds of Neanderthal sites. Some major ones include Shanidar in Iraq; Kebara in Israel; Krapina in Croatia; Combe-Grenal and Abri in France. As a result of all these finds and use of modern means of examination some comparisons can now be accurately made between Neanderthals and MH:

  • They were generally 13cm (6") shorter;
  • Their average brain size was very slightly larger than MH (size is not necessarily synonymous with thinking power);
  • Neanderthals were of stockier build and had heavier facial features, with a sloping brow;
  • Like MH they were artists, but were much older artists with Neanderthal cave paintings, crude animal and lines, discovered in the Maltravieso Cave, Spain. They are dated 64,000 years old.

MH come in a wide variety of shapes, heights and colours. If a Neanderthal was brought forward to modern times, shaved, given a good haircut and put in jeans and a polo shirt, he would not look out of place on the South Bank in London.

Neanderthals were the dominant bi-pedal species in Europe for about 200,000 years, during the Middle Paleolithic. But once MH began moving into the continent around 45,000 years ago their numbers began to wane. Neanderthals eventually died out about 40,000 years ago. The last out-post is thought to have been Gibraltar. However, there is some evidence to suggest they may have survived longer in sub-arctic Russia, at Byzovaya. At their peak it’s estimated that there were 70,000.

p.73

They extended across most of Europe; the Ukraine; parts of Russia and the Levant (roughly Syria, Lebanon and Jordan). No remains have been found in Africa.

Both MH and Neanderthals shared a common ancestor. There seems to be debate about timing. Our common ancestor could have lived about 600,000 years ago, possibly Homo Heidelbergensis, or perhaps Homo Rhodesiensis - discovered in Rhodesia (now Zambia) by Arthur Woodward in 1921.

Neanderthals hunted big game, just as did humans, but their weapons were cruder and more limited. They had to get close to use short, stabbing spears. As a result, many Neanderthal skeletons show signs of bone fractures, perhaps from having to get closer to their prey than human hunters. This may also be evidence that although Neanderthal brains were at least as large as MH, it did not mean they were as intelligent. They were artists too as evidenced by cave drawings at El Castillo, Spain which date back nearly 41,000 years.

If you query their part in popular culture, referred to earlier, what is indisputable is the role they play in us, i.e. in our bodies. Research into DNA has revealed that in present day Eurasians up to 4% of human DNA is from Neanderthals. So, we are partly Neanderthal, sort of. “They are not totally extinct. In some of us they live on, a little bit,” said Professor Svante Paabo, from the Max Planck Institute for Evolutionary Anthropology in Leipzig, Germany.

Whether or not Neanderthals and Palaeolithic MH were kissing cousins is in dispute. Some research suggests that although some European MH and

p.74

Neanderthals share a limited amount of DNA the origin of this may be from an earlier time. It could come from the common ancestor, probably Heidelbergensis, of both MH and Neanderthals. The diversity offered to MH through Neanderthal DNA is beneficial. It helped strengthen the MH immune system and so increased survival rates. They were an evolutionary advantage.

Neanderthals are known to have engaged in intentional burial which indicated the possibility of religious or spiritual beliefs. The earliest such burial, so far discovered, dates back 50,000 years ago. In a 70,000-year-old site the Shanidar Cave, Iraq, Neanderthal skeletons appeared to have been buried with flowers. The site in Sima de las Paloma in Spain found three Neanderthals buried with their hands close to their heads. There is the possibility of an even earlier burial site at Krapina in Croatia, dating to 130,000 years old. In February 2020 Dr Emma Pomeroy of Cambridge University said: “In recent years there has been increasing evidence that Neanderthals were more sophisticated than previously thought…” Rebecca Wragg Sykes explained these discoveries in her book ‘Kindred’.

Evidence of intentional burial is not surprising given how close they were to MH in intelligence, habitat, hunting weapons and prey. Neanderthals are also thought to have used the feathers of birds for decorative purposes. This usage is common to many older MH societies. But they were a very long way back from the more sophisticated religious practices of later Mesolithic, Modern Humans, and even further away from the first biblical written works.

So, if there is God, how does he deal with our cousin the Neanderthal? Here was a creature very similar to Modern Humans. He had a brain the same size, at least, as ours. The Neanderthals art and spiritual beliefs seem similar to ancient MH. It appears God, if he exists, decided to remove our close cousins completely from the scene. They have been extinct for about 40,000 years. Not surprisingly, modern, major MH religions ignore the question of Neanderthals and offer no cogent explanation of their relationship to a God.

Our evolutionary predecessors

We, Modern Humans (MH) or Homo Sapiens, follow behind a long line of long legged and increasingly intelligent bipeds. This lineage is known as Hominins. Apart from a few Ardi creatures our ancestral Hominins were all other varieties of Homo. Homo is the Latin for “human” and is the genus name designated by the great Swedish taxonomist Carl Linnaeus in his ground-breaking classification system.

p.75

Our emergence is from a long line of Hominin lineage. Bipedalism, better brains and opposing thumbs were the main forte of this line. The evolutionary process concluded that these anatomical features were definite winners. So, each succeeding bipedal Hominin was better equipped physically and mentally than their predecessor.

Our Hominin line meanders and has a few dead-end offshoots. It also is unclear in places. We are not entirely certain who exactly were our earliest or latest bipedal ancestors. It’s likely that some very ancient bipeds were neighbours and cousins rather than parents or grandparents. But as more fossil discoveries emerge we are viewing a clearer picture of who was great (x times) granny or granddad. They would have been several thousand generations back.

About 6 million years ago some tree living apes in Africa decided life was a bit easier if they came down from the trees. These were go-ahead types who learned to stand on their own two feet and walk tall. Why did this ground-based habitat start to become favoured? Probably because of changing climate, maybe several to-and-fro changes within a relatively short geological period. These climatic fluctuations would have put pressure on food resources. Smarter bipeds could scavenge for food further and faster when on the ground than when swinging through trees. Climate change may have eventually resulted in fewer trees and more open country, with the need to adapt to different foods. Our direct ancestors were the ones able to evolve and adapt to the changing environment. Other hominids died out because they were too stuck in the mud, or in the trees, and were specialized eaters who ran out of food. But, the Pliocene epoch tree dwellers did not all die out. Some went on to become present day chimpanzees, bonobos, orangutans and gorillas.

Who exactly were our bipedal ancestors?

Hard to pronounce as well as hard to define, Sahelanthropus Tchadensis (named Toumai, “hope of life”) who padded around Chad 6/7 million years ago may have been the original ancestor of us MH. Toumai had his feet more firmly on the ground than his mainly tree living predecessors. It is disputed though whether he really differs from the earliest Ardipithecus (Ardi for short). Ardi is generally thought to be the first intelligent biped in direct line to us. The more pronounceable and later Orrorin (original man) from Kenya is another front-runner vying for the title of first in line. Orrorin seems to have more going for him/her anatomically, but the jury is still out. Ardi holds on to the gold medal for now.

p.76

This is probably the current best presumption of Modern Humans main evolutionary chain origin but does not encompass all the intelligent bipeds. Apart from Toumai and Orrorin some others were offshoots from the main line. Most of these died out without gifting an evolutionary successor. This included a much more recent biped, discovered in 2013, Homo Naledi, who lived in South Africa between 2 and 3 million years ago and may have ritually buried their dead. Ancient burials of Naledi have been found in the Rising Star cave in South Africa.

The other dead-enders include: Australopithecus Sediba and Australopithecus Africanus. There are several others but two much more recent species are particularly interesting because they were definitely contemporaries of MH. First we have Denisovans. In March 2010, a finger bone was discovered in the Denisova cave, located in the Altai Mountains in Siberia.

The bone was dated to 41,000 years ago and genetically distinct from Neanderthals and Modern Humans. Denisovans are thought to have had the same ancestor, Heidelbergensis as both Modern Humans and Neanderthals. Their descendance began about 350,000 years ago.

These stone-agers were a separate species of human but crossbred with some of our ancestors. DNA analysis shows that up to 6% of their DNA is in present day Melanesian (South Sea Islanders) and Aboriginal people. It is thought that Tibetans successful adaptation to high altitude is due to inheritance of a Denisovan gene.

Second up, in the list of dead-enders, we have the aptly named hobbit species, Homo Floresiensis (Flores Man). This 3ft high hobbit-sized had long arms and a skull about as big as a large grapefruit. Flo lived about 95,000 until as recently as 12,000 years ago on the island of Flores in Indonesia.

p.77

For a while they co-existed with Modern Humans on the same island. It is disputed whether Flo was a separate species or just a smaller than usual Homo Sapiens. It can be very difficult for anthropologists to determine whether ancient MH ancestors were slight variations of the same species or a completely separate species. Imagine an alien landing on planet Earth today and meeting a short, yellowish skinned and narrow eyed Japanese then a beefy, black curly haired Nigerian and finally a tall blond blue-eyed Swede. He might think these were all different species, but we know better. These are all part of the great modern human family.

Anthropologists can fall within one of two camps. There are the lumpers - those who think that the many possibly different intelligent bipeds are in fact slight variations of the same species. And then the splitters, not a hard one to guess what they think - that there are many different species.

Our immediate ancestor in the Hominin line is believed to either be Homo Heidelbergensis or Homo Erectus. Both have been found through Europe, Asia and especially Africa. H. Erectus were uniquely long-lasting among all Hominins. They were about for at least 1.9 million years and spread around the planet from their original African home at least 1 million years before MH made their move.

Five Homo Erectus skulls, dated from 1.8 million years ago, were found in a cave at Dmanisi, Georgia in 2013. These have been analysed and show a wide variation in brain case size. This has led some palaeontologists to believe that some of the claimed different species thought to be discovered in Africa and elsewhere, like Homo Habilis, are of the same lineage as Erectus. Other experts still disagree, saying the brain size is just one aspect of differences. The debate continues, we still have the two camps, lumpers and splitters, vying for the supremacy of their hypothesis.
