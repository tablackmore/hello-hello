Chapter 24

Physics and Quantum Mechanics

Pages 397–436

Classical and Modern Physics: Anyone seriously interested in questions such as: “where did it all begin” and “where will it all end” has to take a hard look at physics. Physics gives us some strong, supported answers. These are not yet perfect and by no means complete, but they give alternative possibilities to Godly scriptures in telling how it was, how it is and how it will be.

In the scientific world physics and its supporting mathematics are crucial in understanding not only the world but also the universe we inhabit. Because of its wide-ranging nature, physics is broken down into several specialist areas. Two of the main disciplines are astro-physics, which is closely linked to cosmology, which looks at the broad picture, and particle physics, which is synonymous with quantum mechanics.

Astro-physics deals with the detail of the very big - the whole universe. It includes galaxies, stars, black holes, planets and the elusive dark energy and dark matter. All this is the stuff of classic physics. This was the original development of physics, which was broad-brush in nature. Classical physics is powerfully underpinned by the theories of Newton and Einstein. Here, Einstein’s theory of General Relativity is the ultimate explanation of the very big, although not the whole story. Since Einstein’s seminal paper we have come to have a much clearer understanding of how the universe works, not just the very big, as expounded by Newton and Einstein, but also the very small. This is the world of minuteness, quantum mechanics.

Quantum mechanics, is part of modern physics.

QM is a tricky subject. In Terry Pratchett’s book, ‘Eric’, the boy Eric asks wizard Rincewind: “What is quantum mechanics?” Rincewind replies: “I don’t know, people who repair quantums I suppose.” Rincewind did not get it quite right. QM is not a job title. QM deals with the very, very small; that is the atom and particles that are far smaller than the atom.

The amazing, ultra-micro universe of sub-atomic particles such as electrons; quarks and neutrinos are increasing revealed by work on QM, which also encompasses these areas:

p.398
  • Interaction between matter, and forces that interact with matter like gravity, the weak and strong nuclear force and the electro-magnetic force;
  • The concept of motion;
  • Space-time.

Not unexpectedly, there is some overlap between classical physics and quantum mechanics. For instance, both deal with space-time. Classical physics is concerned with gravity while quantum mechanics speculates about the existence of the graviton, a possible force carrying particle, and gravitational waves, first suggested by physicist and mathematician Oliver Heaviside in 1893. These were finally discovered, disputedly, in 2016. So, the study of physics, especially quantum mechanics, helps us on the road to understanding the entire universe. It may eventually tell us what came before the Big Bang.

Quantum mechanics, what was its origin? Some of us might think of QM as a very modern science, but its origin goes back to 440 BCE. This is when the Greek Democritus decided to call the smallest possible amount of substance an atom. He did not know it at the time but he had opened up the road of particle physics and fundamental particles. It took about 2,000 years before anyone else drove further down this road. In 1865 James Clerk Maxwell published his theory of electromagnetism; one of the four forces of the Standard Model - more on the SM shortly. British physicist JJ Thompson followed him in 1897. Thompson originated the idea that sub-atomic particles, now known as electrons, were a fundamental component of atoms. He also calculated how tiny they were; about 1,000 times smaller than an atom. Since Thompson’s discovery there has been an explosion of particle discoveries. In the early 1900’s Ernest Rutherford correctly defined the planetary-like structure of the atom. He nearly got it right. Ernest supposed electrons orbiting a central nucleus – like planets orbiting a sun. We know now that electrons don’t orbit. They seem to flicker in and out. Their position is impossible to determine accurately. There’s more on of these four; Democritus, Maxwell, Thompson and Rutherford, and on others later on.

In modern times we realize that the atom of Democritus is not the smallest particle, although his concept of something that cannot be further divided is still relevant. These tinies are now called fundamental particles. ‘Fundamental’ is the qualification used to describe particles, which are not made up of other particles. They are on their own, fundamental in fact. Some fundamentals, quarks and electrons, can be found within an atom.

p.399

For example, the simplest atom, hydrogen, comprises of one electron and one proton. The proton is made up of three fundamental particles - quarks. All atoms have electrons and protons. All bar the lightest atom, hydrogen, also have a third negative component, neutrons. Like their positive neighbour, the proton, these are also made up of three quarks.

In the period between Einstein’s annus mirabilis, 1906, and the early 1960’s great theoretical physicists teased out the weird properties of life within and outside the atom. From this emerging world of quantum mechanics, more and more fundamental particles were discovered. Some aspects of the quantum mechanics world do not fit neatly with the classical physics defined by Newton and Einstein. This annoyed Einstein no end. He wanted quantum mechanics to show the sort of tidy order that would lead seamlessly into the larger world he had so brilliantly constructed a template for. Newton, the other giant of classical physics, could not be bothered at all, that’s because he was dead long before QM detail appeared on the scene! Einstein was so dismayed at the quantum mechanics he apparently said: “God does not play dice.”

Bizarre Aspects of Quantum Mechanics: Quantum mechanics starts with the simple idea that energy does not come in just any amount; it comes in discrete chunks, called quanta. This is quite easy to understand, but other properties of quantum particles are not at all easy. They are strange and hard to fully comprehend, as even Einstein found. If it weren’t for the fact that they have been tried and tested, these properties would be unbelievable.

Here are those weird quantum properties:

p.400
  • Superposition: A particle exists in all possible states or locations simultaneously, that is, until it is observed. It can be in two places at once; move at different speeds, and have different energy levels. It is only when observed or measured that the particle ‘chooses’ one particular state. Just looking at it makes it decide a single position. There’s the example of Schrodinger’s cat, both alive and dead in its box until the box is opened, then a choice is made and it settles into one or other state. The ‘particles’ referred to in superposition, Schrodinger’s moggy aside, are extremely tiny. The biggest to be scientifically superimposed, as at 2019, had only 356 atoms, so you’ll need a powerful electron microscope to see them.
  • Entanglement: Two particles can become entangled so that their properties depend on each other - no matter how far apart they get. A measurement of one seems to affect the measurement of the other instantaneously, that is faster than the speed of light - an idea Einstein called “spooky action at a distance”. There’s a theory, as yet unproven, that Robins use entangled particles in their retina, together with the Earth’s magnetic field, to navigate. Each year, in Autumn, they fly from Scandinavia to the Mediterranean, without any use of maps!
  • Tunnelling: A particle can push through an energy barrier, it disappears on one side of it and emerges on the other. It’s just like walking straight through a wall without knocking it down. Recent developments in quantum biology have looked at how the quantum world is important to life. Enzymes, essential to life, use the quantum tunnelling magic to transfer electrons and protons from one molecule to another, so speeding up the chemical reaction in a living cell. This was discovered in the 1970’s and 1980’s.

The above are definite, proven quantum effects, apart from the Robin. There are two more speculative, and even weirder possible quantum associations: retro-causality and the multiverse. First, retro-causality:

What’s to come effects what’s past and what’s now Retro-causality

Retro-causality is also known as reverse causality. It proposes that there can be a reverse of cause and effect.

p.401

Retro-causality says you can have something happen before its cause, so the future can impact on the past. The theory suggests particles like positrons could move backwards in time, as an electron, which then gains a positive charge. When the positron meets an electron, which has a negative charge, they annihilate each other. This annihilation is an event happening before the cause – the time travelling positron. Speculatively – there is not an ounce of supporting evidence – forms of retro-causality might allow traversable wormholes, so that we humans could enjoy time-travel. Retro-causality may also have some, as yet unknown, association with the proven effect of quantum entanglement. This could help explain a great deal of the seeming magic of the quantum world. It gives a new slant on the nature of reality.

Some of this magic of the quantum world is very useful in modern applications: computers; tablets; smart phones and imaging. Quantum effects may also be very relevant in biology. In their 2014 book “Life on the Edge” physicist Jim Al-Khalili and molecular geneticist Johnjoe McFadden argue that the quantum effect can be found in animal cells. This follows on the work of Erwin Schrödinger some 70 years earlier. He made a case for a link between quantum physics and biology. In Jim and Johnjoe’s book they cite the example of the European Robin and how it is able to navigate on its migratory route to the south using quantum entanglement.

There’s the well-known double slit experiment, this demonstrates one more quantum effect. Single electrons, which are fired at the two slits, go through both at the same time. The experiment illustrates that quantum particles can be both a particle and a wave. As a wave they can go through both but not as separate particles. This quantum property is called wave-particle duality.

If quantum effects are so common - we know of some used in electronics, which is everywhere - why can’t we humans use all their features? It would be so useful to be able to walk through walls, using quantum tunnelling. This ability would save on the cost of doors. It would also make life more interesting for those responsible for bank vault security. Being able to be in two places at once would be quite handy too. We could navigate without the need for GPS devices, and watch a rugby match while at the same time sitting down at home to catch up on that episode of Downton Abbey or Doctor Who that we missed.

p.402

If we weren’t sure which of two doors to go through, the ability to pass through both at the same time, acting like a wave, would save messing about.

Sadly, we cannot use this quantum magic, at least not yet. This is because the quantum effect seems to work very well with minute particles but progressively diminishes, as they get larger. Perhaps if we dieted extremely rigourously………?

A multiverse? Arguably the strangest and most exciting aspect of quantum mechanics is that it may point to the existence of a multiverse. The multiverse assumes there are an infinitive number of universes, other than our own. In which case anything that can happen will have happened in one universe or other. There are several versions of this theory, none have any supporting proof but the idea seems to be gaining in popularity. The continuing debate, about whether the multiverse does or does not exist, has divided the physics and cosmological community. Those in favour include: Stephen Hawking, Brian Greene, Max Tegmark, Michio Kaku, Martin Rees, Laura Mersini-Houghton, and Sean Carroll. Among the opponents are: David Gross, Paul Steinhardt, Neil Turok, Viatcheslav Mukhanov, Michael S. Turner and Roger Penrose.

Parallel universes Closely allied to the more usual concept of the ‘multiverse’ are ‘parallel universes’. These are said to mimic our own universe except for variation caused by an event, which could be large or small. For example, in a parallel universe Hitler and the Nazis could have won the second World War. That’s a big event. Another parallel universe might diverge because a person went to work on a bike whereas in our universe that same person had caught a bus. US physicist Hugh Everett is seen as the father of parallel universes theory, called the many-worlds theory. He came up with this idea in the nineteen fifties. There is absolutely no evidence at all for parallel universes, but it remains a potent idea with physicists in modern times. The ‘many-worlds’ idea though remains unproven, pure speculation.

In his BBC radio interview by Jim Al Khalili, Martin Rees explained the idea of a multiverse. He said there could be many Big Bangs resulting in different universes with very different physical laws to ours, but he accepts this is a long way from being proven. It could be reasoned that if conditions existed some 13.8 billion years ago, that gave rise to the singularity and Big Bang, why couldn’t the same conditions result in more Big Bangs, and many universes? If it’s a monotheistic God who kicked off everything, why would He stop at creating just the one universe - boring?

p.403

Many universes

Nobel prize winner Steven Weinberg said in 2007 that if the multiverse existed then we could never fully tease out all of the Standard Model reasoning : “…the hope of finding a rational explanation for the precise values of quark masses and other constants of the Standard Model that we observe in our Big Bang is doomed, for their values would be an accident of the particular part of the multiverse in which we live.” These other universes would have different basic physics to our own. In many the electro-magnetic force would not exist. Therefore, atoms could not be formed and so neither could stars or planets.” The multiverse remains an exciting speculation. Sadly, at present, it’s only in Science Fiction that we can move between universes. Now back to those fundamental particles.

Standard Model This theory attempts to impose a structure on fundamental particles, and to some very small extent tame quantum mechanics. It is also the best yet proven attempt to come with a theory that draws together classic Newtonian/Einsteinian physics with the bizarre world of quantum mechanics.

The Standard Model proposes that there are 12 fundamental particles of all matter and that these are acted on by one of more of four force carrying particles.

p.404

The 4 Force Carrying Particles (called Bosons)

Photon – an Electromagnetic Force, like light, often known as a messenger particle, infinite range Gluon – a Strong force Binds together particles within the atom, very short range Z&W bosons – Two weak forces, associated with nuclear decay – radiation, very short range Plus the Higgs Boson – This is associated with the Higgs Field.

Matter Particles

The 12 matter particles are called Fermions. They are split up into 6 quarks and 6 leptons. Each lepton has a corresponding neutrino. These are energy carrying particles with extremely low mass and with a high velocity. All these particles have reverse anti-matter particles. If the matter and anti-matter particles meet they annihilate each other and are converted to pure energy. This is a sort of mini Big Bang. Luckily for us, at the beginning of the universe, for some reason, the number of matter particles exceeded those of anti-matter. Hence stars, galaxies, planets life and modern humans.

  1. Higgs Boson: This particle was predicted by Peter Higgs to be an essential element of the Standard Model. It was discovered at CERN (the European Organisation for Nuclear Research) in 2012. The Higgs Bosun is associated with a field through which other particles pass. Particles such as electrons, quarks, and Z & W bosons, gain mass as they pass through the Higgs field. Other particles like photons and gluons are unaffected by the field. Without Higgs ability to impart mass, our universe could not exist as it is, so it’s sometimes called ‘the God Particle’.
  2. Gravitational Waves: Einstein predicted gravitational waves a hundred years ago. He said they are ripples in space time that come from the beginning of the universe. Colliding black holes are also thought to cause them. Some scientists claim that advanced LIGO, the Laser Interferometer Gravitational-Wave Observatory, has identified them. They say this is the result of minute ripples in space-time caused by two colliding black holes a billion light years away. However, the jury is still out, other scientists are not so sure. They are incredibly difficult to detect.
p.405

Relative size: The particles in the Standard Model are unbelievably small. This is why they have taken so long to detect. Some particles, like photons and gluons, are counted as massless. The heaviest of the three neutrinos is 1 billion times lighter than the lightest atom - hydrogen. Neutrinos are constantly fired out from the sun. They pass through our bodies without trouble. To an incredibly tiny neutrino nothing appears solid. For them there are huge gaps inside every atom and vast gaps between the atoms that combine as molecules to make everyday material objects like trees, houses, tables, chairs, computers and TVs. For a neutrino, if it had intelligence and sight, looking at a dining room table would be the equivalent of a human trying to view the entire Milky Way Galaxy in one glance.

The Standard Model is the best-detailed attempt yet to get to the bottom of all matter, however it does not quite tell the whole story. Apart from gravity and its predicted associate the graviton, there are other predicted but unknown substances that make up all matter: dark matter and dark energy. These two, are theorised but not yet fully known. They are thought to account for about 95% of all matter in the universe. Antimatter is another substance not fully understood.

p.406

Quantum Mechanics alongside General Relativity:

The biggest question of all is how Quantum Mechanics fits with Einstein’s theory of General Relativity. The theories of the very small and the very large are hard to get into synch with each other.

Martin Rees, Astronomer Royal and cosmologist, said:

“The bedrock nature of space and time and the unification of cosmos and quantum are surely among science’s great ‘open frontiers.’ These are parts of the intellectual map where we’re still groping for the truth - where, in the fashion of ancient cartographers, we must still inscribe “here be dragons.”

Well, how can those dragons be slain, or removed from the scene? To achieve this physicists and cosmologists say we need a “Great Theory of Everything”. Perhaps first developing a unifying theory of Quantum Gravity as one step towards this. Gravity is generally a weak force but operates across the vast distances of the universe. It becomes stronger the more massive an object is. Famously, the gravity of black holes is so strong nothing can escape it, even light. Gravity’s predicted but unproven force carrier is the graviton. This is theorised to be so small as to make it undetectable at present.

p.407

Gravity and, the graviton (we’ve found gravitational waves but not the graviton) have to be brought into any unified Theory of Everything.

So, the Standard Model is pretty good, but a little lacking in some areas. The best-known attempt to move beyond the Standard Model is called String Theory.

“In string theory all particles are vibrations on a tiny rubber band; physics is the harmonies on the string; chemistry is the melodies we play on vibrating strings; the universe is a symphony of strings, and the “Mind of God” is cosmic music resonating in 11-dimensional hyperspace.” = M Theory.

Michio Kaku theoretical physicist

A complete understanding of how our universe works, at both the big and small level, is at the heart of String Theory, where fundamental particles are replaced by even smaller strings. It’s proposed these vibrating elements (strings), interact with sheets of energy known as branes. The strings only have one dimension and the branes two dimensions. They are either open ended or close loop. These strings are thought to be the base of all objects within our universe. Another feature is that ten dimensions are proposed in String Theory, i.e. nine spatial dimensions plus a single time dimension. We are normally aware of just four dimensions – height, width, depth and time. The additional dimensions are thought to be extremely small, much, much less than microscopic and confined by the branes. This combination of ultra-tininess and confinement makes them impossible, at present, to detect. The issue of non-detection is the main problem with String Theory. It is nowhere near as tested and proven as the Standard Model. We cannot be sure of it.

The same criticism can be made of associated theories, like Supersymmetry and M Theory. the M signifies either Magic, Mystery or Membrane. You take your choice, according to its inventor, theoretical physicist Edward Witten. It predicts the existence of 11 dimensions, 7 more, on top of the normal 4 - these four are the three spatial dimensions plus time. The new set of dimensions comprise ten spatial and one time. That’s M Theory in brief. Like other theories it has yet to be proven.

The other contender to String Theory, Supersymmetry is even odder than M Theory. The current research into ideas like Supersymmetry goes back to around 1918. This is when the brilliant theorems of mathematician Amalie Noether began to emerge.

p.408

Supersymmetry proposes a number of things:

  • extra space-time dimensions;
  • every particle has a corresponding super-partner; e.g. the photon has a photino, the gluons have gluinos, etc.

But photinos and gluinos have yet to be found. So, Supersymmetry is too odd and too unproven at present to suggest it is the answer.

Theoretical Physicists continue work around ideas like String and M Theory and Supersymmetry. The issue of possible extra dimensions, beyond the 4 we know of, is still very much at the forefront of thinking. The basic idea is that the reality we experience is a limited one, because we can only experience 4 dimensions, what if there are more everything might then be explainable? Early in 2019 Prof. David Berman said that currently up to 258 dimensions are postulated!

We don’t yet know the definitive answer about the composition of all matter and how this fits in with very large, i.e. with the Newton and Einstein’s theories. Another conundrum is the existence, or not, of the multiverse. We, Homo Sapiens, might not be the ones capable of finding the answer, but we are not necessarily the end of an evolutionary line. The rise of our replacement, perhaps Homo Superior, could mean our evolutionary successors have no difficulty with finding a theory of everything and proving the multiverse exists. Or perhaps some super-being from another galaxy or universe will pay us a visit and explain how it all works.

Some Greats of Quantum Physics

This list is deliberately restricted to those people who have made momentous discoveries of the sub-atomic world. No doubt other names might be added. It excludes great scientists of classical physics; like Galileo, Kepler, Newton and Faraday. They and others have their work briefly described towards the end of this chapter. Einstein could go in under both the banner of classic and modern, quantum, physics, as could some others. Classic physics arose in the period roughly before the beginning of the 20th century. Some of those scientists who have had a huge effect by bringing the modern world of quantum mechanics, physics and cosmology to the general public are described at the end of this chapter.

Here are greats of the world of the very, very small:

p.409

1. Democritus (460 to 370 BCE)

Democritus has already had a bit of an airing in the philosophy chapter, but he must be mentioned aging as an early scientist. He was born in Thrace, then part of Greece. We start with Democritus because he was the first with a definite interest and theory of the very small. Nearly 2,500 years ago this great Greek thinker reasoned that if you kept cutting up a rock you would eventually get a fragment so tiny it would not be capable of further division. It would be an irreducible and indestructible, basic building block of matter; the smallest particle possible. He came to this conclusion without the benefit of any scientific instrumentation. Democritus, together with his mentor Leucippus, are thought to have been the first to formulate this theory and give a name to this smallest particle, the atom. Of the two, Leucippus and Democritus, it is the later who is chiefly historically recognised. Democritus was a pre-Socratic philosopher. His name means “chosen of the people” and hence this name’s link to the modern concept of democracy. Not a bad achievement having some influence on both modern particle physics and democracy. Democritus’ other claim to fame was that he was among the first to attempt a scientific approach to understanding the world. He rejected the normal, unthinking approach of his time; this identified all unknowns with the work of the Gods. Perhaps his rigorous scientific approach to metaphysical philosophy is why Plato so disliked him. He thought that all Democritus books should be burned. It could be argued that Democritus has had the last laugh. His idea of the atom has outlasted Plato’s concept of perfect forms. Democritus was also fairly unique, certainly for his era, in living to the ripe old age of 90.

2. James Clerk Maxwell (1831 to 1879)

Many physicists reckon Maxwell is on a par with Newton and Einstein, such was his original and fundamental contribution, particular in describing the electromagnetic force. Einstein said of his work: “…. the most profound and the most fruitful that physics has experienced since the time of Newton." In 2000, a poll of 100 top physicists voted him the third most important physicist of all time, after Newton and Einstein.

James was a Scot, born in Edinburgh. He was a precocious child. At age eight he could recite all 176 verses of the 119th psalm. Later, while attending Edinburgh Academy, he won prizes in

p.410

scripture, mathematics, English and poetry. Age 18, while at Edinburgh University, he published a paper “On the Equilibrium of Elastic Solids”. This was intrinsic to his later major work on electro-magnetics. He attended Cambridge University for six years from 1850, first at Peterhouse then Trinity. At the latter he joined an exclusive, secretive group called the Cambridge Apostles. This was a highly intellectual debating club. Here James was able to discuss and develop his understanding of Christian faith as well as science. He clearly would not accept an uncritical, faith-based approach to biblical study: “Now my great plan, which was conceived of old, … is to let nothing be wilfully left unexamined. Nothing is to be holy ground consecrated to Stationary Faith, whether positive or negative. ……Never hide anything…” His religious zeal brought him to conflict with Charles Darwin and his theory of evolution.

In 1865 James showed that light, electricity and electromagnetism were all connected to the electromagnetic field. He also said that light was a form of electromagnetic radiation and was a wave. Thanks to James we know that electromagnetic radiation is both a particle and a wave. He said the speed of light is always constant. James Maxwell’s ground-breaking work was an important building block for Einstein’s Special Relativity theory. His work contributed hugely to TV, radar, optics and the science of light.

3. Max Planck (1858 to 1947)

Max is renowned as the originator of quantum theory. A very brilliant man. In his case, the adage referring to someone lacking a few grey cells, ‘as thick as a plank’ would mean an extremely thick plank, several metres thick in fact. In December 1900 Max put forward a paper offering a solution to Blackbody Radiation (this is a theoretical object that absorbs all electromagnetic radiation falling on it). His work gained him the Nobel Prize in 1918: “in recognition the services he rendered to the advancement of physics by his discovery of energy quanta.”

What Max did was revolutionary in developing our understanding of the atomic and sub-atomic world. He devised a formula which calculated that the energy, either absorbed or emitted, of the smallest item of light of a given frequency is equal to the frequency multiplied by a particular constant figure, E=hf. E is the energy, h the constant and f the frequency. This constant, h, Max calculated. It is an incredibly tiny figure and is known as Planck’s constant, where h is 6.626x10⁻³⁴.

p.411

Like the speed of light, Planck’s constant is universal and unchanging. Max had introduced the world to the idea of a single packet of light energy, called the quantum. One quantum of electromagnetic energy is known as a photon. Einstein and Niels Bohr followed up on his ground-breaking work, as did other great quantum pioneers.

Apart from his Nobel prize, Max is also remembered and honoured in Germany by the establishment of the Max Planck Society (MPS) in 1948. This society has 83 institutions pursuing a wide range of science. Max was a believer in God, a Lutheran. In his 1937 lecture, ‘Religion and Science’ he said: “ Both religion and science need for their activities the belief in God…God stands for the former in the beginning, and for the latter at the end of the whole thinking.” Well, what about this next ‘thinking’ - no one should worry if they are referred to as ‘thick as a Plank’, that means they are a genius, but not necessarily a believer !

4. Amalie Noether (1882 to 1935)

Emmy, as she preferred to be called, was a brilliant German mathematician. She was of genius standing. Albert Einstein said she was the most significant and creative female mathematician of all time. High praise from him.

Emmy had a hard time in getting anywhere in education and in paid work in the early 1900’s. Women, in Germany, were not allowed to take degrees at this time, nor were they able to teach at university level. Eventually though, through sheer persitence, in 1907, Emmy got her PHD. She then worked for years unpaid, living in poverty through the first World War. Emmy was politically a socialist and pacifist. Eventually she was permitted to teach at the University of Gottingen, under the name of a male colleague. She was born to Jewish parents, although she does not seem to have had any strong religious views. The rise of the Nazis was part of the reason for her move to the USA in 1933, where she appreciated the much greater freedom allowed to both Jews and women. Emmy became a visiting professor at Bryn Mawr College, Pennsylvania.

After a short time back in Germany, in 1934, she again returned to Bryn Mawr. While still in the USA Emmy died quite suddenly in April 1935 after being diagnosed with cancer.

p.412

Her major work in mathematics was with algebra. Here, she came up with a ground-breaking theorem, that conclusively linked the physics of symmetry in nature with the universal laws of conservation. For the lay person, the meaning of her theorem, let alone the advanced maths involved, is not easy to understand. Symmetry’ means is a rule which says if y happens then x will always result, regardless. With a conservation law it means an object to which some form of transformation is applied remains the same, either in terms of moentum, angular momentum or energy. For example, whatever action you perform on some matter no energy can be actually lost, it has to go somewhere, perhaps transferred into momentum.

Emmy’s theorem was a huge breakthrough and is of fundamental importance to modern physics. Theoretical physicist Frank Wilczek of MIT said: “That theorem has been a guiding star to 20th and 21st century physics,”

5. J. J. Thompson (1884 to 1940)

John Joseph Thomson, eventually Sir John, was commonly known as J.J. His main claim to fame, in the pantheon of great particle physicists, is his discovery of the electron. He discovered this in 1897 while experimenting with cathode ray tubes, although he was not the first to use the term ‘electron’. Irish physicist George Johnstone Stoney has this honour.

The electron was the first sub-atomic particle to be discovered. This revelation dealt a death-blow to the atomic theory of John Dalton. The latter, a nineteenth century chemist, had produced the first, modern atomic theory. Dalton’s theory suggested atoms were indivisible, just as Democritus had surmised more than 2,000 years before.

J.J. was born in Manchester, quickly showed his brilliant mind while at Owen College, Manchester. He went to Trinity College; Cambridge where, quite quickly, he became second wrangler (not an important cowboy on a cattle drive!). He was later appointed Cavendish Professor of Physics and later still was made Master of Trinity.

J.J. was influenced by the work of James Maxwell and himself inspired other great physicists; both Earnest Rutherford and Niels Bohr were students of his. Awards: Nobel Prize in Physics, 1906; knighthood 1908; Royal Medal; Hughes Medal.

p.413

J.J. married one of his students, Rose Paget, their son; George went on to win a Nobel Prize just like his father. In a final recognition of the national and international importance of J.J.’s work he was buried at Westminster Abbey, near Newton and Darwin. Paul Dirac’s memorial is in the same area. J.J. was a devout, practicing Christian throughout his life, so he probably feels quite comfortable residing in Westminster Abbey, perhaps not so Paul Dirac.

6. Ernest Rutherford (1871 to 1937)

Democritus had imagined there must be atoms but Ernest Rutherford developed experiments to actually prove their existence and indicate their core structure. Ernest is known as the father of nuclear physics.

He grew up in New Zealand; the son of a Scottish wheelwright émigré and an English mother. She was a teacher. An early demonstration of his ability was the double first in Mathematics and Physics he gained at the University of New Zealand, Wellington. With the help of a scholarship in 1851 he went to Trinity College, Cambridge and worked at the Cavendish Laboratory, under J.J. Thompson J. J. said of him: “I have never had a student with more enthusiasm or ability for original research than Mr. Rutherford.”

Ernest then went to Canada and eventually ended up in Manchester University. Here he conducted his most important research, along with H. Geiger. He discovered alpha and beta radiation. His work on Alpha rays led to an experiment called the gold foil experiment. From this he concluded atoms had a dense core; a nucleus. He had discovered the nucleus and proposed a new model for the atom, replacing Thompson’s plum pudding version. This work was reckoned to be his greatest achievement. In 1908 he was awarded the Nobel Prize for Chemistry. He worked with many other greats at Manchester, including Niels Bohr. Ernest was a workaholic. Ernest ended his career at Cambridge, where on J.J.’s retirement. he became Cavendish Professor of Experimental Physics. There is no evidence that Ernest had any great interest in religion, although he did get married in St Paul’s Anglican church, Christchurch, New Zealand.

7. Niels Bohr (1885 to 1962)

Niels was highly unusual for a brilliant theoretical physicist. He had a talent most others of his ilk lacked. As a great organiser, and people person, he was able to draw together other brilliant minds to exchange ideas. He did this in 1920 when he became the first director of the Institute of Theoretical Physics in Copenhagen, now known as the Niels Bohr Institute. Niels successfully made this the gathering place for the top physicists of his time.

Physicists, mainly from Europe, would come to the Institute to consult with Niels about their latest thoughts. Niels was a welcoming, congenial host and early on he attracted to Copenhagen Hans Kramers from the Netherlands, Oskar Klein from Sweden, George de Hevesey from Hungary, Wojciech Rubinowicz from Poland and Svein Rosseland from Norway. These were top physicists in their day, but they may not be so well known in the 21st century. Later, at various times, more famous notables arrived: Werner Heisenberg; Erwin Schrodinger; Wolfgang Pauli and Paul Dirac.

Neil’s was born of a Lutheran father and Jewish mother. He was baptised a Lutheran but later quietly withdrew from the Lutheran church. On religion he said: “…the idea of a personal God is foreign to me.” But he knew there was no certainty over whether there was or was not a God: “The opposite of a fact is falsehood, but the opposite of one profound truth may very well be another profound truth.”

Niels Bohr’s version of an Atom

Apart from Niels skill at bringing together the great quantum physicists of his era he also made important personal contributions to this science. When at Manchester University he altered the atomic structure proposed by Rutherford to fit with Max Planck’s model. He put forward the idea that electrons moved in fixed orbits around the atomic nucleus. There were some later, further changes to the atomic model, especially resulting from Heisenberg’s work, but essentially Niels 1913 version is still valid to this day.

p.415

In recognition of his work in quantum physics Neil received the Nobel Prize in 1922 he left Denmark, to avoid the Nazis, in 1943, ending up in the USA working on the atomic bomb. Uneasy about this, he returned to Demark after WW2 and concentrated on the peaceful uses of atomic energy.

8. Albert Einstein (1879-1955)

Albert is rightly famous for his theories, perhaps topped by his ‘Special Theory of Relativity’. In the 1850’s it was worked out that the speed of light was fixed, unchanging, at 670 million mph. It was also calculated that nothing could go faster than the speed of Light. Albert picked up on these proofs. By his thinking, given if light speed could not change then something else had to give. It must be mass that changes, so that an object gets heavier the closer it gets to the speed of light., ergo mass and energy must be interrelated. Following up on this in 1905, at the ripe young age of 26, he came up with his famous equation: E = mc², where E = energy, m = mass and c = the speed of light. This was at the heart of his Special theory of Relativity, pointing out that matter and energy are different forms of the same thing, i.e. mass has an associated energy and vice versa. Albert went further, claiming there was a close link between space and time, a space-time concept. The curvature caused by space time interaction resulted in gravity, he theorised. After these amazing insights he then realised that the gravity of massive objects could cause distortion in space-time. Such objects could make light bend around them - gravitational lensing.

p.416

Astronomers could use gravitational lensing to see what was behind huge objects like black holes. These concepts, including a series of equations, known as field equations, were central of Einstein’s, theory of General Relativity. This was, revolutionary, gave some revision to Newton’s laws, and provided a new understanding, underpinning giant systems, the galaxies, the cosmos, space and time. But he also made a big impact in quantum mechanics, partly by his papers on the Photoelectric Effect but mostly by his rigorous opposition to quantum theory. Einstein with his colleagues Poldalsky and Rosen attempted in 1935 to argue against the very viability of Quantum Physics. They devised a thought experiment, known as the EPR Paradox. Quantum physics is fundamentally based on probabilities, whereas in Einstein’s view: “God does not play dice”. The EPR trio attempted to show that the Pauli Exclusion Principle was wrong (and thus Quantum Physics had failed in the most basic test of its validity).

“Quantum mechanics is very worthy of regard. But an inner voice tells me that this is not yet the right track. The theory yields much, but it hardly brings us closer to the Old One’s secrets. I, in any case, am convinced that He does not play dice.”

Albert engaged in critical, questioning exchanges with Niels Bohr but eventual failed to succeed in seriously challenging the emerging world that was quantum mechanics and the Copenhagen interpretation.

Experiments determined that the Pauli Exclusion Principle and Quantum Physics were acceptable concepts. Having someone as clever as Albert trying to pull quantum theory apart proved very useful in ensuring its validity. Albert queried and attacked in several directions but his main focus was on something known as quantum non-locality. The key issue here was his challenge to Werner Heisenberg’s uncertainty principle. The idea of probability, and not certainty, in quantum mechanics was, at least initially, an anathema to Albert. Niels Bohr emerged triumphant from their debates over these issues. Albert eventually accepted this.

Albert did have religious views and drew this connection between science and religion: “…science without religion is lame, religion without science is blind.” He explained what he thought was humanities’ inability to fully understand a God through his example of a child in a library. He further said that he believed in the cosmic, big scale, pantheistic God.

p.417

The one claimed by Spinoza. Albert did not believe in a personal God. For him, his God does not have any truck with the affairs of mere humans. He also made a deep, ethical statement of why we are here: “Strange is our situation here on Earth. Each of us comes for a short visit, not knowing why, yet sometimes seeming to divine a purpose. From the standpoint of daily life, however, there is one thing we do know; that man is here for the sake of other men.” and, “For those of us that believe in physics, the distinction between past, present and future is only a stubbornly persistent illusion.” That last chimes with Parmenides thinking, 2,400 years ago.

9. Wolfgang Pauli (1900-1958)

Wolfgang acknowledges a debt he owed to Niels Bohr whom he first met in 1922. Bohr’s lectures encouraged him to think about issues with electrons. As a result, the Pauli Exclusion principle was developed. The Nobel Prize in Physics 1945 was awarded to Wolfgang Pauli: “…for the discovery of the Exclusion Principle, also called the Pauli Principle”. The Pauli Exclusion Principle is as fundamental a characteristic of Quantum Physics as there is.

Wolfgang was also interested in philosophy and studied Plato. His early interest in metaphysics may have had some influence of his work in quantum physics. Perhaps reading of Plato’s cave encouraged him to take a sideways look at the emerging quantum world.

In 1925, Wolfgang Pauli gave physics his exclusion principle as a way to explain the arrangement of electrons in an atom. Electrons orbit the nucleus in an atom. They have four quantum numbers identifying them.

The four quantum number symbols: (n) (ℓ) (m) (s)

Pauli said no two electrons can have the same four numbers. Up to two electrons can be in the same orbital but their spins will be different, one will spin clockwise the other anti-clockwise. The Pauli Exclusion principle is fundamental to the Periodic Table (the arrangement of all elements by their atomic number) because it allows different elements to be distinguished.” Henry Margenau, German physicist and philosopher, said: “Pauli’s Exclusion Principle accounts for atomic structure. Without this structure, every atom would collapse into a positive nucleus surrounded by an unorganized mass of negative charges.”

p.418

Interestingly Henry Margenau was also a believer in God, despite his scientific and philosophical leanings: “God created the universe out of nothing in an act which also brought time into existence. Recent discoveries, such as observations supporting the Big Bang and similar astronomical phenomena, are wholly compatible with this view.”

Atomic chaos would ensue without the order imposed by the Exclusion Principle. It could be said that Pauli’s Exclusion Principle is the base for the order we see in the universe, and for the existence of life.

Pauli did not stop with his amazing insight of the Exclusion Principle. He also worked out the spectrum of hydrogen and came up with the Pauli spin matrices. Further, he speculated about the existence of the super-small neutrino particle. In this he was later proved to be correct. Neutrinos do indeed exist. They pass through our bodies every second, luckily without damaging us.

He was brought up a Catholic but did not embrace this faith. Pauli said the ancient reason for religion was: “At the dawn of religion, all the knowledge of a particular community fitted into a spiritual framework, based largely on religious values and ideas.”

He worked with Carl Jung to develop a slightly mystical theory known as Synchronicity. He explained: “When one analyses the pre-conscious step to concepts, one always finds ideas which consist of “symbolic images.” You may think this does not actually much elucidate. Perhaps that’s the mystery. Clearly, he could use his cleverness to have a joke about other’s non-beliefs.

At the 1927 Solvay Conference saying this about Paul Dirac’s atheism: “If I understand Dirac correctly, his meaning is this: there is no God, and Dirac is his Prophet.”

10. Werner Karl Heisenberg (1901-1976)

With his fellow student Pauli, Heisenberg began to study theoretical physics under Arnold Sommerfeld October 1920.

While Pauli gave quantum mechanics his Exclusion Principle. Werner came up with another ground-breaking theory, ‘The Uncertainty Principle.’

p.419

In essence the Uncertainty Principle says that a particle’s position and its momentum cannot both be known exactly. It is only in the act of observing that these two states become fixed. This is a hard idea to grasp, Einstein did not like it at all, as already explained.

Werner was saying that it’s not possible to always give an electron a position in space at a particular time, nor can its orbit be exactly determined. In effect he indicated we cannot assume that the planetary orbits put forward by Niels Bohr actually exist. Position, speed, etc. should be represented, by abstract mathematical structures called “matrices” not by ordinary numbers.

He said: “We have to remember that what we observe is not nature herself, but nature exposed to our method of questioning.”

Werner was raised and lived as a Lutheran Christian. In 1974 he publisher ‘Scientific and Religious truth saying: “In the history of science, ever since the famous trial of Galileo, it has repeatedly been claimed that scientific truth cannot be reconciled with the religious interpretation of the world. Although I am now convinced that scientific truth is unassailable in its own field, I have never found it possible to dismiss the content of religious thinking……”

11. Paul Adrien Maurice Dirac (1902-1984)

Paul was a Bristolian, having grown up in Bishopston, a district of Bristol. Here, there is now a Dirac Road and nearby a Blue Plaque in Monk Road, where he was born. He attended Bristol University and gained the best first-class degree in Engineering that had ever been awarded by this university.

One wonders whether he could speak proper Bristle, but perhaps his father and mother tempered his accent; neither of these was Bristol born. His father was a Swiss émigré who married a Cornish woman.

p.420

Paul is generally rated to be the second greatest English physicist after Isaac Newton. Stephen Hawking described Paul as “The greatest theoretical physicist since Newton.” Some may mention Maxwell, but he was a Scot.

His brilliance as a scientist was counterbalanced by an inability to engage socially. He was renowned for his silence or, at best, monosyllabic responses in any form of social conversation. He could have been silent because no one could understand him. First there might have been a problem with his Bristolian speech, perhaps which had a touch of Swiss and Cornish. If the listener was capable of interpreting that then they would have to move on to tackle the depth and intricacy of his comments about quantum physics; a double whammy for most listeners.

Paul developed his own version of quantum theory and was awarded the Nobel Prize for Physics and the Order of Merit. His other major awards included both the Planck and Copley Medals. He turned down a knighthood because he did not want to be referred to by his first name. Sir Paul did not gel with him.

Other great scientists recognized his genius. Albert Einstein allegedly kept Dirac’s book at his bedside and drew a link between his extreme cleverness and extreme unsocial ability, by saying about Paul: “This balancing on the dizzying path between genius and madness is awful” The first sounds right, the second a bit unkind; Paul was not mad and he could not have been wholly unsociable, after all he had a wife and two children. Manzi, his eventual wife, had to woo him, not surprisingly.

What she got out of the relationship included the status of marriage to a famous man. He obviously felt their relationship equation was evenly balanced claiming of her: “You have made me human. I shall be able to live happily with you even if I have no more success in my work.” Paul was probably aware his behaviour could be considered a little bizarre. Perhaps he even knew that he was considered to be autistic.

His unique brilliance was recognized at Cambridge University, where he was made the Lucasian Professor of Mathematics, one of the most prestigious academic posts in the world. A previous LP of M was Isaac Newton. Paul held this post for more than three decades. During this time, he lived at 7 Cavendish Avenue, Cambridge. A neighbour, Rosemary McGregor-Wood, lived directly opposite in Cavendish Avenue. She wrote an intriguing letter about Paul and his family explaining that to some Cambridge dons he was a figure of fun, also saying they recognised his genius.

p.421

While at Cambridge Paul established the most general theory of quantum mechanics and discovered the relativistic equation for the electron, which now bears his name; the Dirac Equation.

The remarkable notion of an antiparticle to each particle - i.e. the positron as antiparticle to the electron - stems from his equation. He was the first to develop quantum field theory, which underlies all theoretical work on sub-atomic or “elementary” particles today, work that is fundamental to our understanding of the forces of nature.

12. Erwin Schrodinger (1887-1961)

Austrian physicist Erwin was fun loving, not a dried up, one-track scientist. His 3 daughters were via three different women. Apart from women, he also had some interest in cats, this is suggested by his most famous work. Lot’s of people have heard of ‘Schrodinger’s cat’.

This clever feline not only had nine life’s but also could be either alive or dead, seemingly at the same time! In 1935 Erwin came up with a thought experiment to illustrate another of the weird quantum laws - the superposition of states. Erwin imagined placing a cat in a steel box along with a phial of poison, cyanide. In addition, the box would have a tiny amount of radioactive substance. In one single atom of the radioactive substance decays during a test period this will cause a hammer to trip, inside the box, and smash the phial, so releasing the poison. The cat will be killed. What Erwin said was any observer cannot know whether an atom has decayed so cannot know if the cat is alive or dead. He used this idea to illustrate that by quantum laws, the cat is both alive and dead - superposition. It is only when the box is opened we know the state of the cat. This is sometimes also known as quantum indeterminacy, i.e. there is no single outcome unless observed. The pioneers of DNA, James Watson and Francis Crick made use of this idea. Erwin was born a Lutheran. His views on God were not made with the same clarity as his work in science. Erwin suggested science is a game, with rules made by God: “In the presentation of a scientific problem, the other player is the good Lord. He has not only set the problem but also has devised the rules of the game.”

13. Richard Feynman (1918-1988)

Feynman was a keen populariser of physics through both books and lectures, notably a 1959 talk on top-down nano-technology called ‘There’s Plenty of Room at the Bottom’, and the three volume publication of his undergraduate lectures, The Feynman Lectures on Physics. Feynman also became known through his semi-autobiographical books, ‘Surely You’re Joking, Mr. Feynman!’ and ‘What Do You Care What Other People Think?’. There were books written about him, such as ‘Tuva or Bust!’

He also developed Feynman diagrams, a method which helps in conceptualizing and calculating interactions between particles in space-time, notably the interactions between electrons and their antimatter counterparts, positrons. The Feynman diagram allowed him, and later others, to approach time reversibility and other fundamental processes.

Feynman’s mental picture for these diagrams started with the hard sphere approximation, and the interactions could be thought of as collisions at first. It was not until decades later that physicists thought of analysing the nodes of the Feynman diagrams more closely. Feynman famously painted Feynman diagrams on the exterior of his van.”

14. Schwinger, Julian Seymour (1918-1994)

Born of orthodox, Jewish parents Julian was an American physicist and a child prodigy. He published his first physics paper aged sixteen. Thirty-one years later, he won the 1965 Nobel Prize in physics for basic work in quantum electrodynamics. Quantum electrodynamics is the study of the interaction between electrons and electromagnetic radiation. The improved theory of quantum electrodynamics developed by Schwinger enables scientists to predict accurately the effects of electrically charged particles on each other in a radiation field. Schwinger shared the prize with the American physicist Richard Feynman and the Japanese physicist Sin-Itiro Tomonaga, who independently did similar work in quantum electrodynamics.

15. Murray Gell-Mann (1929 - 2019)

Murray often worked in close proximity to Richard Feynman, another of the greats of sub-atomic physics. They bounced ideas off each other and seemingly engaged in friendly rivalry. Two great minds, in close proximity, must have a real advantage in cross checking the efficacy of each other’s ideas. Murray was so bright he entered Yale at aged 15 and got his Physics PH.D by the time he was 21.

Murray’s big contribution to QM was his theory of quarks, one of the basic and smallest sub-atomic particles. He received the Nobel prize for this in 1964, along with George Zweig who independently proposed the quark model. The name ‘quark’ Murray got from the James Joyce novel, ‘Finnegan’s Wake’. He also proposed a scheme for grouping protons (mesons) and neutrons (baryons) called the Eightfold Way, after Buddha’s Eightfold Path.

p.426

He obviously enjoyed a bit of a joke, slightly doctoring what Newton said, Murray claimed: “If I have seen further than others, it is because I am surrounded by dwarfs.” It is not clear that Murray had any particular religious belief, or emphatic non-belief.

16. Peter Higgs (1929 —)

Peter was born in a district of Newcastle. He got a first class honours degree in - physics and later a PH.D from Kings College, London. This is Peter with the Large Hadron Collider in Geneva.

Peter’s great prediction, the Higgs Boson was discovered by the LHC in 2011. The Higgs Bosun is a particle that with the Higgs field allows other particles to have mass. This essential for all matter in the universe. Independently, François Englert and Robert Brout came up with the same idea as Peter Higgs.

Peter is not a believer in God, but has said religion and science are not incompatible. In his criticism of Richard Dawkins, He expressed some sympathy with religious believers of the non-fundamentalist type: “What Dawkins does too often is to concentrate his attack on fundamentalists. But there are many believers who are just not fundamentalists.” Richard has strongly denied these claims.

The Great Communicators of Classical and Quantum Physics

All the 16 above published and explained their findings in one-way of another so all count as communicators. Naturally though, their papers are heavily science and theoretically based. They are usually aimed at other scientists, or university students of science. Most ordinary people do not easily understand them. You have to be quite a mathematician to fully grasp Maxwell’s equations, or get to the bottom of what monosyllabic Paul Dirac is about. Fortunately, some scientists are not only very able as scientists, but also have a n additional great skill.

p.427

They are renowned for their talent in interpreting and presenting the complexities and relevance of the mysterious sub-atomic world and of the wider universe. These are the people who have, in particular, helped the general public understand and appreciate the achievements of other scientists, or of themselves. These great communicators have had a vital role. They spread and greater understanding of scientific achievements in the field of classical and quantum physics. This helps broaden human knowledge and takes away some of the unscientific myth built up over centuries. It also puts a bit of a hole in religious certainty.

These expert publicists’ work also encourages others to get closely engaged; perhaps to study science subjects at university. It importantly prods the politicians into giving monetary support for expensive projects like CERN, with the Large Hadron Collider, then there’s the £600 million LIGO project.

Classical Physics and some other sciences

There are many other communicators of science, other than those listed above. We owe a debt to all for sharing with us their great understanding. So far, this chapter has included a soupçon of cosmology but its main light has fallen on the very small, the world of particles embraced by quantum mechanics.

Now for quite a brief look at the universe from a larger, classical and energy related, astrophysics/physics perspective. Einstein is of course of both classical, large scale and quantum, tiny scale, but he’s already had his entry, above. Here are some others:

<u>Nicolaus Copernicus</u> (1473-1543) This Polish born polymath swam against the tide when he proposed that it was the sun, not the Earth at the centre of the universe. He set out his findings in 'De Revolutionibus Orbium Coelestium1 ’ (‘On the Revolutions of the Celestial Spheres’) in 1543.

<u>Galileo Galilei</u> (1564 - 1642) A little after Copernicus we have Galileo. He’s already has a mention in the philosophy Chapter 20, no.111. Here we focus on his scientific contribution. He constructed a telescope and used this to confirm the Copernican theory of a sun-centered solar system. His work in fields such physics, astronomy and science methodology made him a central figure of the 17<sup>th</sup> century science revolution. In a sense he’s still with us, as the satellite, Galileo. He now orbits the Earth on behalf of the European Space Agency.

<u>Johannes Kepler</u> (1571 - 1630) Yet another great of the 17<sup>th</sup> century Scientific Revolution. German born Kepler used his astronomical and mathematical expertise, together with the observations of Tycho Brahe, to work out the laws of planetary motion. His work gave detailed support to Galileo and Copernicus’ heliocentric view of the solar system. Little Earth became littler in the vast cosmos, much to the disappointment of Abrahamic religions.

p.436

<u>Christiaan Huygens</u> (1629 - 1695) A Dutchman, what did he do? Well, he discovered Saturn’s moon, Titan; improved the telescope with the invention of a new eyepiece; invented the pendulum clock, which became the most accurate timekeeper for the next 300 years; worked out a formula to measure centripetal force and produced a new theory of light. He also wrote on probability theory. In his honour the Huygens spacecraft landed on Titan in 2005.

<u>Isaac</u> <u>Newton</u> (1642 - 1727) Isaac, born in Lincolnshire, is one of the most famous and influential scientists of all time. This mathematician, astronomer and physicist is well known in the world of fruit too. That apple that fell on his head was a eureka moment; what followed was Newton’s Universal Law of Gravitation and the linking of motion with gravitational attraction. This is still used today as is his equation F=MxA, where force = mass x motion (attraction). He made great, original, contributions in maths and optics too. Isaac put the lid on the 17<sup>th</sup> century’s Scientific Revolution.

<u>Michael Faraday</u> (1791 - 1867) Of a poorish London family Michael left school at 14 to become an apprenticed bookbinder. After attending lectures by the chemist Humphry Davy, Davy took him under his wing. Years later Michael published his ground-breaking work on electromagnetism.

<u>William Hershel</u> (1738 - 1822) He played the oboe in a band and wrote symphonies, but is better known for The Hershel telescope, which he constructed. This was the first of the biggies. and showed more detail of the cosmos than had ever been seen before. William worked with his sister, Caroline, and discovered the planet Uranus, among other discoveries, including infrared. His main interest was studying double stars and nebulae.

God and religion, what did these great scientists and communicators believe?

There is a mixture of believers and non-believers among scientists world-wide. An increasing number of more modern scientist are atheists. The science of classical physics and quantum mechanics, together with cosmology has gone a long way to unravelling the mysteries of the universe. They have not yet told us the full story, but our understanding ever inches forward. The process involves observation, experiment, deduction, equations and lots of numbers. No doubt some religious chiefs are fearful of these weapons of maths destruction!
