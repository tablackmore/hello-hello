Islam

Pages 199–214

This is the third and most recent of the three Abrahamic religions, in terms of the defining date of its holy book, the Qur’an, also called the Koran. After Christianity, Islam is the second largest religion in the world with about 1.8 billion followers in 2018. It’s a monotheistic, Abrahamic religion because it has the same origins as the other two in the prophet Abraham, and recognises Adam. Islam acknowledges other important people referred to in the Jewish Torah and Christian Bible as prophets: Noah; Ishmael; Moses; Joshua and David among others. It sees Jesus as yet another prophet, although a very important one. Islam does not view Jesus as God. The Torah and Bible are considered to be significant Godly guides but only as precursors to the final and definite words of Allah (God). For Muslims, these definitive words are contained in their holy book, the Qur’an.

The word Islam is generally thought to mean submission to the will of Allah and Muslims are the followers of Islam. The Muslims’ holy book, the Qur’an, means revelation. Expanded, this becomes the revelation of Allah’s will.

The Prophet Muhammad and the Qur’an

The Qur’an derives from visions that Muslims believe the prophet Muhammad had over 23 years concluding in 632 CE. These visions were of the archangel Gabriel who orally communicated Allah’s divine messages. The visions began in 610 CE as he meditated in the cave of Hira. At first Muhammad did not fully understand what the visions were. He discussed these with his wife in Mecca.

Muhammad was born in Mecca in about 570 CE to the Quraysh tribe. His father died before he was born and his mother died when he was six. He was raised by his grandfather and then his uncle. At about 25 he married Khadija, a wealthy widow. Muhammad is said to have been a trader and successful merchant. He was troubled by the social problems of his time and sought answers by meditation.

After his first vision, Muhammad began to preach, initially in his hometown of Mecca. His message was not well received by the city’s ruling elite who saw him as a threat. In 622 CE Muhammad and his followers migrated to Medina (then called Yathrib), about 200 miles north of Mecca. This migration, called the Hijra, marks the beginning of the Islamic calendar. In Medina, Muhammad’s following grew and he became both a religious and political leader.

p.200

Muhammad and his followers eventually returned to Mecca in 630 CE and took control of the city. By the time of his death in 632 CE, much of the Arabian Peninsula had converted to Islam. The Qur’an, as it exists today, was compiled by Muhammad’s followers after his death, based on his recitations and notes.

Originally the Qur’an was written in old, classical Arabic, a Semitic language; different to modern Arabic. This has required much needed explanation and some dispute over the true meanings of a number of surahs. Some expert commentators say the early verses of the Qur’an were appropriate to only the earliest Muslim communities. They go on to say that these beginning surahs were cancelled out or abrogated by Muhammad himself in his later text. Yet other Muslim scholars say no abrogation occurred. There is not a 100% consensus in the Islamic community. As a result, various sects have arisen over these and other differences.

Islamic Sects

Islamic sects are several. Sunni (85%) and Shia (10%) are the main two. Their differences go back to disagreement about the Caliphate succession following the death of Muhammad. There are also variants on Sunni and Shia, such as:

Wahhabi, Ibadi, Sufi, Salafi, Ahmadi, Alawite, Kalam, Ash’ari, Maturidi, Jahmis and Hizmet.

Sufism

Sufism is a little different in that it is a form of mystical Islam which goes back to the 8th century. Some claim it is not exclusively Islamic. There are thought to be millions of Muslim Sufi today, although they can be at base Sunni or Shia Muslims. Sufi believe in recitation and chanting to achieve a form of ecstasy so that the word of God is more easily assimilated. They think there is a network of saints who act as teachers to pass on divine knowledge to Sufi followers. Sufi have questioned the rituals and strict theology of orthodox Islam. This may explain why some Muslims believe Sufi not to be truly Islamic. Sufi followers have even been killed because of this. Saudi Arabia has prohibited Sufi books and punished those caught practicing Sufism. Some Muslim influencers’ dislike of Sufism may have led to the rise of Wahhabi and Salafi Islamic sects. These two are similar.

Wahhabi and Salafi

p.201

Wahhabi and Salafi are more rigid and traditional than other sects, especially Sufism, and can advocate jihadi (holy war) violence. Wahhabism has a powerful base in Saudi Arabia and has been exported from there in recent times to other countries by use of Saudis’ vast oil-based wealth.

Muslim Clergy, or not

Islam is unlike Christianity in that it does not have clergy and a church. This can make it difficult to find an authoritative doctrinal interpretation. It also means it is awkward for Governments who may want to discuss issues, especially connected to terrorism. Islam has people called Imams, but these are not the equivalent of Priests or Vicars. They are simply teachers and Mosque leaders at prayer. There are also Mullahs, an honorific title given to someone who acts as a teacher and judge with expertise in Muslim law, more of a Professor of religion than a priest. Ulama are a type of Mullah who are highly trained and acknowledged interpreters of the Qur’an. An Ayatollah is a high-ranking Iranian Shia Muslim. He is an expert in Islamic law and the Qur’an.

Women in Islam

Historically, Muslim women have not been treated as men’s equals. Some Islamic scholars have justified this by their interpretation of the Qur’an and example from the life of Muhammad. He had an estimated 15 wives and 4 concubines in his lifetime. The Qur’an allows Muslim men to have up to 4 wives, but Muslims can also make use of slaves, from conquered non-believers. The Qur’an has been used to justify this practice in recent times. Isis, was a proponent of sex slavery. This organisation was a violent, extreme Muslim movement that gained territory by force of arms in parts of Syria and Iraq from 2014 before its final defeat in April 2019. It was classified as a terrorist organisation by the United Nations. Isis treated thousands of Yazidi and Iraqi women as sex slaves. Yazidi and other captured men were routinely butchered. Sometimes this was done by beheading, which was deliberately caught on camera.

Traditional Islamic Rules for Women

Traditionally, there are a number of Islamic rules and customs that treat women differently from men:

  • Men may divorce their wives without cause and retain sole custody rights over children who have reached a certain age;

Muslim societies have nearly always been exclusively patriarchal and male dominated. It’s extremely rare for women to be allowed to reach any position of prominence in the 1400 years of Islam, i.e. since Mohammed. Benazir Bhutto bucked the trend by becoming Prime Minister of Pakistan, in 1983. A suicide bomber later assassinated her. Some sources say this was Taliban inspired. Tansu Ciller became Turkey’s Prime Minister in 1993. This was a period of change following Kemal Ataturk, the great reformer. He was a transformational Turkish leader following the collapse of the Ottoman Empire. President Ataturk relaxed some strict Islamic cultural rules and gave women equal civil and political rights.

Progress for Women

In many areas of employment and personal freedom Muslim women have been severely constrained in most Islamic societies. Saudi Arabia only allowed women to drive cars for the first time in 2018. Gaining higher education has been a problem for girls in most Muslim dominated countries. Sometimes, any form of education for girls has been at issue. Famously, Malala Yousafzai went against the Taliban’s ban on girl’s education in Afghanistan. As a consequence, she was shot in the head in 2013. Later she received the Nobel Peace Prize for championing the right of Muslim girls to receive an education. Malala was fortunate to be strongly supported by her father. He was determined to see his daughter educated, free to choose her own way and not be forced into marriage at a very young age.

Honour Killings

It can be very dangerous for Muslim women to refuse an arranged marriage, or to leave one. In the UK, it’s estimated that up to 12 Muslim women a year were killed in so-called ‘honour’ killings in the first decade of the 21st century. Usually the murderer is the father, and/or other male relative. This is claimed to be mostly a cultural issue, not an Islamic one. Some Muslims clearly think this action is allowable within their religion. Questions could be asked about this such as: How is it honourable to take away a daughter/relative’s choice and brutally kill her, and where in the Qur’an is this practice approved? Are Qur’an 2:224 and 4:35 used as justification?

In the twenty-first century the issue of women’s involvement in leading the salat - prayers, has been debated but there is a long way to go here. Sharia Law and Modern Society

Most discussion and dispute concerning women arises over Sharia laws to do with marriage and divorce. Sharia law presents another and a more fundamental problem: what is its role in secular societies? Many such societies, mostly western, have large Muslim populations, but they also have a secular based legal system. These legal systems are likely to have been honed over centuries and at base have international commonalities and acceptance. All this debate has resulted in the slow emergence of a form of Islamic feminism.

LGBT and Islam

In early 2019 the Sultan of Brunei fully adopted the draconian version of Sharia law. This meant those found guilty of gay sex would be stoned to death. The penal code also allows the removal of a hand and foot for those guilty of theft. LGBT people are subject to discrimination and potential maltreatment in many Muslim dominated countries. This is the complete antithesis of the more liberal views and laws encouraged by the United Nations. In September 2015, 12 United Nations organisations issued a joint statement that called for an end to violence and discrimination against lesbian, gay, bisexual, transgender and intersex people. It asked its member states to repeal any discriminatory laws that they had.

The attempt to teach children something about LGBT in some areas with large Muslim population in England had caused public mass protests and even threats against teachers. In April 2019, the English Education Secretary, Damian Hinds, wrote to all school heads saying the policy of educating children about LGBT was to help children leave school “prepared for life in modern, diverse Britain.” He left it to schools to consult locally and decide age-appropriate level for this type of teaching. The Muslim Council of Britain has moved a little to give women a more equal say. It recognised that women must be given a greater role in Mosques by setting up the ‘Women in Mosques Development Programme’ in 2019. They also bravely elected their first women leader, Zara Mohammed in January 2021.

As a religion Islam has a unique claim to prominence in the early years of the 21st century. In recent times it is by far and away the religion that most inspires both individual and group terrorists’ attacks in its name. Every other main-stream religion does not get anywhere near Islam in this regard. Even if the death and injury caused by all other religious extremists are added together, Islam still tops the pile by a very big margin.

Islam and Terrorism in Great Britain

Let’s take a look at what impact and reaction there has been to Islamic terrorist action in one country, Great Britain. There are around 2.8 million Muslims in Great Britain, representing about 4% of the population. Many were born in GB. Most have originated from countries of the old British Empire.

In 2017 Great Britain suffered more Islamic inspired terrorist attacks than any other country in Europe. In three attacks 35 people were killed. These were part of the near 90 deaths from Islamic terrorism since 2005 up to 2018.

Three Major Attacks

Three of the biggest attacks in this 13-year period were:

1. July 2005, London Underground and bus attacks: 50 people killed, hundreds injured. Four people aged 30 to 18 carried the attacks using rucksacks full of explosives. Their leader, Mohammed Khan, posted a video before the attacks praising Al-Qaeda and founder Osama Bin Laden.

2. March 2017, Westminster Bridge attack: Lone wolf Khalid Masood, a 52-year-old Muslim convert, drove a van deliberately at people and then used a knife to kill a policeman. Three people were killed and 29 injured. Masood was born Adrian Elms. He was said to be intelligent and popular at school but as an adult, he bitterly complained to his mother about his childhood. He had life chances, was married with two children and had several jobs, one as a teacher of English to foreign students. Throughout his life he was prone to rage and violence. This led to several convictions. He became a Muslim convert and was radicalised, probably by members of the Al Muhajiroun terror group, when serving a term in prison.

3. May 2017, Manchester Arena suicide bombing: 23 people died, including an 8-year-old girl, and 800 were injured. Salman Abedi, a 22-year-old Muslim, carried out the attack. Abedi was born in Manchester. His parents were highly religious, deeply conservative Muslim refugees from Libya. They had been members of the Libyan Islamic Fighting Group. The UN Security Council said this group had links to Al-Qaeda. Abedi had dropped out of Salford University and did not seem to have joined clubs or organisations. Abedi had made trips back to Libya and is thought to have gone to Syria. His backpack bomb was quite sophisticated. He may have come into contact with Abd Azzouz, an expert bomb maker. Azzouz lived near Abedi’s family in Manchester and was accused of running an Al-Qaeda network. Abedi’s sister, Jamona, said she thought Abedi had become angry at the American bombing of Syria and wanted revenge. Isis claimed that the Manchester bombing had been carried out by one of its soldiers. An eleven-year-old Muslim girl, Sara Hussein, spoke out movingly against this attack. She said that she wished she had a time machine so she could go back and explain to Abedi how wrong he was. In August 2020 Abedi’s brother, Hashem, was jailed for 55 years for his part in the bombing.

The conference made these statements:

  • WHEREAS Muslims strongly believe, as emphasised by the Qur’an, that Islam is a religion of peace (Q2:208), that Allah (God) invites to peace (Q10:25) and that Prophet Muhammad (peace be upon him) was sent by God as a messenger of peace and mercy to the world and to all humanity (Q21:107); and

Q2:256 clearly states that there is no compulsion in religion.”

Education is a key factor in encouraging an integrated society. All pupils should be taught in non-religious schools. Final Thoughts

Islam, like Judaism and Christianity, is one of the great Abrahamic faiths that has shaped world history and continues to influence billions of people today. It is a religion with a rich intellectual tradition, beautiful art and architecture, and has contributed significantly to human knowledge in fields such as mathematics, astronomy, medicine, and philosophy during its golden age.

However, like all religions, Islam faces challenges in the modern world. The tension between traditional interpretations and contemporary values, particularly regarding women’s rights, LGBT rights, and secular governance, remains a significant issue. The problem of violence carried out in Islam’s name, whether through terrorism or sectarian conflict, is undeniable and requires honest examination and action from both Muslim communities and wider society.

The vast majority of Muslims worldwide are peaceful people who wish to live in harmony with others. The challenge for the 21st century is how to preserve religious and cultural identity while embracing universal human rights, how to maintain faith traditions while accepting secular pluralism, and how to build bridges of understanding in an increasingly interconnected world.

Integration, education, mutual respect, and open dialogue offer the best path forward. Muslims should be able to practice their faith freely while also accepting the principles of democracy, equality, and human rights. Non-Muslims need to combat prejudice and Islamophobia while also being willing to have honest conversations about difficult issues. Only through such mutual effort can we build societies where people of all faiths and none can live together in peace and prosperity.
