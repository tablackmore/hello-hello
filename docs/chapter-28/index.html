Chapter 28

Conclusions

Pages 463–470

We’ve seen how the notion of spirits, Gods, or finally the one God, has permeated every sphere and every period of Modern Humans’ existence. As explained in earlier chapters, there’s compelling reasons for this: the safety deposit box; evolutionary advantage; comfort blanket; clubbiness; promise of eternal life in paradise; the fear of hell, or equivalent; the attempted COTHAM six proofs and PSR - Principle of Sufficient Reason; the cajoling and threats made by clerics or government leaders, and the simple, but so powerful, “acceptance” - belief via location. Added to this is the proven predilection of children towards credence in the supernatural. It’s no wonder we’ve had a myriad of religions and a multitude of Gods and Goddesses, with the Abrahamic God now firmly in the lead

Many philosophers, scientists, theologians, artists, fiction writers as well as “ordinary” people have pondered the issue: is there really a God(s), and if not what else? Despite huge efforts by lots of extremely clever individuals, proof of an omnipotent being’s existence remains elusive. There seems to be no clear evidence. Some would say there is no evidence at all, particular having in mind the relentless march of scientific discoveries. The origin of the universe, or universes, is not yet fully known, but is less of a mystery now than in the past. Much of what was thought to be due to “the will of God” by previous generations is now explainable in scientific terms. Darwin’s uncovering of evolution put a spanner in Godly creationism. It is increasingly harder for religious leaders to point to the guiding hand of God in forming our universe and shaping man’s part in it. Cosmologists and theoretical physicists have been able to offer theories to support the natural development of the universe, from the Big Bang onwards. We now know that both mankind and the planet we live on are tiny parts of an enormous universal whole, and our universe may be just one of countless others. Despite these great strides forward, science, same as religion, has not yet come up with a final, provable answer to the origin of everything.

The deepest sin against the human mind is to believe things without evidence.

Aldous Huxley, author of Brave New World

Those who do think there is a God often do so because they are encouraged to believe in some form of ancient religious texts. These have been written exclusively by men. It’s curious that half of Modern Humans - women - were not involved.

p.464

The male authors have long since died. They cannot be cross-examined about either the evidence or logic base for their written creeds. What it boils down to is that God has adherents, usually goodly people, who have faith, belief, or simple unquestioning acceptance. They may have one or all of these but they do not have incontrovertible proof. There is not one shred of hard evidence for any type of God. This is despite the efforts made over thousands of years by theologians and various clerics.

Research shows that most philosophers and many scientists do not see any evidence or logic to show there is a God. Paul Dirac and Carl Sagan, among others, are of this view. These are atheists. Some are just doubtful - agnostics. Philosophers such as Soren Kierkegaard and William James claim that there is a limit to use of reason. They suggest that it is right to employ the leap of faith when that limit is reached. Is this a leap into the light or into the dark?

Some fence sitters are dubious about their religion and doubt God, but hope that He exists. The prospect of eternal bliss, or re-incarnation after Earthly death, is alluring, and the clubbiness engendered from embracing a religion is comforting. For these people, hedging one’s bet seems a good idea - just as Blaise Pascal argued. It’s reasonable to conjecture that there are many such people. These are the half-hearted supporters of one or other religion.

The materialistic, hard-headed, modern world has probably thrown up a larger corps of non-believers than at any time in recorded history. People may not believe because they are too tied up in the process of living. They have no faith because they do not need it to pursue and enjoy their lives. Other people research the issue and then decide they cannot believe in a religion or God. To change perceptions up front blatant proof is needed that He exists. This simply is not to be found. For those that seriously ponder this issue all that can be done is take one of three approaches, either have blind faith in some type of religion, hold a form of atheistic non-belief or be a ‘don’t know’. The latter two types are influenced by the lack of evidence. The former, the religious and spiritual people, may ignore the lack of hard evidence and have faith in the teachings of a holy book. They may justify the use of faith, just as have Kierkegaard and James. Religious people believe without supporting proof, but similarly atheists do not have absolute evidence for their dis-belief.

The fact is that no religion, or atheism can claim certainty for what they believe or don’t believe. We just don’t yet know all the answers, yet.

p.465

The ‘don’t knows’ - the agnostics - sit between the religious and the atheists. Agnostics are convinced that at present we cannot be certain sure one way or the other. They might speculate on the possibilities that lead to our universe and Modern Humans.

So, what is it that can be conjectured? What are the possibilities that could explain the existence of the cosmos and us – aside from a Poplawski’s hyper black hole? No one knows for sure, despite many religious devotees think they do - God started it and made us. Is a God, of some sort involved? Interestingly, author Karen Armstrong touched on this in her detailed, well researched book “A History of God”. Karen mentioned that philosopher A.J. Ayer claimed the idea of God only had meaning if it could be either falsified or verified. She went on: “Similarly a traditional believer in the ‘Old Man in the Sky’ is not making a meaningless statement when he says: ‘I believe in God’, since after death we should be able to find out whether or not this is true.” Here Karen seems to think we will know whether or not God exists, after we die. However, that assumes we will have some sort of conscious afterlife - a very big assumption, completely unproven. If, as the ancient Greek philosopher Epicurus says, death is the end of all sensation, then after death we won’t exist and will be incapable of having a clue about anything.

At the moment we can only guess at original cause, death won’t tell us the answer, probably. What are the possibilities in the frame to explain the origin of our universe? Here’s seven possible answers:

  1. Scientific Rationalism: There is no God. The universe and everything in it, including modern humans, has a scientific explanation, based on eternal mathematics, if only we can find it. The Great Theory of Everything needs to be cracked for this to be proved;

  2. Computer Game: Our whole universe, including us, Modern Humans, operate within a computer programme. We are but bits and bytes, 0 and 1, a little like the film Tron. Everything is based on numbers. Pythagoras, Max Tegmark and Roger Penrose might feel vindicated. We have no reality; our world is virtual and so are we. Super intelligent beings from somewhere in the multiverse are the programmers.

    Imagine another universe 10 billion years older than ours. It’s had time for a race of super-beings to evolve. These beings live for 500,000 years. A couple of bright children, just 50,00 and 55,000 years old, have a school exam project. They must design a new universe, using computer binary code.

p.466

Their mother reviews their work: “It’s not bad, Pascal and Georgia, there’s some quite clever constructions. I like the mountains, and that blue is a good colour for those seas. Interesting that you’ve developed carbon-based lifeforms, but you’ve cheated with the timescale. You simply cannot have galaxies, that planet Earth and those human beings all arriving on the scene in their timeframe of just 6,000 years. Now, go back to your desks and work out a proper evolutionary timescale, consistent with the eventual emergence of these humans.” “Awhhh mum” groaned Pascal, the youngest by 5,000 years. Grumbling, Pascal and Georgia got back to their desks. Georgia, who knows their mother, says: “We simply have to change the timing Pascal, although it’s going to take a lot more work. Pick a big number.” Pascal sullenly complied, “14 billion years.” Georgia encouragingly responded “That’s a reasonable timeframe, but I bet mum will say we cannot have nice round numbers. Let’s make it 13.8 billion and a bit years.”

Together, Pascal and Georgia write an enormously complicated and lengthy program to construct the evolutionary history of our universe and us. But like all complex computer programmes there are some bugs Even so, they come first in their school’s end of year exam. Pascal and Georgia’s programme is run in full, using time-loops so that the humans can be seen to be on the scene.

Their teacher praises their efforts, but adds some advice for next time: “Those intelligent sapiens creatures were fascinating; the antics they get up to - completely unbelievable! It might have been better though to have left them in the trees, and your programme has a couple of bugs that need fixing.” These bugs are to be found in the seemingly nonsensical world of quantum mechanics where a single particle might have a link to another, anywhere in space - entanglement, and where retro-causality may be found. We await the super-beings, the nerdiest of nerdy programmers, to release an update to their cosmic programme, with a fix for these obvious bugs. Of course, they might update the whole programme and put us in the trash can, or leave us in the trees;

  1. Alternate Universe Super Beings: We and our universe are the constructs of vastly superior intelligences who derive from an alternate and much older universe. Unlike the virtual images of possibility 2, this time we have a solidity, and in that sense, are real;
p.467
  1. Deism: There is a God, but this is a hands-off deist God. HE sets the universe off with a bang then sits back and lets it all hang out. HE does not interfere with anything that happens subsequently. There’s only a small variance here with possibility 3. The Deist God here in 4 is just a single entity and the very superior intelligences of 3. are many. This God is not known and does not want to be known. HE is amused by the efforts of humans to construct various deities that are nothing like Him. Perhaps He will allow a certain far-future, very advanced MH in both abilities and attributes, to accurately unravel His existence;
  2. Abrahamic God: The Bible, Torah and Qu’ran have it right, there is a God of Abraham. All the many contradictions and seemingly intractable problems from any literal interpretation of the various holy scriptures, and the miss-exercise of religion throughout the ages, can somehow be explained and justified. The problem of evil, Satan’s existence, and how freewill is possible can all be beautifully rationalised with unassailable logic. At present the feeble minds of human beings cannot wholly comprehend all this, nor the meaning and will of the supreme being - just as Einstein, with his child in the Library, and Plato’s cave have hinted at;
  3. One Universal Consciousness: We are contiguous with the whole universe. There is a general, vast gestaltic consciousness. This cosmic Gaia encompasses not just the totality of the material world, but the unseen, unknown, conscious, and unconscious one too. This is broadly the proposal of philosophers, Parmenides and Baruch Spinoza and is close to Emmerson’s pantheist view. It is obliquely and poetically put by Wordsworth, Coleridge and Tennyson;
  4. H.A.E. (Human Accelerated Evolution): Mankind’s self-enhanced, multi-stepped evolution, AI++, eventually produces a race of super-intelligent beings who, after a very long time, become unified as one single, non-corporeal entity. This homo super-superior entity is able to time travel. It goes back 6 million years in time; to the period on Earth just before mankind’s bipedal ancestors were grounded. It tweaks the genetic makeup of tree living apes in Africa. These apes then leave their trees to walk upright on the ground…and so on. This can be seen as a case of the universe leading to God, instead of the usual, religious based claim that it was God who came first. Our God, of sorts, is what the homo super-superior would be seen as.
p.468

Of the seven possibilities above, two of them involve a being or beings that we would consider to be a “standard” God, i.e. numbers 4 and 5. Of the rest, four others involve beings with Godlike powers. Only one, number 1, proposes that there is no God, of any variety, concerned with the universe’s existence, but one may very well win over the six. None of the answers suggest how the very first of anything came into being. They’re all about the possible origin of our universe, or us. But eventually something has to begin from nothing. You cannot have “nothing” according to Parmenides and Rene Descartes, but Berkeley thinks it’s all in the mind and Winnie the Pooh finds “nothing” easy.

And from the seven possibilities above the answer is… I don’t know. Do you? Douglas Adams, in his book ‘The Hitchhiker’s Guide to the Galaxy’ attempts to come up with the ‘Answer to the Ultimate Question of Life, The Universe, and Everything’. Hyper-intelligent mice used the greatest of computers, Deep Thought, to solve this ultimate question. After 7½ million years of convoluted computation Deep Thought finally came up with an answer, 42. At the present stage of human knowledge, 42 is as good a solution as any other.

Deep Thought’s answer of 42, does not tell us what came before 42. The super-duper computer does not say how anything, anywhere first came into being, just as the other seven possibles do not. How did a God, a singularity, or anything at all, first come into being? If you know the answer to this one, and can offer proof, you are unique and will have a great TV and media career explaining to everyone.

If you put the question another way though, the answer may be worked out very exactly. Here’s the revamped question: “I am the beginning of everything, the end of time and space, the beginning of every end, the end of every place. What am I?” Can you answer? It’s really easy at first.

Significance of seven

The total of 7 possibilities - does this number have any significance? Well, for quite a few people it does. God, apparently, took it easy on day 7, after 6 days of hard word setting up Earth, Adam and much else. A week consists of 7 days. Deep Thought’s answer, 42, = 6x7. The Jewish Menorah most often has 7 candles. Snow White had a romp with seven dwarfs. T. E. Lawrence’s autobiography was ‘The Seven Pillars of Wisdom’, not six or eight.

p.469

Then there’s that traditional English nursery rhyme:

As I was going to St. Ives, I met a man with seven wives, Each wife had seven sacks, Each sack had seven cats, Each cat had seven kits: Kits, cats, sacks, and wives, How many were there going to St. Ives?

None is the answer. The wives got fed up when they reached Newquay, where they met some jolly sailors. Cats, kits, husband and drawers were let loose.

An English phrase indicating extreme bliss is ‘seventh heaven’. Seven is the highest Jannah, or heaven, in Islam and there are seven hells. Hinduism gives us the seven great gurus (Rishis). That ancient ascetic monk, Evagrius, came up with the seven deadly sins. St Ambrose and others countered with the seven heavenly virtues. These were formed by the symbolic addition of the 3 theological virtues (faith, hope and charity) with the 4 cardinal ones (prudence, justice, temperance and fortitude). The 7 sins and 7 virtues are a form of dualism, with some similarity to the far-eastern idea of Yin and Yang.

In Chinese culture 7 represents the combination of the opposite forces of Yin and Yang (concept of interconnected dualism) together with the 5 elements (Water, Fire, Wood, Metal and Earth). Confucianism views this mixture as representative of harmony. Chinese Taoism sees it as connected with kindness and beauty.

All very interesting, but with the 7 possibilities for our universe, are they just by coincidence, 7, or maybe by convenience and coincidence?

And finally, that book title, Hello, hello, hello

Dixon of Dock Green was a popular BBC TV soap. It ran for more than 429 episodes from 1955 to 1976. In the days of Dixon, bobbies (policemen) pounded the beat, i.e. they walked the streets of their patch. Dixon, a bobby, was a vital community character, gently putting right all the wrongs in his neighbourhood.

In a parallel universe Dixon was a real figure, this time he came from the west country of England, rather than London. On one occasion, while plodding his Bristol beat, he saw a discarded copy of “Hello”, a celebrity news magazine. “Ello, 'ello, 'ello” said Dixon, as he bent down to pick it up - h’s were an anathema to him - “what have we 'ear.” His spelling wasn’t too hot either.

Little did he know, but the dark dot in the top right corner of the magazine was a super-microdot. It contained a detailed message from God explaining how He had kick-started the universe. Also answered by the dot were man’s many questions about why we are here, what’s it all about, and who will win the World Cup.

Gabriel had tried to persuade God to use social media rather than “Hello” to give his explanatory message to mankind, but God said: “Well, it’s a magazine telling stories about celebrities and I’m the greatest story and the greatest celebrity ever. The super-microdot will let me fit everything in, otherwise the magazine would run to 25,000 pages, then poor Dixon wouldn’t be able to pick it up. Some clever-clogs is bound to notice the dot and will tell all the other humans.”

You can keep a look out for that magazine, but I fear you won’t find it. We are in the wrong universe. Hello and goodbye.
