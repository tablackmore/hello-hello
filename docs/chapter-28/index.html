Yin and Yang

In Chinese culture, the symbol represents the combination of the opposite forces of Yin and Yang (concept of interconnected dualism shown as complementary forces in the universe, as connected with, yet in contrast to, each other).

Yin is dark, feminine, mysterious, hidden, and passive. Yang is light, masculine, open, obvious, and active. They are complementary opposites that interact within a greater whole, as part of a dynamic system. Everything has both Yin and Yang aspects (for example, shadow cannot exist without light). Either of the two major aspects may manifest more strongly in a particular object, depending on the criterion of the observation. The Yin Yang (i.e. taijitu symbol) shows a balance between two opposites with a portion of the opposite element in each section.

p.464

In Taoist cosmology, the duality of Yin and Yang symbolizes the interplay of opposing forces that are in constant flux and transformation, yet interconnected and interdependent in the natural world. The concept lies at the origins of many branches of classical Chinese science and philosophy, as well as being a primary guiding principle of traditional Chinese medicine, and a central principle of different forms of Chinese martial arts and exercise, such as baguazhang, taijiquan (t’ai chi), and qigong (Chi Kung), as well as appearing in the pages of the I Ching.

p.465

The notion of duality can be found in many areas of classical Chinese science, philosophy, and humanities. The earliest Chinese characters for Yin and Yang are found in inscriptions made on “oracle bones” (skeletal remains used in ancient Chinese divination practices dating from the second millennium BCE). In these inscriptions, Yin and Yang simply mean weather conditions and the movement of the sun. Over time the two characters came to mean “the shady side” and “the sunny side” (of a hill). These meanings are still used today: mountains in China will often have the character for Yang on south-facing slopes (which are sunny) and the character for Yin on the north-facing slopes (which are in the shade).

p.466

In the cosmology pertaining to Yin and Yang, the material energy which this universe was created from is known as qi. It is believed that the organization of qi in this cosmology of Yin and Yang has formed many things. Included among these forms are humans. Many natural dualities (such as light and dark, fire and water, expanding and contracting) are thought of as physical manifestations of the duality symbolized by Yin and Yang. This duality lies at the origins of many branches of classical Chinese science and philosophy, as well as being a primary guiding principle of traditional Chinese medicine, and a central principle of different forms of Chinese martial arts and exercise, such as baguazhang, taijiquan (t’ai chi ch’uan), and qigong (Chi Kung), as well as appearing in the pages of the I Ching.

p.467

Duality is present in many aspects of life, and the Yin Yang symbol illustrates the interplay of opposites. In music and language, there are also dualities—the interplay of different sounds, tones, rhythms, and patterns. The concept of Yin and Yang can be applied to understand these linguistic and musical phenomena as well.

The Sound of “Seven”

p.469

Now let’s take a little journey through some languages and explore the significance of numbers and patterns.

Traditional English Nursery Rhyme

As I was going to St Ives, I met a man with seven wives, Each wife had seven sacks, Each sack had seven cats, Each cat had seven kits: How many were going to St Ives?

An English phrase indicating extreme bliss is “seventh heaven.” Seven is the highest level of heaven in Islam, and there are many references to seven in religious and spiritual traditions. There are the seven deadly sins. St. Ambrose and others identified these, which included the 4 cardinal ones along with pride and vanity. These concepts were formed with some similarity to the traditional ideas found in Eastern philosophy, including concepts related to Yin and Yang.

All very interesting, but what are the possibilities for our universe—are they just coincidence, or maybe correspondence and connection?

“Hello, Hello”

And finally, that book title: Hello, Hello…

Dixon of Dock Green was a popular 1947 TV show that ran for more than 429 episodes, from 1955 to 1976. Dixon pounded the beat—he was a bobby (British police officer). The program was set in the United Kingdom. In the streets of his patch, Dixon, a bobby, was a straightforward neighborhood policeman.

Jack Warner was a well-known British actor. In a small universe, Dixon was a real force of order, bringing stability and justice to his community. Dixon came from the west country of England, rather than London. On one occasion, he came across someone carrying a copy of a magazine while planning to commit a burglary. As he bent down to pick it up, Dixon said, as he examined it—it was a magazine to him—“what have we got?” His spelling wasn’t too hot either.

Little did he know, but the article in the top right corner of the magazine was about cosmic questions. Also, it promised an article explaining how the universe was formed, addressing the big questions about why we are here, what’s it all about, and who will win the World Cup.

Gabriel had tried to persuade God to use social media rather than a magazine telling stories about celebrities, and had tried to persuade God to use social media rather than “Hello” to communicate the “explanatory” message to humankind. But, as the saying goes, God works in mysterious ways. The super-microfiche will let me fit everything into 25,000 pages, then poor Dixon—though he is bound to notice the magazine—wouldn’t be able to read it all in his lifetime.

You can keep a look out for that magazine, but I fear you won’t find it. We are in the young universe, my friends.
