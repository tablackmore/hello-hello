Chapter 25

The Universe, history, size and content

Pages 437–454

Back then, 13.8 billion years ago, it was a singular beginning, in both senses. The singularity that went bang was odd, in the extreme, that sort of singular. We are not sure whether it was the only one, so no claim to this dictionary version of singular. And, as the original, cosmological meaning, the singularity was so dense it’s well short of the mark to call it thick. It did, though, very quickly become as thick as a plank, becoming quite a few light years thick in super-quick Planck time.

Planck Epoch

The very, very early universe is known as the Planck Epoch, after the German, Max Planck with his genius at working out impossible numbers. He calculated a time of 10<sup>-43</sup> seconds after the Big Bang during which the laws of physics did not yet apply. That time is 0.0 of a second with another 42 ‘0s’ before 1. Stuff was still sorting itself out during this miniscule time and, it’s thought, the four fundamental forces were combined into one. Luckily for us, the universe did begin to sort itself out. The laws of physics cranked into life. Fundamental particles, full of community spirit, started happily joining up to form those first atoms of hydrogen, then helium. Atoms started bumping into each other, and with the help of a weak gravitational force, began to clump together into larger and larger masses. It took about 100 million years for this process to result in the first stars. Early stars got together to create the first galaxy, 200 million years after the BB.

Some original particles, swarming around just after the BB, were aggressive and not at all community minded. These, when meeting similarly bolshy anti-matter particles, wiped each other out in their own mini Big Bang. But there were enough kind and reasonable particles left to allow our universe to grow.

Cosmic Microwave Background

This ancient radiation has, amazingly been detected. It is a leftover of the Big Bang, originating about 380,000 years after the beginning of our Universe, that’s more than 13 billion years ago. Here it is, the Universe long before us, before dinosaurs, before Earth, the Sun and even before the Milky Way:

p.438

In 1964 two radio astronomers, Robert Wilson and Arno Penzias came across some continous noise in a huge microwave receiver. This proved to be the evidence of our early Universe, the Cosmic Microwave Background. Both were recognised with the Nobel Prize for this breakthrough in our understanding of the Universe’s origin. Some fifty years later, the European Space Agency’s Planck mission probe collected light in very fine detail. This allowed the CMB map to be made.

And in the End

We have a great deal of evidence to suggest how the universe began and developed, but we know much less about how it might end. The type of ending and its timing is uncertain. It’s so uncertain as to be not in any way certain, Physicists and astronomers give very broad ball park figures of the time to the end, anywhere between 5 billion to 1 trillion years from now. The manner of demise is also not known. Guesses include: the big freeze - all energy is used up; big rip - everything is pulled asunder; big crunch - everything collapses; cosmic bounce - allied to the big crunch. This says after the crunch there will be another Big Bang = a reoccurring universe.

That’s what is guessed about the universe’s eventual demise, now more about its beginning.

Supernova

Those early atoms, hydrogen and helium, were a bit light on mass, but en-masse in stars they got very hot. There were some very big stars in the early universe who did not last too long. Forces of gravity eventually overcame the very gradually cooling star as it ran out of fuel. The star’s weakening propellent force and pressure from nuclear energy, pushing outwards, was vanquished by gravity. In an almighty collapse some giant stars crushed so violently and quickly - in just a few seconds - they exploded. This was a supernova.

p.439

Supernova, the Key to Life

These, the biggest explosive events in our universe, were vital to the eventual formation of life. The immense pressures and incredible heat generated, reaching billions of degrees kelvin, was transformational on the atom front. Many of those simple atoms of hydrogen and helium in the exploding star had their electrons and nuclei knocked about a lot, first by the enormous pressures created by gravity then by the explosive force and heat. Hydrogen and helium re-formed into new configurations of heavier atoms. Supernova gave birth to these heavier, more complex atoms of carbon, oxygen and iron, among others. Some of these make up human bodies, plants, animals and Earth itself. We Modern Humans need carbon and oxygen. Our bodies have 18% is of the former with a huge 65% of oxygen. Iron is included in a small, but vital quantity. There’s about 4 grams of iron in adults.

Supernova, ergo, are essential to all life and have been a feature of our universe throughout nearly all of its history. On average, in our Milky Way galaxy, there’s one supernova every 100 years.

Giant Early Stars

The very earliest stars were super-massive; known as Population 111 stars. Their size meant they were more unstable than most of today’s stars, who are much smaller. Small is a relative term in respect of stars. Our Sun is an average size star at 864,000 miles in diameter, more than 100 times the diameter of Earth. On this comparison our Sun seems huge, but it’s quite minuscule compared to the early stars, some P3 were more akin to Betelgeuse in size, big B is 600 million miles in diameter. P3’s could be even bigger than B.

The early, giant stars did not last long, sometimes just a few hundred thousand years, maybe up to a few million years.

p.440

When they exploded, in a supernova, they spewed out the vital heavier elements. Meteors, asteroids and planets picked up these essential elements. All this eventually led to Modern Humans, us. Thank you, supernova.

How Big is the Universe?

This universe of ours is mightily big. Some people will say: “Ah, I can work out the size of the universe because I know its age and that man Einstein said nothing can travel faster than the speed of light, so its diameter has to be 2 x 13.8 billion light years = 27.4 billion light years.” This is an understandable calculation, but wrong. To the edge of the observable universe is about 46.5 billion light years, or it’s 93 billion light years in diameter. Expansion of the fabric of space is the reason for this. Space expansion is not governed by the limitation of the speed of light, this rules how fast matter can move. Space simply says “No matter, I’m going to ignore Einstein’s speed limitation.” Expansion means that the universe is continually getting bigger. By the time you read this it might be an extra 0.1 billion light years larger, or more.

Expanding Universe

We have that great American astronomer, cat lover, Cambridge University student, and Nobel Prize winner Edwin Hubble to thank for the proof that the universe is expanding. In 1924 he used the 100-inch telescope on Mount Wilson to measure the distance of galaxies. Edwin identified certain stable stars that could be used as a sort of base line, called standard candles, or Cepheid variables (originally discovered by a woman astronomer, Henrietta Swan Leavitt). Not only did he find that our universe was expanding but that the further away a galaxy was, the faster it is receding - Hubble’s Law.

The work of Hubble blew out of the water Einstein’s ‘steady state’ theory of the universe. Albert’s brilliant early equations would not give him the ‘steady state’ theory he was wedded too. To make steady state work he had to shove in a made-up factor, the Cosmological Constant, an imagined repulsive force, in one of his field equations of General Relativity. Albert honestly later admitted the Cosmological Constant was his biggest mistake.

This is Einstein’s not quite so famous equation - the dodgy ‘fix’ he used to get to an unmoving universe: R<sub>μν</sub> − Rg<sub>μν</sub> = <sub>2</sub> T<sub>μν</sub>,

p.441

If translated into a football match score, Hubble’s expansion team versus Einstein’s steady state eleven, the result would be a walk-over, say 10 for Hubble and 0 for Einstein. Albert may have lost out on this one but he was a great winner in other areas, relatively speaking.

Back again to Hubble. He generally gets most of the plaudits re the expanding universe theory, but two before him deserve serious mention, George Lemaitre, and the interestingly named Vesto Slipher. George was a Belgian Catholic priest. He was also a mathematician with a keen interest in astronomy, and like Edwin, went to Cambridge University.

Two years before Hubble’s 1924 breakthrough, George identified an expanding universe, but his work was not as widely publicised or accepted as Hubble’s. George also came up with another remarkable, and now established idea, that of the universe’s origin, the Big Bang. He really should have been a shoe-in for a Nobel Prize. Sadly, he proved that while ground-breaking scientific work is important, so is publicity and recognition, which he never sufficiently received. In acknowledgment of his work, Hubble’s Law is now commonly, and rightly, referred to as Hubble-Lemaitre Law.

Now for Vesto Slipher, no, his first name is not a mis-spelt box of matches (Vesta). He was an American astronomer, who did not go to Cambridge University. He was working about ten years before Hubble. Vesto discovered distant galaxies were ‘redshifted’. Redshift is nothing to do with ladies’ underwear. It means viewing the light wavelength, some move quicker or slower than others, to indicate galactic standstill, recession or growth.

p.442

In this case the redshift indicated galaxies receding from us. Hubble’s work was more detailed, and his arguments backed up with lots of solid evidence. It helped that the Mount Wilson telescope was the largest in the world at the time. Observations made with it were convincing. His papers were therefore widely read and accepted by the scientific community, so he still retains the top-man status in bringing the expansion theory to prominence. Slipher slipped away from public renown.

What’s beyond the Universe?

Our universe started from nothing so it’s everything there is. Just like the surface of a balloon it has no edges. There’s also no middle. In this sense it is not expanding into anything. Supreme astrophysicist Stephen Hawking said that there is no outside to the universe, asking what’s beyond the universe is like asking what is north of the North Pole. Yes, but what is north of the North Pole?

Universe Size in Parsecs and Astronomical Units

Now we can understand why the universe is so much bigger than might be expected. Coming back to its size, if you want to calculate that 46.5 billion light years in another way, try counting it in parsecs. These are 3.26 light years each. This makes the radius of our universe about 14.3 billion parsecs. Parsecs are calculated based on 1 astronomical unit and the distance from the intersection of 1 arcsecond angle. Light years are easier to comprehend!

In terms of astronomic units - one of these is the distance from Earth to the Sun - we have a much bigger number: There are 63,241 AU in a light year, so 46 billion times 63,241 is?? We won’t bother to try and calculate in miles or kilometres. There are just not enough numbers, well there are, but unpowered it would take up too many lines.

Size and Type of Stars

We’ve seen something about the size of the universe, who’s top when it comes to stars? At the moment a super-red giant star with an unmemorable name, UY Scuti, is in first place. This monster is much bigger than mighty Betelgeuse; almost 2.5 times.

To put it in a solar system perspective, UY Scuti is 1,700 times larger than our Sun. It’s a long way from us, at 5,219 light years, with intervening stars and dust. This is why we don’t easily see it.

p.443

Even UY Scuti would have been a titch alongside the Quasi Stars of the early universe. These were Black Hole centred and estimated to have a diameter 10,000 times that of the Sun.

Back to types of stars. There is a detailed classification of stars by spectrum and temperature.

In a condensed form, other than already mentioned Super-Red Giants and Red Giants like Betelgeuse, here are the main varieties of star:

  • Protostar This is cheating a little, because as the name suggests, it’s something that’s on the way to becoming a star. A Protostar is a great mass of gas that’s collapsed. Further collapse takes place because of the influence of gravity and pressure. This initial Protostar phase lasts about 500,000 years before a new star is born;
  • Main Sequence Star Most stars in the universe are Main Sequence Stars, this applies to our galaxy, the Milky Way. These are the basic, work-a-day stars. Their brightness and their size can vary from about 100<sup>th</sup> the size of our sun to 100 times its mass. They are in balance, from a sort of violent harmony of the outward pressure of fusion and the inner force of gravity. In this state the stars are known as in ‘hydrostatic equilibrium’. Main Sequencers are all engaged in the important job of converting hydrogen into helium, in their cores, and so spewing out tremendous amounts of energy. They only get into trouble once the hydrogen runs out, normally after several billion years. As can be seen by the chart, our Sun is nothing special, in the cosmic scheme of things. It’s one of the very many Main Sequence stars;
  • White Dwarf Star These are stars who have run out of hydrogen and are do not have enough mass to use higher atoms to make a fusion reaction. In a White Dwarf the pressure outwards is no longer in balance with gravity. The latter takes charge and collapses the star, but the star is not big enough for the collapse to result in a supernova then neutron star. Instead the star becomes a much smaller, less energetic version of its former self. Its contraction makes it very dense, typically, 200,000 times denser than the Sun. Now it is a White Dwarf;
p.444
  • Red Dwarf Star This is a broad definition, covering a variety of Main Sequence Stars. Red Dwarfs are small stars with a very low mass, from 7.5% to 50% of our Sun. They are relatively cool, hence they are red in colour and not so visible as some other stars. Red Dwarfs are the most common type of star in the Milky Way. Proxima Centauri, our nearest neighbouring star, is a Red Dwarf. Because they burn fuel so slowly and sparingly they are very long-lived stars. Our Sun’s has an anticipated life of 10 billion years. Red Dwarfs may last for until the end of the universe.
  • Neutron Star These are stars who have been roughly 1.4 to 2 times the size of our Sun. When they run out of fuel they go supernova. But they still leave some material behind. The force of stupendous gravity crushes protons and electrons together to form neutrons. These neutrons make up the entirety of what had been a Main Sequence Star. Stars that had been bigger than about 2.3 times our Sun after their supernova explosion, collapse under gigantic, inescapable gravity, the remnant then becomes a Black Hole, instead of a Neutron Star;
  • Supergiant Stars These will be dozens of times the mass of our Sun, and more. They consume their hydrogen fuel at a tremendously fast rate, in cosmic, time. Supergiants, like some rock stars, live fast and die young. They explode in a huge supernova, after just a few million years;
  • Binary Stars These are two stars relatively close together. There gravity attracts one to the other. They orbit around their common centre, called a barycentre. The great, German born, British astronomer William Hershel first named these types of stars saying: “If, on the contrary, two stars should really be situated very near each other, and at the same time so far insulated as not to be materially affected by the attractions of neighbouring stars, they will then compose a separate system, and remain united by the bond of their own mutual gravitation towards each other. This should be called a real double star; and any two stars that are thus mutually connected, form the binary sidereal system which we are now to consider.” He was expertly helped in his work by sister Caroline. William became the first President of the Royal Astronomical Society. (There’s a bit more about him in Chapter 24.) Binary stars are fairly common. Sometimes there are more than two stars who orbit around a common centre. These are called multiple systems. It’s estimated than in our own Milky Way one third of stars are either binary or multiple.
p.445
  • Brown Dwarf, the in betweener These objects are too big to be classified as planets. They form in the same way as stars, through collapsing gas and dust clouds, but don’t become dense enough for fusion to start, as it does with a star. They are a sort of failed star. If they had a bit more mass they would become Red Dwarf Stars.

Black Holes

Black Holes are the greediest, most omnivorous entities in the entire universe. They will eat any and everything, even light. The photons of light travel so fast they can reach our moon from Earth in one second, but light is not fast enough to get away from a Black Hole, if it gets too close. These supreme gobblers exercise incredibly powerful gravitational muscle to draw in their prey - gas, space dust, planets and even stars. Once inside their maw Black Holes crush their victims down to a tiny fraction of their previous size.

The Event Horizon Telescope project came up with this, the first clear image of a Black Hole. The project was an amazing collaboration. It brought together 200 scientists in observatories around the world. These observatories combined and synchronised in such a way that to produce a telescope the size of planet Earth. It collected an enormous amount of data about galaxy M87, this took 2 years to analyse and produce the picture above. M87 has an enormous Black Hole, but this is by no means the biggest. The largest discovered, as at 2019, is in the galaxy cluster, Abell 85. It is 40 billion times our Sun’s mass and 700 million light years from Earth.

Very big Black Holes are the Kings of the cosmos. They sit in the centre of their galaxies. Galactic stars and planets swirl respectfully around them.

What else about Black Holes? Well, as the name ‘Event Horizon Telescope’ suggests, All Black Holes have an Event Horizon. This is the boundary around a Black Hole beyond which nothing can escape it, not even light. It’s the point from which nothing can return because the gravitational pull of the Black Hole is too strong. Black Holes spin at close to the speed of light so stuff is both drawn in and around them.

p.446

Wormholes

A favourite of science fiction, these are a predicted by Einstein’s General Relativity theory. They are claimed to be bridges, Einstein - Rosen bridges, across space-time. They are like tunnels, from one place to another. If they work and we could find how to use them, it could mean a form of speedy short cut over vast distances in the universe. Wormholes remain speculative.

How Many Galaxies and Stars?

Among many things about our universe that’s hard to imagine and understand is how could there be an object incredibly tiny in size, far smaller than the smallest atom, have enough matter and energy to produce the current universe? This ‘singularity’ has resulted in today’s 2 trillion galaxies, with maybe 100 billion stars or more in each - exact numbers are not known. Cosmologists say that on average there are 3 or more exoplanets around every star. That means we need a big sheet of paper to write down in full, unpowered arithmetic, the possible number of planets in the universe.

Nebulae

These are the most gigantic factories in the cosmos. They churn out stars, acting both as a birthplace and nursery. Well, the churning is to a cosmic timescale and very slow in human terms. Nebulae are enormous clouds of interstellar dust and gas, hydrogen mostly. They can be millions of light years across. Sometimes, they’re formed by the material spewed out from a supernova. The death of one star can lead to the birth of another, eventually. The process can take several hundred million years. In nebulae atoms begin clumping together. The force of gravity results in bigger and bigger clumps. It’s mostly all hydrogen at first, when the accumulated mass recaches a critical stage it collapses and this starts a nuclear reaction - a star is born.

p.447

If the mass is insufficient to cause a nuclear reaction, a planet emerges. Nebulae give birth to big babies, stars, or small babies, planets.

What about Exoplanets?

Unlike the estimated total number of alien planets, which is assumed to be at least 3 for every star, there are exoplanets which have been firmly discovered. These are planets that have been observed to orbit stars other than our local Sun. The number found rises constantly. By October 2020 nearly 4,300 exoplanets had been found. Most of these were revealed by the Kepler space telescope. The biggest discovered by June 2020 is Kepler 88d. This is 6 times more massive than Jupiter. It obviously has plenty of space but there would still be an almighty crush. Of course, many exoplanets, just like most planets in the solar system, are not conducive to supporting life, or as we know it, carbon-based life. But given the gigantic number of exoplanets, some will have conditions with some similarity to Earth. Some discoveries possibly have water. and it is by no means certain that all life must be carbon based. Statistically, it seems unlikely that planet Earth has the only intelligent life in the universe, so the question is…

Are we Alone?

Are we, MH, the only intelligent life in the universe? We don’t know for sure. No aliens have made their presence unambiguously known on earth. The SETI project, which has been running for 50 years, has not yet made contact. However, short of this type of certainty we now know a lot more about exoplanets and the construction of the universe. These new findings throw up some indicative numbers which give statistical weight to a “no” we are not alone answer.

NASA’s Kepler mission reveals that about 17% of stars have Earth like planets orbiting them. That means upwards of 17 billion such planets in the Milky Way, our own galaxy. And as there are estimated to be 2 trillion galaxies, there will be many trillions of rocky, i.e. Earth like planets, in the universe

Even if only one in a million of these rocky planets has water and other minerals conducive to life, there will be many millions of planets like Earth. So, it’s reasonable to assume intelligent life has evolved on many planets in the universe.

p.448

And some of these alien intelligences have had time to become much more advanced than us. On older star systems in some galaxies Earth like planets could be 2 to 3 billion years older than Earth. One more thought about the possibility of discovering alien life. Since light takes so long to reach us we are always looking back a long way in time. Aside from a limited number of stars local to the Sun, light reaching us is from several hundred up to several billions of years ago. Advanced, detectable, alien civilisations may not have had time to develop. They could be there now, but we cannot detect ‘now’ because when we look up at the stars, we only see the past.

The Hubble Space Telescope, among several, is probably best known. It overlapped with Kepler. Next up for NASA, plus other partners, is the James Webb Space Telescope, due to be launched in 2021 by an Ariane 5 rocket from French Guiana.

It is known as a ‘Next Generation Space Telescope (NGST)’; meaning it has the very latest, more sensitive, technology, and will be able to probe deeper and more fully than its predecessors, Kepler, Hubble and Spitzer.’

Excitingly it will seek out potentially habitable exoplanets, especially looking at the Trappist-1 system, this is 40 light years from Earth, with 3 exoplanets. Apart from looking for possible life bearing exoplanets Webb will also study every stage of the history of our Universe. It had better find something of great interest, having cost 10 billion dollars.

p.449

While Webb is definitely on the go, NASA has other more hopeful plans. One is for a similar Hubble type, infrared scope, named ‘Luvoir’ for Large Ultraviolet Optical Infrared Surveyor. Luvoir would have 6 times the resolution of Hubble. Potential costs are enormous so Luvoir remains partly a dream as at June 2020 with a proposed launch date of 2039…

Comets, Asteroids and Meteors

For life to get going on our planet, or exoplanets, comets are essential early on, because they deliver water. Later, they are not wanted because their impact can cause extinctions. These icy, gassy tailed, lumps of rock and can be over 10 kilometres in size. Comets are fragments left over from when solar systems first formed. They can occasionally be drawn in by a planet’s gravity and cause devastation on impact.

Asteroids are similar but are of airless rock. They don’t have gassy tails. Meteors are very small, often they are bits of asteroids or comets that have broken off entering our atmosphere. They burn up in fiery streaks sometimes called shooting stars.

Solar System - Some Facts

Our home planet Earth is the third from the Sun, at an average of 93 million miles distance, or one Astronomical Unit.

The full planetary line up is: little Mercury (about a third of Earth in size), just 36 million miles from the Sun, then Venus, Earth, Mars, Jupiter, Saturn, Uranus and finally, at 2.8 billion miles from the Sun we have Neptune. Pluto was also included until 2006 when it was relegated to dwarf planet status. Planets do not orbit in perfect circles. Orbits are elliptical so distances from the sSun vary , for instance max distance for Earth is 94.5 million miles, on aphelion day in July. Its January minimum, the perihelion, is 91.3 million miles. Light can take 4 hours 9 minutes to reach Neptune, furthest planet, compared to 8.3 minutes to get to Earth.

The solar system’s 4 rocky planets are Mercury, Venus, Earth and Mars. Jupiter and Saturn are gas giants, with more gas than rock. The final 2, Uranus and Neptune, are known as ice giants. Giant really is an appropriate adjective. Compared to Earth, the smallest giant, Neptune, is 58 times bigger in size and 17 times as massive. The biggest, mighty Jupiter, is 1,321 times bigger, although lower density means it’s just 318 times as massive. If you ever manage to travel to Neptune, take a very warm coat. The temperature is minus 200 Celsius, but no worries about gravity. You will only feel 14% heavier. Not good for those on a diet

p.450

The solar system size is defined by the Oort Cloud. This is like a giant shell completely surrounding space beyond the planets. It’s between 0.3 to 3 light years from the Sun. The Oort Cloud has a huge number of orbiting rock particles and is the home of long-period comets, i.e. those that take more than 200 years to orbit the Sun. Closer to the Sun is the Kuiper Belt, a ring of icy material, including short-period comets. It is similar to the asteroid belt but much larger, at 2.8 billion to 4.6 billion miles from the Sun. The asteroid belt, with its millions of asteroids, is in a torus shaped orbit between Mars and Jupiter. Most planets have attendant moons. Saturn has the most at 82. Some moons of Jupiter and Saturn, like Europa, Titan, Enceladus, Ganymede and Callisto, may have water and could support life.

The Milky Way and its Neighbourhood

Our solar system resides in the Milky Way, a big galaxy some 100 thousand light years across.

Our solar system sits in a spiral arm called the Orion Arm, about 20,000 light years from the centre. The Voyager spacecraft travels at some 10 miles each second. It could go around the entire Earth in less than 1 hour, that seems fast, but it would take Voyager 450 million years to reach the MW centre.

We serenely orbit the centre, well if you call moving at close to 500,000 miles an hour serene. But we don’t notice the movement. There’s not even a sign of a strong wind. Our solar system speed in the Orion Arm is sufficient to twirl us around the MW in just 250 million years. Cosmic numbers are big.

p.451

A for Andromeda

Our mighty galactic neighbour, the Andromeda Galaxy, is 2.5 million light years away and 260,000 light years across. With a top estimate of 1 trillion stars it’s over three times the Milky Way’s 300 billion or so, Andromeda is due to crash into the Milky Way in 4.5 billion years. Given the size of the gaps between stars it won’t be much of a crash. Those Andromeda stars who have been well brought up will be saying to a new MW neighbour: “I’m so sorry, is this your orbital path? I could not help coming in this direction. Dark Matter and Dark Energy gave me a shove. I just couldn’t see what they were up to.”

Andromeda is our neighbouring galaxy but the nearest star to the solar system is Proxima Centauri, one of the Alpha Centauri binary stars. If we could travel at the speed of light it would take us 4.2 years to get there.

Monster Galaxy Godzilla

Thanks to NASA’s space telescope, Hubble, the biggest local galaxy yet known, in terms of size, is the Godzilla galaxy. At 463,000 light years across, it’s bigger than Andromeda and 4.6 times the size of our own, Milky Way galaxy. The MW is recognised as a large galaxy but Godzilla dwarfs it. Godzilla has a super-massive black hole at its centre, 1 trillion stars and is 232 million light years away in the northern constellation of Perseus.

Quasars

They are extremely energetic and extremely long towers of particles streaming out from the poles of some Black Holes at almost the speed of light. Quasars derive from the ‘accretion disc’ of material around a super massive Black Hole. Their energy is so great, it can be trillions of volts. It outshines the entire galaxy they are located in. Quasars brightness can be between 10 to 100,000 times that of the entire Milky Way galaxy.

They are not only supremely bright they also extend a very long way. Material from a quasar can project one million light years. Planets containing life have to stay clear of these quasar jets, otherwise kaput.

p.452

Fortunately, our own Earth is not in any quasar’s sights, mainly because quasars are relatively rare, that’s because they require super-massive Black Holes to power them.

Pulsars

These are not so powerful as quasars. Pulsars send out pulses of light from a very small but extremely dense Neutron star.

Neutron stars have a mighty magnetic field, which gives them their pulse power. They are about the size of a large city, but have more mass than our Sun. Radiation pulses arise because of the star’s rapid rotation, commonly one per second. The super quicks spin at more than 200 times a second.

Dark Matter and Dark Energy

What’s the Universe made of? This is it:

  • 68% dark energy, equivalent to Einstein’s cosmological constant,
  • 27% dark matter,
  • 4.9% normal the stuff of stars, planets, asteroids, dust etc, i.e. protons, neutrons and electrons,
  • 0.1% neutrinos and antineutrinos,
  • about 0.008% photons.

This breakdown means that we don’t see most of it. Nor can we detect, yet, the 95% represented by dark matter and dark energy. These are just theoretical things, which must be there because something is operating against gravitational forces to produce space expansion. Dark matter and dark energy have yet to be confidently discovered.

p.453

And Before the Big Bang?

Yes, we know, or think we do, that our Universe started with the Big Bang, that immensely dense singularity that blew up, balloon like. What came before the Big Bang? The BB arose from an incredibly tiny particle which can be seen as a seed of a new Universe or as some would say, a God Particle. But what created this seed? One idea has been put forward by Polish physicist Dr. Nikodem Poplawski. He suggests that in a Black Hole, of super-super size, compacting material is stopped by the BH spin. Black Holes can spin close to the speed light. This creates an enormous torsional effect on the super-compacted material. It becomes not only super heavy but also violently twisted. The twisting can suddenly release in the form of a Big Bang, or big bounce as Dr Poplawski prefers to say. Thus, we have a new Universe. Black Holes are a one-way door between two Universes, claims Dr Poplawski. Now, given there are billions of Black Holes, with each one likely to pop out a new Universe, this idea might also add credence to the Multiverse concept.

p.454

What of God?

The Abrahamic religions claim creation of our universe was not by a Black Hole, but by God. As we’ve already seen, there is no proof of this, but likewise nor is there proof of the Black Hole progenitor idea. Over many centuries, scientific understanding of our Universe has increased. We’ve moved on a long way since our hunter-gatherer forbearers may have thought stars were deceased ancestors (see Noth’s story Chapter 5), and from Aristotle and the Catholic church’s belief that the Earth was at the centre of it all.

In the early 20<sup>th</sup> century, the Milky Way galaxy was thought to be the entire Universe, and that our Universe was static, unchanging. We now know there are billions of other galaxies and that the Universe is not static at all, it’s expanding with increasing rapidity.

Cosmologists and astrophysicists are continuing to discover and understand more. Eventually we may be able to come up with an answer to Universal origin, the Great Theory of Everything, and be able to prove it. Meanwhile, it’s hard for us to contemplate the origin of all, i.e., what it is that comes from nothing. Is it possible for there to be nothing? In pondering this question we should ‘bear’ in mind what Winnie the Pooh said about ‘nothing’:

People say doing nothing is impossible but I do nothing every day.

Winnie the Pooh
