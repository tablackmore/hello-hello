The Big Bang

In 1964 two radio astronomers, Robert Wilson and Arno Penzias, were working with a giant horn antenna for satellite communications at Bell Telephone Laboratories in Holmdel, New Jersey. They were confused by a persistent “noise” signal that they couldn’t eliminate. No matter where they pointed the antenna in the sky, a constant microwave radiation was present. The noise was so persistent that they initially suspected pigeon droppings in the antenna! They eventually realized the signal was coming from everywhere in the sky and that it was the oldest light in the universe - the cosmic microwave background radiation leftover from the Big Bang. For this discovery, Penzias and Wilson shared the 1978 Nobel Prize in Physics.

The existence of this cosmic microwave background radiation supports the theory that our universe has been expanding since it began in what we call the “Big Bang” - a cataclysmic event about 13.8 billion years ago that produced an incredibly hot, incredibly dense state. This expansion continues today.

And in the End

We have a great deal of evidence to suggest how the universe began and how it has expanded to its current state. But we know much less in certainty about what the universe looks like now, what it will look like in the future, and indeed, how the universe will eventually end. It’s uncertain if it’s so uncertain: there’s no theory broad enough to be able to tell us what’s going on out there. Physicists and astronomers have been able to piece together a lot about how the universe began by examining what’s happening now. However, because about 5 billion years ago the universe appears to have started expanding more rapidly, we can’t predict the ultimate fate. Several theories exist about the ultimate fate of the universe. Let’s dig into them, but first, we need to understand something called “density.”
