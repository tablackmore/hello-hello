Chapter 13

The ancient world of the Far East and elsewhere

Pages 108–115

While the likes of Sumer, Akkad, Assyria, the Hittite empire, Babylon and Egypt were developing all the trappings of civilisation, things were moving in the Far East too.

China

The first Emperor of China was Qin Shi Huang. He, by force of arms, unified the whole of China in about 225 BCE. His legacy is still to be seen, in thousands of terracotta warriors, the Great Wall of China, and in establishing the first controlling bureaucracy. But Qin’s empire was not the first Chinese civilisation. It was the first to cover the whole of China, mostly as now known. A much earlier dynasty, the Xia, ruled a large Chinese territory, but not the whole of China. They were probably the first civilisation in China. The Xia dynasty was around in 2000 or 2200 BCE, according to different calculations. That’s one claim, but because of lack of permanent written records, it’s not certain Xia was the first. The Shang Dynasty, about 1600 BCE, might have that honour. The ancient Chinese sensibly used the materials to hand to both build and to some extent write on, i.e. mainly bamboo. This does not last as long as the stone work of Mesopotamian and Egyptian civilisations, so old records and structures for ancient China are sparse, or non-existent. Anyway, whatever, Xia or Shang, both were located around the Yellow River. Unlike the Nile farmers, Xia clans did not appreciate the major flooding that regularly occurred with the Yellow River. Chinese myths talk of the efforts of Gun followed by his son Yu to stop flooding. The latter succeeded by building a series of canals, that took water to the sea. This story suggests a large, organized society, capable of major constructions.

People of the Shang Dynasty worshipped a lot of different Gods, just as did ancient Middle Eastern civilisations. Many of these early Chinese Gods were associated with the natural world: they had a weather God and a sky God. Their chief deity, was named Shangdi. Shangdi had a lot of roles to perform: responsible for success in battle; the harvest; weather and the capital city. Later in the Zhou dynasty, Shangdi was supplanted by Tian, a supreme deity meaning heaven.

Gods and ancestors were thought important because they could impact on crops, childbirth, weather and warfare. Therefore, attention, had to be paid to them.

The religion of the Shang probably developed from a much older, maybe 3,000 years older, form of ancestor worship and shamanism. This can be inferred from burial practices of the Neolithic Chinese including orientation of the dead. These were buried with offerings liquids and animal skulls.

Eventually, some philosophies emerged between 600 and 450 BCE, which can be likened to a religion. These were Taoism or Daoism, Buddhism and Confucianism.

All three have some commonality in their teachings. They are known as generally peaceful, non-threatening religions or philosophies. Their focus is on personal ethics and they tell people how best to live life in harmony with themselves and others. None involve a personal God of the sort favoured by Abrahamic religions. Buddhism, eventually, had widest support.

Buddhism was made the state religion by China’s only woman Emperor, Wu Zetian, in about 683 CE. This was partly because of the ideological support she received from the Buddhist community. A similar pattern was found in Mesopotamia and ancient Egypt where the state religion was used to prop up the power of the chiefs. Nowadays, Buddhism is the most prevalent of the three religions or philosophies in China with about 250 million followers and 13,000 temples. (see Philosophy, Chapter 23 for more on Buddhism, Taoism and Confucianism.)

Apart from the religious form of Buddhism, China has had many indigenous and traditional religions. Together, these represent about 10% of the world’s religious population. They have in common certain features: an oral tradition with no holy literature; a belief in spirits; veneration for the dead; encouragement of traditional medicine and attempt to use magic. Some take on elements of Christian beliefs, i.e. they are syncretic.

India

It’s not clear what religious beliefs were held, if any, by inhabitants of India’s ancient Indus Valley, the Harappan culture. No evidence has emerged. There are no temples, or written clues and burial practices were varied. It has been suggested, arguably, that some female figurines from around 2,000 BCE may represent a fertility Goddess.

After the end of the Indus Valley civilisation period came the Vedic age in India, leading directly to Hinduism.

Hinduism arose in India and Nepal and is the oldest major religion in the world. It still has many followers in this part of the world, about 900 million. Hindu is Greek for the land beyond the Indus River. The religion has no single source or founder. It’s thought to be an amalgam of various ancient beliefs and cultures, which go back to 2500 BCE, i.e. 4,500 years ago.

Hinduism

There is no clarity about the timing and beginning of Hinduism. Early Hinduism may stem from religious rituals of the Indus Valley civilization, centered on the defunct, ancient cities Harappa and Mohenjo-Daro. It’s thought these had a religion that involved temple rituals, mass ritual bathing, animal sacrifice and possibly Goddess worship. Such practices feature in later Hinduism.

Hinduism further developed in the Vedic period, 1500 to 500 BCE. The religious guidance described in the four Vedas, (sort of scriptures) was written in Vedic Sanskrit during this time. They are the oldest writings in Hinduism and thought to originate from revelations made to ancient sages after a period of heavy meditation.

The four Vedas are: the Rigveda, Yajurveda, Samaveda, and Atharvaveda. These describe various mantras, rituals and sacrifices. It’s not clear whether this also involved use of mind-bending substances. Other ancient literature, containing some of the central ideas of Hinduism, is found in the Upanishads texts that encourage meditation and acquirement of spiritual knowledge. The modern practice of Yoga, which has become popular in the west, originates from the Rigveda, perhaps before, even beyond, Hinduism. Yoga is both a physical and mental - meditative - exercise, aiming to promote well-being. The Hindu God Shiva is thought by some to have been the first yogi. Others may thing a man had a hand in it. One well known, common mantra used in yoga is the word, or hum, ohm, ohmmmmmmmmmmmm. So repetitive, this does not just go on and on but ohm and ohm. Yoga is proven to be good for humans for its gentle exercise which improves flexibility and strength, and because it is mentally calming. It is sometimes used in association with ‘mindfulness’ - self-awareness conditioning.

Some cross-fertilization of religious ideas occurred around the time of Buddha’s death, including the idea of the problems caused by human desires - hence yoga. The next major contribution to the development of Hinduism was the arrival of the Bhagavad-Gita (“Song of God”, 100 CE). This text explains three ways to salvation: duties; knowledge and yoga.

All this ancient textual advice is intended to guide believers in order to achieve a sort of divine goodness. Such moral improvement might take many lifetimes. Re-incarnation, called Samsara, is an essential belief of Hinduism. The religion claims that in an endless cycle of re-birth Hindus need to pursue knowledge of truth and reality. In the process Hindus must seek a moral order and a peaceful tolerance towards others. The right karma, that is the way a person acts or re-acts, which can be either good or bad, is how this is achieved. They believe there is one ultimate reality called Brahman. Confusingly there is also an important God, named Brahma. Brahma is one of a triumvirate of Gods, he is the creator, second up is Vishnu who keeps things going and finally we have Shiva the destroyer. She is the sort of necessary destroyer to allow things to start up again. Hindus think Brahman pervades the whole universe and is inside humans in the form of atman, the soul. The eventual aim of samsara is to move close to Brahman.

There is a wide variety of Hindu Gods and Goddesses. These are thought to be aspects of the Supreme Brahman. Hinduism easily outstrips every other religion in terms of number of Gods. They hold the gold medal for Multitudinous Deities without a worthy challenger in sight.

This proliferation of divinities arises because every village, group, caste and even family can have their own set of Gods, resulting in many thousands of Gods.

As with other major religions Hinduism has different sects. The main sects are: Vaishnavites, for them Lord Vishnu is top God; Saivites, whose chief God is Siva; Shaktas, where Goddess Shakti reigns supreme lastly there are Smartas (an unfortunate name. It has quite a different meaning in colloquial English), these are the liberals, not necessarily smugly clever as the English word would indicate.

For the Indian Hindu associated with the Smarta sect the choice of Deity is left to the believer but usually they claim loyalty to all the Hindu Gods. Smartas’ are members of the upper classes - Brahman, Kshatriya or Vaishya.

Other Eastern Religions

Hinduism, with 1.1 billion followers is by far the biggest Eastern religion, closely followed by Buddhism, if this is considered a religion rather than a philosophy. Most other religions of the area bear some association with features of Hinduism but are not as popular. They include:

  • Bahai Began in Persia, now Iran. It is a relatively new religion starting up in 1863. Bahaullah, the founder, claimed he was a new prophet of God, like Jesus or Mohammed. It has about 6 million followers in more than 100 countries. The central message is twofold: 1. The whole of mankind is one unified spiritual whole. Under Bahai, there is no racism or nationalism. All people are equal. The world is a unified oneness, or should be, according to Bahai.; 2. God is unknown but is gradually revealing himself. In this process all major messengers of monotheistic religions, and others, are a part of this unfolding knowledge of God. These include Buddha, Abraham, Moses, Zoroaster, Jesus and Mohammed.

  • Cao Dai This is a one-size-fits-all religion with about 2 million followers. Meaning “High Tower” or supreme God Cao Dai is a newish, 1920, Vietnamese religion. It’s an amalgam of many beliefs: Confucianism; Taoism; Buddhism and Roman Catholicism. Cao Dai’s list of saints is unusual, embracing both famous religious and non-religious figures, include Brahma, Jesus Christ, Mohammed; Confucius; Pericles; Julius Caesar; Sun Yat-sen, Joan of Arc and Napoleon I.   • Jainism This religion began and is largely based in India with about 4.2 million adherents. Like Hindus Jains believe in leading an ethical, non-violent life and in an endless number of re-births. They are also vegetarians and try not to harm any living creature. Their first teacher, they claim, was Rishabhanatha. He is meant to have lived millions of years ago. Given that we Modern Humans are about 300,000 years old this seems unlikely. Mahavira, who was around in about 540 BCE, is more likely to have influenced modern Jainism through texts called Agamas. The oldest Jain religious text is the Acaranga Sutra, 5th to 4th century BCE. Jainism is mainly confined to India, but pockets exist in a few other countries. There are about 4 million Jains.

  • Shinto Just over half of modern day Japanese practice Shinto, which means “Way of the Spirits.” It has no founder nor does it have just a main, single sacred writing. In essence it encourages devotion to spiritual beings known as kami. These kami are not Gods but have the power to help with happiness, health, exam results and prosperity. They need proper respect. Japanese Shinto followers may have a small shrine-altar in their homes to appeal to the kami. There are also public shrines and public festivals. The spirits are considered as one with our world. There is not a transcendental, other world. Shinto is specific to Japan.

  • Sikhism This is yet another Indian founded religion with 20 million followers. It originated in the Punjab province in the 16th century by Guru Nanak. It differs from Hinduism in its monotheism. But some other teachings show a similarity. Sikhs think everyone should be treated equally. People should conduct themselves honestly, be generous, work hard, and keep God in mind. The key Sikh scripture is the Guru Granth Sahib.

  • Sinism, or Mism, or in China Wuism This is an ancient, Korean folk religion based on Shamanism. Its title means “religion of the spirits”. Gods, ancestors and nature spirits are a feature. Spirits and Gods abound. In modern times it does not feature much, if at all. Koreans are now much more likely to be mainly non-religious, second up are Christians then Buddhists.

  • Zoroastrianism It vies with Hinduism as the world’s oldest religion, arising in Persia about 3,500 years ago. It was the main religion of the great Persian Empire of Darius the Great and his son, the not-so-great Xerxes. Astutely though, the Persian conquerors allowed their defeated foes to retain their own religion, or beliefs of

another ancient religion, Hinduism, is still today a major religion. However, Zoroastrianism is not. It was Persia’s (Iran) official religion for over 1,200 years, from 600 BCE up the time of the Arab Muslim conquest in 650 CE. Now, there are only about 190,000 followers. Zoroaster was a man who lived about 1,000 years before Jesus Christ. He thought of himself as a seeker after truth and wisdom; just as did any philosopher, but he preached a form of metaphysics that was religious in nature. Zoroaster claimed the universe was forever split between light and darkness, with light representing good and darkness evil. People were at liberty to go towards the light or dark, this sounds a bit like the Star Wars where Luke had the goodie “Force” with him while chief baddy, Darth Vader, gloried in the “Darkside”.

Early on, Zoroastrian had two Gods. One was the chief of the light and the good, Ahura Mazda. The other, representing the peak of darkness and evil, was Ahriman. This dualistic religion over time gradually moved to monotheism with Mazda recognized as sole chief God. Zoroaster is sometimes known as Zarathustra; a name given greater prominence by the writings of Nietzsche and by the music of Strauss in his Opus 30. The Zoroastrian God, Ahura Mazda, is also known as the Wise Lord. Mazda, who now creates cars, started off by cranking up the universe, while allowing for good and evil in the process. This has great resonance with the later religion of Christianity. Did early Christian Biblical authors do a bit of copying of this concept? It also has similarity with Taoism’s idea of light and dark. Zoroastrians did not allow artistic holy images of any type. This ruling is also a feature of later Islam. Since much of early Muslim conquest was of the Persian, Zoroastrian, Empire, could this no-imagery rule have been copied? Zoroastrianism’s sacred text is The Avesta. It teaches that the elements are pure and that fire represents God’s wisdom. The Parsis, a sect of this religion who escaped persecution in Persia, exist now in India and number about 50,000.

  • African religions We’ve now covered development of religions in the middle and Far East, what about Africa? About 85% of Africans today are either Christians or Muslims. The former predominate in sub-Saharan Africa and the latter is very heavily represented in countries bordering the Mediterranean and in the Horn of Africa (Red Sea coastline). An estimated 15% are mostly of indigenous religions and Bahai.

  • South America Early South Americans, Aztecs, Incas, Mayans, Toltecs and others had naturalistic Godly beliefs that were part and parcel of everyday life. Their Gods had to be appeased or appealed to by animal or human sacrifice. Spanish and Portuguese conquerors brought Catholicism to South America. They also brought, eventually, many African slaves. Some African beliefs translated in amended form to South America, largely as a result of the slave trade. Candomblé is probably the main one of this type. It has about 2 million followers, mostly in Brazil. The principal form of devotional expression is through dance, not surprising since the name Candomblé means, “dance in honour of the Gods”. Oludumaré is top of the Candomblé Godly tree, serviced by lesser deities called orixas. Every person is believed to have his own orixa.

  • Other parts of the world Ancient religion in other parts of the world, such as Australasia and Arctic regions, was based on nature and mysticism. It still is to some extent. Australian Aborigines believed in ancestral creator beings. These brought into being living creatures and natural features like rock formations and water holes. The creator beings were thought to have roamed through the land naming and creating as they went. The time of creation was known as dreamtime.

  • The Maoris of New Zealand originated from Polynesian stock and their religion was similar to that of Polynesia. It identified with many Gods of nature and, as with Australian Aborigines, believed dreaming important.

  • The Sami (reindeer herders) and other far northerners have traditionally engaged in animism which included a form of bear worship.
