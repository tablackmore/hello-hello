Chapter 17

Christianity

Pages 139–171

In terms of numbers of adherents Christianity trumps every other religion currently and in the past. It has 2.4 billion followers. Catholics have the most followers, at 1.3 billion, followed by Protestants, 920 million. The rest are mostly of the Eastern Orthodox church.

The Christian Bible

This is the holy book revered by Christians. It is central to the Christian faith. The Bible is in fact several books, comprising of both the Old and New Testaments. The old really is old because it was probably written in Hebrew or Aramaic between 1200 and 165 BCE. And the New Testament is only relatively new. It was written almost 2,000 years ago. Books of the New Testament total 27: four Gospels; the Epistles, mostly by St Paul; and the Revelation of St John. The Old Testament has more books. Catholics and Protestants count 39 but some other Christian denominations, Coptic and Ethiopian, add an extra 4.

There was more than one author of the Bible, probably about 40; not surprising given it was written over a period of about 1,300 years. Even by some biblical standards, no one lives that long. Moses, he of the famous ten commandments and leader of the Jewish flight out of Egypt, is thought by Abrahamic religious adherents, to have written the first five books of the Bible, (see the claim explained here that this is impossible: https://listverse.com/2014/09/08/10-theories-about-who-really-wrote-the-bible). At a distance of 3,000 or so years we cannot be quite certain of the exact timing or precise authorship. When describing the Bible as consisting of several books this seems apt, because of the word’s origin. The word “Bible” comes from Roman Latin, which in turn derives from the Greek ta biblia = the books. We have more on the Bible a little later but for now concentrate on Christian beliefs.

p.140

You could also argue Christianity really began with Abraham, maybe 1,500 BCE, or perhaps Adam, 4,000 BCE (by Biblical counting). In the start-up frame we also have John the Baptist. He, a relative of Jesus, began his preaching and baptizing before Jesus got underway. He announced the coming messiah, i.e. Jesus. John, sometimes known as John the Forerunner or John the Immerser, later got carried away and lost his head in foolishly denouncing Herod, the local chief, for divorcing his wife and marrying Herodias. As a result, he really did lose his head.

3. Anabaptists

p.142

These believe that baptism only works when the baptisee really wants to be baptised and says that he really does believe in Jesus. Faith in the Gospel must come before Baptism, for the Anabaptists. Anabaptists literally means again Baptists. Perhaps though it should mean later Baptists’ because the baptisee must be an adult. Anabaptists think this is when a person can confess sin and properly embrace the faith. Children are considered not to be able to do this, thus they should not be baptised. Swiss Ulrich Zwingli, in the early 15th century, came up with this idea. Because it was against both Protestant orthodoxy and the Catholic church Anabaptists were heavily persecuted by both in 15th and 16th centuries. Today there are about 4 million Anabaptists. They are closely linked to Amish and can be referred to as Mennonites.

4. Amish

These are of German and Swiss origin. They believe in a literal interpretation of the Bible and are one of about 25 very slightly different Amish communities. The Amish are closely related to Mennonites, with some distinct differences. Amish don’t like modern technology and try and avoid it, that’s why they frequently use horses for transport and working the land. They also have little contact with the world outside their community and dress very traditionally. All these traits are not found in Mennonites. Together with Mennonite and Brethren groups Amish are mainly located in Pennsylvania USA. These are known as the Plain People.

5. Anglicans

Originated in the 16th century English Reformation when Protestantism broke away from the Catholic Church. It is the main branch of Protestantism. Its mother church is the Church of England with the Archbishop of Canterbury at the head. A worldwide group of churches fall under the Anglican umbrella.

6. Baptists

p.143

An avenue of Protestantism that insists believers must be baptized by a process of total immersion in water. Just a little sprinkling does not do the job for Baptists. But some Baptists, known as Reformed Baptists do believe that babies can be baptized. Some Baptists trace their origin to John the Baptist - forerunner of Jesus Christ. The Baptist sect could be seen as a 17th century offshoot of Congregationalism. Early Baptists were one of two groups; Particular Baptists and General Baptists. The former believed in the doctrine of particular atonement and the latter were a bit freer. The Generalists thought Christ had died for all.

7. Calvinists

John Calvin, kicked off this version of Protestantism. He was a Frenchman who died in 1564 and was counted as one of the leading Protestant reformers of his day, although he has a rival here - Martin Luther. His work the “Institutes” set out his beliefs, founded on a rather adaptive interpretation of the Bible in a humanistic way. Appeal to the heart rather than bludgeon people with too much reasoning, rites and rituals was his view. He said salvation was only possible through God’s grace, not via good works. He believed in original sin and in predestination, so, for Calvin, God had already decided, probably some billions of years ago, who was to be saved. Living a moral life only shows that a person just might one of the lucky ones, but God had already made his decision before you were born, so, tough, you just have to hope for the best. There’s no way that God’s divine decision making can be influenced.

8. Christian Fundamentalists

p.144

This developed in the USA late on during the 19th century. Charles Hodge, a Princeton USA theologian, helped establish Fundamentalists ideas. The movement was a reaction against the revised Christian theology, known as modernism, following on from Darwin’s theory of evolution. The latter put a very big hole in the ship of Genesis. Subsequently, the ship was hardly able to get out of the dock. The Fundamentalists thought Darwin fundamentally wrong, ergo the Biblical ship was completely unscathed. They believe in the historical accuracy of the Bible, including Genesis, with the 7-day timescale for all creation. They also think the ‘second coming’ is imminent. It is not clear exactly how long is imminent, given early Fundamentalists first thought the coming was imminent over one hundred years ago. Of course, something can forever be imminent. Many Fundamentalists do not smoke, drink, dance, go to the cinema or theatre. They do engage in congregational singing though. Do they watch TV and what about mobile phones, iPads, laptops etc? None of these are mentioned in the Bible.

9. Christadelphians

This word comes from Greek meaning ‘brother of Christ’. It kicked off in the USA during the mid 19th century thanks to John Thomas. Some early Christadelphians used their beliefs as an argument against military service during the time of the American civil war. Just like Jehovah’s Witnesses the C’s do not believe that man has an immortal soul. There are thought to be about 50,000 Christadelphians in 120 countries.

10. Christian Scientist

‘Science and Health with Key to the Scriptures’, a book by Mary Baker Eddy in 1875 set this one going. It was a reaction to Darwin and evolution. She said that Christian Science was: “…the law of god, the law of good.” She and her followers founded the Church of Christian Scientist. Its principle intent was to re-start the “primitive Christianity and its lost element of healing.” Mary argued that sickness was just an illusion. It could be sorted out by prayer. For Christian Scientist all is in the mind. CS considers Jesus to be divine (i.e. has Godly attributes) but not a deity. There are about 400,000 CS worldwide.

12. Church of the Latter-Day Saints (Mormons)

Mormon is the common name associated with this sect, but this title embraces some slightly different, although far smaller denominations, up to 12. The ‘Community of Christ’, with 250,000 members, and the ‘Apostolic United Brethren’, who have around 10,000 followers, are two of these smaller Mormon denominations. The ‘pure’ church’s real name is “Church of the Latter-Day Saints”, or the LDS Church. The churches are in fact mostly temples.

Apparently in 1819 the angel called ‘Moroni’ paid a visit to a 14-year-old American, Joseph Smith. Moroni told Joseph of ancient writings setting out God’s work for Americans. The angel visited Joe three times and told him to get on with it. Purportedly, the angel turned up brandishing a sword on his last visit and threatened to kill the cowering Joseph if he did not follow instructions. This, understandably, spurred Joe into action. He found those Godly records, interpreted them, then wrote them up into the ‘Book of Mormon’. Apart from his book, Joe was also well known for his lust for the opposite sex, sometimes quite young girls. This might explain why he believed in polygamy, although this was apparently on orders from God. He knew a lot of women very well, and ended up with 40 wives. The youngest, Helen Kimball, was 14. Early on in his marriage to Emma, he seduced Fanny, his wife’s 16-year-old housemaid. Other men’s wives were not safe from his attention. This sexual selfishness caused him to have to deal with a court case against him. William Law, on 23 March 1844, filed a suit against Smith for committing adultery with Smith’s foster daughter. Writing in the Salt Lake Tribune newspaper in 2014, Mormon psychologist, Kirsty Money said: “Joseph may have erred in his practice of polygamy”. The ‘may have’ suggests the possibility that he may not have erred. Poor Joe Smith upset a number of more traditional Christians and probably quite a few husbands and fathers. As a result, while in prison, he was attacked by a mob and killed in 1844. This murderous action was a copy of the Christian mob treatment handed out to that astonishing woman, Hypatia. She was mob tortured and murdered in Alexandria, some 1,500 years before Joe’s demise. The comparison with Joe is very unfair on Hypatia. In nearly all respects she was head and shoulders above Joe Smith.

p.146

After Joe, Brigham Young, from 1847, was the LDS Church President and governor of the territory, later state, of Utah, USA. Brigham gave the LDS a great boost, especially in Salt Lake City. He was also, like Joe Smith, strongly supportive of the state of marriage, having 55 wives. Brigham was really very busy: founding Salt Lake City; working away as state governor; and presiding over the whole Mormon community. Even so, he must have been able to spend some time with his many wives - they bore him 59 children. The poor chap, fancy having to remember the names of those 55 wives and 59 children, exhausting work for his brain. “Latter-day” - those earlier saints missed the cut. Mormons believe in total immersion baptism and in confirmation. There are about 16 million Mormons today.

13. Church of United Brethren in Christ

This evangelical church started up in Indiana, USA in the 18th century. It has historic links to Mennonites and Methodists. After splits between liberals and conservatives it first merged with the Evangelical Church then the Methodists to become the United Methodists Church.

14. Congregationalists

This one is of English origin and oldish, having started off in the late 16th century with founder, Robert Browne. They opposed both the Catholic church and the structured Protestantism of the Church of England. They took up and developed up the reforming ideas centuries old, i.e. those of of the Lollards, John Wycliffe, a philosopher/preacher, and later Martin Luther. As opposers Congregationalists were, along with other groups, called Puritans at first. Governance is a key difference in Congregationalism. Each church is required to look after its own affairs, independently of any other higher, human command. The authoritative governing structure of the Church of England and Roman Catholics is an anathema to them. Sometimes they are called Independents, especially in Wales. Congregationalists were a bit more prominent in Victorian times. In 1972 some Congregationalists’ merged with Presbyterians. However at least three groups remained in the Congregational fold: the Evangelical Fellowship, the Federation of Congregational Churches and the Fellowship of Independent Evangelical Churches.

15. Coptic Catholic Church

These are a location orientated Catholic sect, mainly in Egypt (over 8 million) and Sudan (500,000). The word Coptic comes from the Greek for Egyptian. The Coptic church closely associated with the smaller Eritrean and Ethiopian Catholic Churches. All use a liturgy called the Alexandrian Rites. Coptic language is spoken in this Rite by, you’ve guessed it, the Copts of Egypt and Sudan. The other two speak Ge’ez in their liturgical services. The Coptic church HQ is St Mark’s Cathedral, Cairo, Egypt. A place that recognizes the claimed Coptic founder as St Mark.

16. Eastern Orthodox (excluding Russian and Greek Orthodox)

This is a biggy. It’s estimated there are more than 200 million members. Sometimes churches under this general umbrella is referred to as ‘Orthodox Church’. This term includes: Church of Constantinople; Church of Antioch; Church of Russia and Church of Poland and Church of Greece among others. The BBC describe the Orthodox Church in this way: “It is made up of a number of self-governing Churches which are either ‘autocephalous’ (meaning having their own head) or ‘autonomous’ (meaning self-governing). The Orthodox Churches are united in faith and by a common approach to theology, tradition, and worship. They draw on elements of Greek, Middle-Eastern, Russian and Slav culture.” The big chief is the Patriarch of Constantinople. Eastern and Western Christians began to split up following the seventh Ecumenical Council of 787CE. They very finally and resentfully divorced after the Fourth Crusade in 1204. During this time Constantinople was sacked by the Crusaders. Eastern churches keep to the language of the local community while Western, Catholic, churches use Latin. One distinct difference with most other Christian religions is the Orthodox cross. This has three cross bars. The bottom one is slanted and is commonly said to be a foot rest for Christ. The shorter, top bar is for Pontius Pilate’s inscription in Latin, Greek and Hebrew saying “Jesus the Nazorean, King of the Jews.” This is usually shortened to ‘INRI’.

17. Episcopalians (Episcopal Church, United States)

p.148

Like several other sects this one is based mainly in the USA where it started up in 1785. It became separate from the Church of England after the American Revolution. Its beliefs and practices are extremely close to main stream Church of England - Anglicanism. It’s an American branch in a way. The Episcopal church can occasionally describe itself as Protestant yet Catholic, so attempting to keep a foot in both major camps. One difference is that it ordains both women and gay people. There are over 1.5 million Episcopalians, nearly all are in America.

18. Ethiopian Tewahedo Church Orthodox

In its title the word ‘Tewahedo’ means made one’ in the language of this church, Ge’ez. It’s a reference to the unity of Jesus who combines both human and Godly characteristics. The first Ethiopian church member was, apparently, a eunuch. The Ethiopian Orthodox church is closely associated with the Coptic church and its Alexandrian liturgical rites.

19. Evangelicals

This is a broad title which embraces many Protestants denominations including Methodist, Baptist, Mennonite and Pentecostal. The Protestant origin goes back to at least the early 1700’s. Luther, Calvin and Zwingli were influencers. Others, hundreds of years before these, also were heeded, such as John Wycliffe (Oxford University philosopher/theologian of around 1360) and Peter Waldo (French religious leader of about 1170, a believer in simplicity in religious observance and poverty in life style). Their core beliefs are founded on three Latinised, Biblical concepts: Sola Scriptura, by scripture alone - meaning pay sole heed to the Bible, both old and new testaments. The Bible tells all that’s needed; Sola Gratia, by grace alone is the way to get salvation, emphasizing that God is in charge; finally, Sola Fide by faith alone, being saved by grace through faith. Does this mean that these latter two ‘alones’ make a couple, hence neither are alone! And in fact together with the former alone they make a trinity.

20. Friends of Man

p.149

Quite a recent one this, starting off in 1919 by the writings and preachings of the long titled Frederic Louis-Alexandre Freytag. Friends are found mostly in Switzerland and France. They have a precise figure in mind about the number of people allowed in heaven - 144,000. This is OK though, because with only 71,000 members all these have a good chance of making it. The Friends don’t believe in a Godly trinity. It’s all one for them. They are apparently strong on the Bible. Members are vegetarians, so maybe not be quite so overly strong on the Bible which refers to meat eaters. Two unusual feasts are celebrated by the Friends, that of the Army of the Lord on 18th April and about 6 months later there’s the feast of the Little Flock.

21. Greek Orthodox

A biggish church with about 25 million members, half of whom are in Greece. Greek Orthodox is a part of the family of Eastern Orthodox churches. The founder is thought to be the Apostle Andrew. It grew out of the Eastern Roman Empire - Byzantium. The old capital, Constantinople, now Istanbul, remains a special place for Orthodox Christians. The main differences with Protestantism and Catholicism is in the way they worship, language used and, in the belief that the Holy Spirit is involved in everything. They also reject the supremacy of the Pope and don’t accept the Pope’s decisions on religious issues are infallible. The nave and altar in GO churches are separated by an iconostasis. This is a wall of religious paintings of Jesus and saints. Traditionally, only men are allowed behind it, in the altar section.

22. Hutterites

Hutterites are sometimes called ‘Hutterian Brethren’. They are associated with Anabaptists and Mennonites. Like them they are firm believers in the literal truth of the Bible. Hutterites came about during the 16th century Reformation when founded by German Jacob Hutter. They believe in communal living and the 45,000 members of today are based mostly in North America and Western Canada.

23. Jehovah’s Witnesses

p.150

This is another Christian sect from the USA. It originated in Pittsburgh during the 1870’s, as the Bible Student Movement. They only adopted the name 'Jehovah’s Witnesses" in 1931. A group of elders in New York oversees the movement and its doctrines. They have a fairly unique view of the soul - that it does not exist. This goes completely against orthodox Christian beliefs. There is a mistranslation of the old Hebrew and Greek word as soul when, according to JWs it means ‘life’. After Armageddon, just those good and faithful JWs will make it to everlasting life, as immortal ‘spirit beings’ but apparently there is a limit of 144,000. The Bible, Revelation 7 and 14 are the source of this figure, which is disputed by most other Christian denominations. Jehovah’s Witnesses are famous as Biblical door-to-door salesmen, also for refusing medical treatment involving blood. They claim: “Both the Old and New Testaments clearly command us to abstain from blood.” and, “God views blood as representing life. So, we avoid taking blood not only in obedience to God, but also out of respect for him as the giver of life.” Of course, medically, when an operation is required, blood is going to be involved. The person needing the operation may die without it. This has caused a few headaches when parents try and insist their child does not have the recommended operation. They are very strong on promoting and explaining the Bible, requiring members to go out and tell others about it. Their meetings take place in a “Kingdom Hall”. One of their claims is: “When bad angels married women on Earth they had sons who were giant bullies. Violence was everywhere. But Noah was different. He loved and obeyed God.” JW think secular societies are morally corrupt and under Satan’s influence. They’ve made various predictions of: Christ’s Second Coming; the advent of God’s Kingdom and Armageddon. So far none of these have happened, although with Climate Change looming ever larger, the latter might come about. JWs are anti the idea of the Trinity and don’t observe Christmas or Easter, thinking these to be pagan festivals. There are about 8 million evangelic JW followers and maybe 12 million more non-evangelicals.

24. Lutherans

p.151

Martin Luther cranked this one into life in 16th century Germany. There are estimated to be more than 74 million adherents. See a fuller description below under the heading: During the Reformation Martin Luther, ditched Catholicism’

25. Mennonites

This is a broad-brush alternate title. The Mennonites refer to those Christian groups called Anabaptists. Its name comes from Menno Simons (1496 to 1561) who preached and wrote in Holland and elsewhere. Mennonite beliefs were enshrined in the Dordrecht Confession of Faith in 1632. Mennonites are great believers in pacifism.

26. Methodists

p.152

So called because of the methodical way in which they practiced their Christian faith. We have John Wesley (1703 to 1791) to thank, mainly, for launching Methodism. His brother Charles helped as did George Whitfield. The church is one of the big ones, having 70 million members worldwide. In Great Britain it has 6,000 churches with 25,000 members. John Wesley said he had a spiritual experience: “I felt my heart strangely warmed. I felt I did trust in Christ, Christ alone, for salvation; and an assurance was given me that he had taken away my sins.” The experience encouraged John to preach. He proved to be a most skillful operator. Methodists are known as non-conformists, well they are to some in the know. This means they do not recognize the authority of the Church of England or keep to its rules. Interestingly though, John Wesley himself thought it should remain within the Anglican Church saying: “I live and die a member of the Church of England.” There have been a few past schisms resulting in minor branches such as: Primitive Methodists; Wesleyan Methodists and United Methodists Church. Today these have mostly joined up into the present-day church which is world-wide with a claimed 80 million followers in 1956.

27. Moravian Church

Jan Hus (1372 to 1415), a Czech, started up the Hussite movement which later became the Moravian Church (Moravia was formerly a region in the Czech republic). For his troubles The Catholic Church burned him at the stake for heresy in 1415. Bohemia and Moravia both had majority Hussite followers until wars resulted in the Hapsburg empire in the 1620’s forcing people to return to Catholicism. In 1727 refugees from Moravia, seeking to avoid persecution, landed up in land provided by Count Nickolaus von Zinzendorf in Saxony, Germany. They called their new base Herrhut, meaning ‘under the watch of the Lord’. This is now the main base of the Moravian Church, but there are churches in other countries, including Britain. The church is also called ‘Unitas Fratrum’ - Unity of Brethren. The church says, as one of its beliefs: “In the light of divine grace, we recognise ourselves to be a Church of sinners. We require forgiveness daily, and live only through the mercy of God in Christ Jesus our Lord.”

28. Mormons

See Church of the Latter-Day Saints no. 12 above.

29. Pentecostals

Another from the USA. This started up in the early 1900’s. It emerged from beliefs that the second coming of Christ was imminent. The word, Pentecostalism, comes from the Greek Pentecost meaning the Jewish Feast of Weeks. But it also refers to the Biblical Book of Acts, where Christians received gifts from the Holy Spirit. Pentecostals are very strong on the literal truth of the Bible and they think Baptism is an essential link to God. The early proponents were William J. Seymour and Charles Parham. There is no authoritative structure of this church branch and Pentecostals are split, some believe in the Trinity other do not. A number of slightly different sects fall, loosely, under the Pentecostal umbrella, including the Nigerian founded Redeemed Christian Church of God. There are estimated to be more than 200 million of one sort or another Pentecostalists.

p.153

30. Presbyterians

Scotland is to the fore with this Christian sect, although it has since spread to other countries. It was started up in Scotland by John Knox in the 16th century. He had an eventful life, being a galley slave for a while and engaging in several arguments with the Catholic, Mary, Queen of Scots. She asked him by what right did he rebuke her so openly. John responded: “…I am a worm of the earth, and yet a subject… But I am a watchman, both over the realm and the Kirk (church) of God.” The name Presbyterian comes from its form of church government. Here a group of elders do the general governing. “Presbyter” means elder. There are no Bishops or tiered hierarchy. The Bible, again, is the most important in their church because it was given by God, say Presbyterians. They believe God has control over all and has already picked out some people to follow Jesus Christ but not others. Who are the fortunate chosen?

31. Puritans

The name tells all. These were reformed protestants who, in the 16th and 17th centuries, considered that both the Church of England and Roman Catholic church needed purifying. The reforms that began under Elizabeth 1st were well short of the mark for Puritans. They disliked the church structure with its Bishops and wanted a more localized authority. Their followers believed in a purely Biblical led approach to their entire way of life. Puritans were closely akin to Presbyterians, Congregationalists and Calvinists. A big sideways impact was the huge colonisation movement. By 1631 21,000 had emigrated to New England, USA. Puritans were a major political party in the English Civil War (1642 to 1651). Oliver Cromwell was a Puritan and during this time became Head of State after King Charles 1st was executed.

32. Russian Orthodox

This is estimated to be more than 200 million of one sort or another Pentecostalists.

33. Salvation Army

This is an army promoting Christ and his teachings through the Bible. It is a derivative of Methodism and founded in 1865 by William Booth. They say: “At the Salvation Army, we are dedicated to doing the most good.”, and “The Salvation Army, an international movement, is an evangelical part of the universal Christian Church. Its message is based on the Bible. Its ministry is motivated by the love of God. Its mission is to preach the gospel of Jesus Christ and to meet human needs in His name without discrimination.” The Salvation Army is unique in having what it calls, officers, soldiers as well as adherents. There are 1.5 million of these in 131 countries. It is very much hands-on giving solid, practical help in areas such as running shelters for the homeless, helping the unemployed, fighting human trafficking, disaster relief and other good deeds. They are more heavily into action than words.

34. Scripture Union

p.154

This world-wide movement main aim is to get the young particularly but also adults to know more of God through the Bible. Joseph Spiers kicked off in 1867 in London. He spoke to 15 children in an Islington house. This led to the Children’s Special Service Mission (CSSM) which later on became the Scripture Union.

35. Swedenborgian

This one came about in 1787 when a Swedish Lutheran leaning theologian, Emmanuel Swedenborg, claimed a divine revelation. Emmanuel claimed that God had explained the Bible in such a way to show that the second coming of Jesus Christ was definitely on the cards. He also had seen the Last Judgement. So, Emmanuel set up this, his New Church. It’s also got other titles: Swedenborgian, Church of New Jerusalem and The Lord’s Church, among others. The various churches have between them less than 10,000 members.

36. Quakers

p.155

These are also known as the Friends, or more completely the Religious Society of Friends or even the Friends Church. “You are my friends if you do what I command you” (John 15:14). Their founder was George Fox in the middle of the 17th century. Allegedly he told a magistrate to tremble at the name of God. Hence the name Quakers. Worldwide there are over 200,000 Quakers with 17,000 in Britain. For Quakers, every human has something of God within them, despite we can all think of people where he must be hiding extremely well inside of them, and never seems to appear.

Their key beliefs are:

  • God’s light is in everyone;
  • God, this light, is pure love;
  • Someone who allows the light to guide their life will achieve complete relationship with God;
  • People do not need the intervention of a minister or priest. They can have a direct relationship with God;
  • Both redemption and the Kingdom of Heaven can be experience now, in this world.

Quakers do not celebrate Christian festivals like Christmas and Easter. Differently to other Christian sects, Quakers do not see the Bible as being the actual word of God. Other books can guide their lives. Quaker meetings are silent until someone, anyone attending, stands up and speaks. Quakers are focused on peace, human rights and living simply in the world.

37. Unitarians

p.156

Really quite unusual this one because it espouses no particular creed. Unitarianism is a kind of ‘one size fits all’ approach to religion. While its roots are Christian with a bit of Jewish it welcomes all varieties of believers. People are accepted to their get-togethers who might be Muslim, Jewish, Buddhist or even atheist. Unitarians promote inclusivity, that means supporting freedom of speech, equality of race, gender and belief. People should also hold the natural world in deep respect, for Unitarians. On top of all these basics, people then apply reason to get to wherever they are going. In the process, when pondering new information and fresh ideas, people should be prepared to change their minds and switch the direction of their personal journeys. After the first Unitarian church opened up in 1774 there are now reckoned to be 170 congregations in the UK. Quite a few famous people have been Unitarians: novelists Charles Dickens, Elizabeth Gaskell and Kurt Vonnegut; poet and actress Sarah Adams; 3rd President of the United States, Thomas Jefferson; aircraft pioneer, Frank Lloyd-Wright; film star Paul Newman and inventor of the World Wide Web, Tim Berners-Lee.

38. United Christian Church

This one cranked up, as UCC, in 1878. It is another American Protestant offshoot, from Pennsylvania. The UCC broke away from the Church of the United Brethren in Christ because members rejected the doctrine of ‘total depravity’. The latter meant humans were endowed with original sin and were unable to influence God’s decision about where they ended up, no matter how hard they might pray. Originally the flock were steered by the Rev. George W. Hoffman. For some years in the early days of UCC, they were known as Hoffmanites. Important rituals are Baptism, the Lord’s supper and feet washing. Any type of baptism is OK with UCC. They believe in the Trinity. There are reckoned to be about 770 members in Pennsylvania. There are also some in Cambridge, England.

39. United Reformed Church

This is the one that came to life after a merger of Presbyterian and 75% of Congregational churches in 1972. The formal merger took place after a historic House of Commons Bill passed in that year. Another merger was with the long titled Re-formed Association of Churches of Christ in 1981. In yet a further, bigger, attempt at integration in 1982 the URC proposed a covenant with the Church of England, Methodists and Moravian church. The Church of England turned them down. The reforming nature of this church resulted in the URC allowing the conduct of same-sex marriages in 2016. The URC is reckoned to have a membership of about 56,000 in 1,400 congregations, but it has been in serious decline, losing 21% of members between 2013 and 2018.

40. Unity Church

This is a spiritual movement, formerly the Unity Church, which began in the late 1800’s. Myrtle Fillmore and her husband claimed they cured her tuberculosis. Her husband, Charles did something similar to heal his injured leg. This happened Kansas City, USA. Their farm is now Unity Village and HQ of Unity. The original philosophy is Transcendentalism, now known as ‘New Thought’, basically saying human thoughts have creative power. Unity believes all people are inherently good. It gets its message across in a lot of spiritual literature.

41. 7th Day Adventists

What’s unusual about the 7th Dayers is the 7th day. This they count as Saturday which they treat as the Sabbath, the day of observance. Saturday is the 7th day in both Christian and Jewish calendars. Added to this is their emphasis on the second coming of Jesus Christ, hence we have their name, 7th Day Adventists. During the Reformation Martin Luther Ditched Catholicism

During the Reformation Martin Luther ditched Catholicism, because of its adulteration, as he saw it, of the Bible’s purity through addition of sacraments, traditions and Papal edicts. He Protested (hence supporters were Protestants) and identified just a small cadre of essentials for his idea of religion. Martin came up with snappy Latin slogans to emphasise his simplistic, evangelical, teaching:

  • Sola Scriptura - the sole supremacy of the Bible as God’s authoritative teaching;
  • Sola Fide - salvation is through faith;
  • Sola Gratia - God’s benevolent favor, generosity and forgiveness of sin;
  • Solus Christus - claiming that Christ is the only mediator between God and man. That shuts out those Catholic intercessors; the saints and the blessed Virgin Mary;
  • Soli Deo Gloria - simply glory is to God alone.
p.161

These five were included in Martin’s 95 Theses. He dramatically nailed these to the door of Wittenberg Cathedral in 1517, with all this he founded the Protestant variety known as Lutheranism and kicked off the Reformation.

In the 1600’s King Henry VIII saw some merit in the embryonic, European Protestantism. He decided that England should switch from rigid Catholicism and the supremacy of the Pope.

Of course, his decision was made on a very pragmatic, wholly self-interested, basis. He did not want the Pope to interfere with his marital affairs. If Henry wanted to ditch a current wife and get a new one, that should be up to Henry not the Pope, so the King thought. Henry made himself Head of the Church of England thus giving himself both the secular and spiritual power to do whatever he liked. How a New Pope is Appointed

The top man gets to be installed through a ritualised process. A fresh Pope excitement arrives in a cloud of white smoke. In contrast large numbers of its individual members have been “good”. These are mentioned under the TDF banner in Chapter 22. Harmful areas of Catholicism are too long to fully include. A 2009 major televised debate, chaired by Zeinab Badawi (television and radio presenter) did set out some of them. The debate was part of the Intelligence Squared series.

The motion debated was: “The Catholic Church, a force for good in the world?”

Archbishop of Abuja Nigeria, John Onaiyekan, and Anne Widdecombe (Conservative MP) supported the motion. Christopher Hitchens (renowned atheist, journalist and author) and Stephen Fry (actor, broadcaster and author) spoke against. The four speakers gave a good summary of the major pros and cons, but it was quite clear the ‘cons’ overwhelmed the “pros”. CH and SF were proven winners with the audience by a big margin.

At the start of the debate the large audience, more than 2,000 people, was asked to vote anonymously. Before the debated started, 678 supported the motion, 1,102 were against with 346 undecideds. After the debate this changed considerably: 268 in favour and 1,876 against (a 70% increase), with 34 undecideds. The Independent Catholic News reported: “… the debate was still a resounding failure for Archbishop Onaiyekan and Ann Widdecombe.” It’s worth seeing - https://youtu.be/JZRcYaAYWg4
